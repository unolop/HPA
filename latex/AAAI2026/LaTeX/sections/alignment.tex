\subsection{Human Priors Patterns}
\label{sec:human_patterns}

\paragraph{Signal convergence and reference reliability.}
We assess the reliability of $\sHH$ as a difficulty measure by examining its correlation with two independent behavioral signals---response time (RT) and subjective confidence---collected alongside answers.
If $\sHH$ captures genuine question difficulty, harder questions (lower agreement) should take longer and elicit lower confidence.
Figure~\ref{fig:conf_rt_sbert_triangle} confirms this hypothesis: RT correlates strongly negatively with $\sHH$ across all control questions ($r{=}{-}0.854$ to $-0.860$; all $p{<}.001$), and confidence tracks positively in two of three variants ($r{=}0.228$--$0.299$; $p{<}.016$)---converging on the same latent difficulty dimension from three independent measures.

\paragraph{Structural patterns.}
Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation} shows human blind agreement variability across question types.
Within operation types, \textit{identity} questions are the most anchor-sensitive ($\Delta\sHH{=}0.196$, $p{=}.012$, Wilcoxon), as the referent disappears under pronominalization and answers scatter. % {\equiv}\sHH(\vOrig)
For example, \textit{``What type of bear is this?''} \emph{(Identity; Animal)} elicits convergent bear-species responses under the original phrasing (\textit{brown bear}, \textit{polar bear}, \textit{asian black bear}; $\sHH{=}0.674$), disperses to broader animal categories when the anchor is weakened to \textit{``animal''} (\textit{mammal}, \textit{dog}, \textit{carnivore}; $\sHH{=}0.458$), and collapses to unrelated responses once fully ablated to \textit{``What type is this?''} (\textit{dog}, \textit{food}, \textit{electronic device}; $\sHH{=}0.341$, $\Delta{=}{-}0.333$).
\textit{Count} questions are the most anchor-robust for humans ($\Delta\sHH{=}0.008$, $p{=}.52$, Wilcoxon), as numerical priors persist even after the object is removed, which we further analyze in Section~\ref{sec:dist_alignment}. % (models, by contrast, collapse to a zero-default regardless of anchor presence (Section~\ref{sec:dist_alignment})).
However, some question groups are naturally narrow in answer space (distributional breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:yesno_distribution_all}). An extreme case is \textit{Existence} group, where they appear near-ceiling and stable across variants because they consist of only \textit{yes/no} answer expectations so $\sHH$ remains high regardless of actual agreement. % ---rather than a genuine prior,
For alignment, we separately report for categorical questions in \S\ref{sec:dist_alignment}. % yes/no and count questions are analyzed distributionally via JS divergence , while free-text questions use SBERT-based semantic alignment (\S\ref{sec:alignment_main}); the per-question Spearman $\pr$ is computed across all types jointly.

Within entity types, \textit{food} shows the steepest model degradation ($\Delta\sHM{=}{+}.095$, $p{<}.01$ for VLMs; ${+}.129$, $p{<}.01$ for SA-LLMs):
\textit{``What type of bread does this appear to be?''} \emph{(Attribute; Food;} Weaker: \textit{``food''};
Pronominalized: \textit{``it''}) sees humans converge on specific bread types
(\textit{baguette}, \textit{sandwich bread}) under the original phrasing, but shift
to less well-defined, broader categories once the anchor is removed ($\sHH{=}0.53{\to}0.32$).

\subsection{Categorical Answer Distribution Alignment}
\label{sec:dist_alignment}

Table~\ref{tab:hm_grouped_distribution_7b_compact_filtered} reports $\djs$ and modal-match rate against the human LOO baseline.
Only two models fall within the yes/no $\djs$ CI: LLaVA-Mistral~(LM) and Qwen3-VL~(VLM); no model falls within the count CI.
Notably, Qwen3-VL achieves this despite being no-biased (38.5\% yes vs.\ human 65\%; \S\ref{sec:dist_alignment}), suggesting it is the \textit{shape} of the full \{yes, no, others\} distribution---not the yes-rate alone---that determines JS alignment.
Mode complements JS by revealing per-question majority alignment: a model can match the aggregate yes-rate while scoring poorly on Mode if it answers yes on the wrong questions; conversely, a no-biased model can achieve high Mode by correctly identifying which specific questions humans predominantly answer no to.

\paragraph{Presence bias in humans and models.}
Humans show a strong positive-world prior, answering \textit{yes} 65\% of the time even though ground truth leans \textit{no} (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:yesno_distribution_all}).
Among LMs, four of five are yes-biased (54--81\%); LLaVA-Mistral~(LM) is the exception at 42.3\%.
Among full VLMs, only InternVL mirrors the human prior (69.2\%); the three LLaVA VLMs flip to a no-default (15--31\%) and Qwen3-VL is also no-biased (38.5\%)---both reversals of their paired LMs, analyzed in \S\ref{sec:ablation}.
SA-LLMs split: Vicuna-7B is yes-biased (57.7\%) while Qwen3-8B is no-biased (38.5\%); Qwen3~(Think) shifts slightly toward yes ($+7.7$\,pp to 46.2\%).

\paragraph{Zero-default on count questions.}
On count questions, all VLMs except for InternVL collapse toward ``0'' (94--99\%; Figure~\ref{fig:count_transition_all}).
InternVL instead spreads answer mass across small counts (0--3), similar to our human distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:count_transition}) and being the closest LM to the human count distribution ($\djs{=}.575$; Table~\ref{tab:hm_grouped_distribution_7b_compact_filtered}). % Qwen3~(Think) the closest SA-LLM ($\djs{=}.610$).
However, InternVL~(LM)'s human-likeness comes at the expense of an unusually high count-question abstention rate (26.9\%), which is absent in its paired VLM and other question types (Table~\ref{tab:abstention_by_qtype}).
% SA-LLMs show the same direction but at much lower rates (Qwen2.5: 10.5\%, Qwen3: 22\%, Qwen3\,(Think): 18\%, Vicuna: 5.1\%).
% Across both \textit{yes/no} and \textit{count} questions, standalone LLMs show a different failure: they often exit the answer space entirely, replacing content answers with refusals, topic-noun extractions, or uncertain non-answers (Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_calibration}).

\paragraph{Can instruction restore scene imagination?}
In most models, instruction reduces refusals but does not resolve the underlying zero-default: for 4 of 5 VLMs, count $\djs$ actually worsens under instruction as abstentions are replaced by zero-answers (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:hm_distribution_blind_vs_inst}).
The exception is the two models with high initial abstention: InternVL~(LM) and Qwen3~(Think) both reduce count abstention substantially (by 19.2 and 20.5~pp respectively) and show the largest count $\djs$ improvements ($\Delta_{\djs}{=}{-}.157$ and ${-}.146$).
This shift is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:dist_human_vs_model}, where the InternVL family's yes/no distribution under instruction moves closer to the human bar relative to the blind condition; the full blind-vs-instruction breakdown for all models is in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:hm_distribution_blind_vs_inst}. %; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:hm_distribution_blind_vs_inst}).

\subsection{Semantic Alignment}
\label{sec:alignment_main}

Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_sbert} plots mean $\sHH$ against mean $\sHM$ per operation and entity group.
Most models track the human range, with \textit{World Knowledge} anchoring the top-right ($\sHH{\approx}0.68$--$0.76$, $\sHM{\approx}0.56$--$0.60$) and low-consensus groups (\textit{Comparison}, \textit{Spatial}, \textit{Causality}, \textit{Text Reading}; $\sHM{\approx}0.31$--$0.38$) at the opposite end. % S_HH approx 0.32--0.44
Meanwhile, entity groups span a narrower consensus range ($\sHH{=}0.45$--$0.66$).%, with \textit{Place} closest to the diagonal yet showing the widest per-model spread ($\sigma{=}0.132$)---different models land very differently on place questions---followed closely by \textit{Vehicle} ($\sigma{=}0.130$).
Vicuna falls consistently below the human range across nearly every group and is excluded from the comparisons that follow.

\paragraph{Backbone decoders over-converge; standalone LLMs under-align.}
LMs sit at or near the diagonal across all operation groups (gap: $\sHM{-}\sHH$=${-.005}$ to${+.005}$), while their paired VLMs fall below (${-.018}$ to ${-.067}$) except for InternVL, whose VLM outperforms its own decoder on both SBERT and difficulty tracking ($\pr{=}0.859$ vs.\ $0.816$).
The three LLaVA VLMs cluster well below their backbone counterparts in $\pr$ ($0.450$--$0.628$ vs.\ $0.816$--$0.850$), and SA-LLMs are the weakest on SBERT alignment (${-.046}$ to ${-.089}$), though Qwen2.5 and Qwen3 reach $\pr{=}0.750$--$0.807$ on free-text questions.
% \textit{Identity} questions show the largest backbone--VLM gap ($\sHM{-}\sHH{=}{+}.083$) of any operation group. On these questions, VLMs also lose almost all $\pr$ under subject removal ($\pr{=}0.66{\to}0.08$) while backbone decoders remain stable ($\pr{=}0.85{\to}0.89$; Figure~\ref{fig:r_combined_ident}).

\paragraph{Models replicate only part of the human anchor-sensitivity pattern.}
Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation} showed that humans anchor on \textit{Identity} ($\Delta\sHH{=}+0.196$, $p{=}.012$), \textit{Food} ($p{<}.05$), and \textit{Animal} ($p{<}.05$).
We apply the same two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test (Table~\ref{tab:degradation_wilcoxon}) to each model group to identify which question types degrade significantly under pronominalization.

All three model groups replicate \textit{Identity} (VLMs $p{=}.004$, LMs $p{=}.012$, SA-LLMs $p{=}.008$): LMs match the human magnitude almost exactly ($\Delta\sHM{=}+0.197$), while VLMs and SA-LLMs over-degrade slightly ($+0.220$ and $+0.251$).
Among entity groups, \textit{Food} is replicated by VLMs ($p{=}.005$) and SA-LLMs ($p{=}.008$) but not LMs, with SA-LLMs over-degrading most sharply ($\Delta{=}+0.129$ vs.\ $\Delta_\text{HH}{=}+0.064$).
\textit{Animal} is significant for humans ($p{<}.05$) but not replicated by VLMs or LMs ($p{>}.06$); SA-LLMs show marginal significance ($p{=}.040$), with degradation occurring along different semantic axes in each group.

\textit{Existence} reveals a model-unique reversal: VLMs show a significant \emph{negative} delta ($\Delta{=}{-}0.024$, $p{=}.016$), meaning pronominalization \emph{improves} VLM alignment on these yes/no questions.
For example, \textit{``Is there a couch?''} elicits a confident \textit{yes} from VLMs (corpus-frequent object), whereas \textit{``Is there one?''} reduces this overconfidence---bringing VLMs closer to the more uncertain human prior.
LMs and SA-LLMs show no such effect ($p{>}.46$), consistent with this being encoder-driven object bias rather than a language-prior artefact.

\textit{Spatial} is the clearest model-without-human departure: all three model groups shift significantly ($p{<}.01$) while humans do not ($p{=}.30$), with per-question tracking fidelity dropping from $\pr{=}0.93{\to}0.69$ for VLMs and $0.88{\to}0.63$ for LMs (Appendix~\ref{app:op_degradation_gap}).
\textit{Attribute} shows the same pattern (VLMs and SA-LLMs $p{<}.05$; human $p{=}.10$), as does \textit{Action} for VLMs alone ($p{=}.047$; human $p{=}.69$) and \textit{Counting} for SA-LLMs alone ($p{=}.007$), suggesting text-only pretraining makes count responses more anchor-dependent on the entity noun.
