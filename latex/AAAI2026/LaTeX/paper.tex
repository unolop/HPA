\begin{abstract}
"How many people are in the photo?" is a typical visual question answering (VQA) task; but what if you had to answer this (visual) question \emph{without} an image? Humans would imagine a (typical) scene, trying to answer based on linguistic priors and world knowledge. Likewise, posing this question to Vision-Language Models (VLMs) would use statistical regularities reflecting their internal learned priors to produce answers without seeing any image. Therefore we use this implicit testing of prior knowledge task as an important lens into VLM-to-human alignment. 
%Vision-Language Models (VLMs) can exploit statistical regularities invisual question answering (VQA)---question-answer co-occurrences, lexical cues, and world-knowledge patterns---to produce answers without seeing any image. Humans, too, rely on linguistic priors when answering visual questions: question wording alone carries expectations about likely answers even before any image is consulted. Do model priors mirror these human expectations, or do they instead reflect corpus shortcuts that diverge from genuine scene imagination?
Here, we probe this alignment for the first time by presenting both models and humans with VQA questions without any image, eliciting prior-driven answers from language alone. Each question carries two linguistic dimensions: a \emph{reasoning operation} and an \emph{entity-typed content noun} serving as the primary lexical anchor. We progressively remove these anchor words from VQA questions%s while preserving grammatical structure
, creating a controlled degradation axis over 113 questions annotated across 11 operation types and 9 entity types.
Using categorical and semantic similarity metric, we analyze the answers of $N=40$ humans in this imagination scenario. We next compare 18 model checkpoints across three architectural groups---full VLMs, backbone decoders, and standalone LLMs---finding that backbone decoders align more closely with human blind priors than their paired full VLMs or standalone counterparts. 
% Overall, alignment is structured by reasoning operation: among open-ended question types, \textit{Identity} and \textit{Spatial} show the widest human--model gaps---models fall further than humans when anchors are removed. 
Importantly, \textit{count} questions reveal a qualitatively distinct pattern: models converge on a zero-default that humans do not share.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) couple a vision encoder with a large language
model (LLM) backbone, jointly trained on massive paired image--text corpora to
perform cross-modal reasoning tasks \citep{liu2023visual,zhang2023multimodal}.
Visual question answering (VQA)---answering natural-language questions about
image content---has become the canonical benchmark for measuring how well these
models integrate visual features with language understanding, and modern VLMs have achieved near-human accuracy on standard splits \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}.

A blindspot of this success is that VLMs encode strong question--answer
co-occurrence statistics during training \citep{mccoy2024embers}: because VQA
benchmarks are drawn from naturalistic images \citep{torralba2011unbiased},
text-only baselines exploiting these regularities achieve non-trivial
accuracy even without any image \citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin,agrawal2018dont},
and benchmark audits confirm that language-only signals remain exploitable
even in vision-critical evaluations: \citet{chen2024are} shows that a substantial
fraction of VQA benchmark questions can be answered correctly
without any image, motivating the MMStar benchmark filtered for visual
indispensability \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
This is analogous to hypothesis-only baselines in natural language inference
\citep{poliak2018hypothesis}, where models predict entailment from the
hypothesis alone---here, VLMs produce answers from question-side priors without grounding them in the image.

This raises a question beyond benchmark accuracy: when visual grounding is
unavailable, what priors drive model answers, and are those priors shared
with human linguistic expectations?
% Characterizing what models rely on is itself a safety and monitoring concern.
% Understanding LLM behavior across diverse settings is critical for reliable monitoring, finding that models produce characteristic default outputs shaped by training even under minimal prompting---priors that are persistent yet invisible to benchmark scores \citet{kwon2025whatllms}.
% Systematic reviews of LLM safety evaluation similarly show that identifying such persistent behavioral biases requires going beyond standard performance metrics \citep{rottger2025safetyprompts}.
% Understanding the structure of these priors matters beyond the blind setting itself.or
VLMs integrate visual evidence with learned priors, and when those priors diverge
from human expectations, they can override rather than defer to visual input---a
mechanism well-documented as a source of hallucination in grounded settings
\citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object}.
Comparing model and human blind responses therefore reveals the prior structure
that visual input will interact with in grounded settings---reinforcing alignment
where priors are shared, and contending with it where they diverge. Models whose priors match human expectations can build on a shared cognitive base when evidence arrives; misaligned priors, by contrast, compound rather than correct hallucination even under clear visual input \citep{bar2007proactive,ji2023survey}.

In this work, to study human and model priors in visual questions, we construct progressively weaker variants of VQA questions that degrade object-denoting lexical anchors. We collect blind responses from 40 participants, establishing a human reference for linguistically grounded priors and revealing which lexical elements drive model priors and where those priors begin to diverge from human intuition. 
We introduce a two-axis taxonomy---reasoning \textit{operation} and \textit{entity type}---where entity type directly links to the degradation variants, tracking which anchor categories carry prior information and how fragile they are under weakening.
We use distributional alignment ($\djs$ divergence, modal-match rate) alongside semantic agreement score (SBERT) in cosine simliarity to compare model and human answer distributions at the question-type level.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Vision-Language Models and Thinking.}
Modern VLMs combine vision encoders with large language model backbones trained
jointly on paired image--text data \citep{liu2023visual,zhang2023multimodal}, and
visual question answering (VQA) has become a standard benchmark for evaluating their
visual grounding and language understanding capabilities \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}.
Recent \emph{thinking models}---such as DeepSeek-R1 \citep{deepseekai2025deepseekr1}
and the Qwen3 series---extend this with reinforcement-learning-trained extended
reasoning traces. Such traces improve calibration in grounded settings
\citep{yoon2025reasoningconfidence}, but can also induce systematic overconfidence
under long reasoning budgets \citep{lacombe2025dontthink}.
% We probe whether deliberate reasoning helps construct more human-like scene priors in the blind setting.

\paragraph{Language Shortcuts and Debiasing in VQA.}
Early analyses of VQA benchmarks revealed that text-only models achieved
non-trivial accuracy by exploiting question--answer regularities baked into
benchmark construction \citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin,agrawal2018dont},
motivating debiasing and counterfactual intervention methods
\citep{cadene2019rubi,clark2019dont,teney2020learning,niu2021counterfactual}. As VLMs scaled, the problem re-emerged in a
different form: rather than benchmark design flaws, shortcuts became embedded
through large-scale pretraining, and recent audits confirm that language-only
signals remain exploitable in modern evaluations \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
Hallucination and shortcut reliance have been widely documented in this context
\citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object,xiao2021hallucination}, with VLMs
exploiting spurious linguistic cues \citep{han2024instinctive,ye2024mmspubench}
and failing compositional visual-linguistic bindings \citep{yuksekgonul2023when}.
Beyond language shortcuts, vision encoder architecture independently constrains VLM behavior: \citet{tong2024eyes} show that CLIP's representational blind spots propagate to downstream VLM failures even when the language backbone is held fixed, and \citet{mckinzie2024mm1} demonstrate that encoder choice and pre-training recipe are primary determinants of VLM behavior---both complementary failure modes to the linguistic priors we study.
These audits diagnose \emph{whether} models rely on language priors---\citet{chen2024are}
filter for vision-indispensable items, \citet{lee2024vlind} test with counterfactual
images. We take a complementary approach to understand the structure of those priors or how they compare to human expectations: removing the image
entirely and comparing model answer distributions against those of humans under the same condition.

\paragraph{Human Reference and Answer Distributions.}
AmbigQA \citep{min2020ambigqa} showed that many questions admit multiple plausible
answers and introduced distributional evaluation---scoring against the full set of
valid human responses rather than a single gold label---a design we adopt directly. % , comparing model answer distributions against those of 40 humans under the same blind condition.
From the human side, dual-process accounts of judgment under uncertainty
\citep{tversky1974judgment,kahneman2011thinking} and the proactive brain framework
\citep{bar2007proactive} suggest that linguistic cues activate structured scene
predictions even without full sensory input, grounding our interpretation of human
blind responses as meaningful prior-driven behavior rather than noise.
A growing body of work evaluates AI systems against this kind of human reference
directly: \citet{lan2025mind} show that human disagreement distributions over VQA answers
reveal systematic discrepancies between model predictions and human responses
that top-1 accuracy masks, \citet{lee2023visalign} study alignment between human and model visual
judgments, and \citet{bao2026imagination} show that AI outputs systematically diverge
from the divergent and negating hypotheses humans generate freely.
Behavioral auditing work further shows that standard benchmarks miss persistent
biases that only surface under diverse input conditions
\citep{rottger2025safetyprompts,kwon2025whatllms}.
Our work extends this human-reference paradigm to visual questions under controlled
referential degradation, using a taxonomy that decomposes prior structure further. %  by reasoning operation and entity type.

\paragraph{Control question variants.}
\label{sec:control}
To test how strongly blind answers depend on entity-denoting lexical anchors,
we generate three progressively abstracted variants of each question (referred to as \emph{variants}): % ,indexed by $v\!\in\!\{\vOrig,\vW,\vPron\}$  
\textbf{Original} — the unmodified question;
\textbf{Weaker} — object nouns generalized to a higher-level category (e.g., \emph{dog} $\rightarrow$ \emph{animal});
\textbf{Pronominalized} — content noun phrases replaced with minimal pronouns (e.g., \emph{dog} $\rightarrow$ \emph{it}) while retaining grammatical structure.
The full generation schema and transformation rules are in Appendix~\ref{app:ctl}.

% A drop in $\sHH$ similarity across variants therefore reflects this increased dispersion rather than a change in question difficulty per se.

% These variants define a controlled abstraction hierarchy that progressively
% reduces entity-specific information while preserving the operation required to

\label{sec:eval_protocols}

\textbf{Preprocessing.} Before scoring, all answers are normalized with the standard VQA pipeline
\citep{antol2015vqa}, and stripped of generic wrappers so human and model
responses are compared on the same cleaned answer string.
We then use regex classifier to detect abstention responses and exclude them for model-human alignment analysis. Details are in Appendix~\ref{app:preprocessing}. %  (see Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_classifier} for exclusion criteria)
%  before all distributional and semantic alignment metrics  % ; uncertain but still committed answers (e.g., \textit{``maybe''}, \textit{``perhaps''}, \textit{``unsure''}) are retained 
% We additionally report abstention ($\mathrm{Abst}(\%)$) as a human-alignment signal.
In our study, participants were instructed to provide an answer in all cases, yielding a near-zero human abstention rate ($<\!0.4\%$). % ; we assume that without such instruction, humans would naturally abstain on most questions when no image is available.
% We therefore treat blind-condition abstention (w/o) as alignment with this hypothetical uninstructed human behavior---higher is better---and instruction-condition abstention (w/) as alignment with observed human behavior---lower is better, approaching the human near-zero rate.
We report the abstention rate of models in Appendix~\ref{app:abstention}. % and instruction-effect and~\ref{app:inst}  Table~\ref{tab:alignment_ranking_7b_ticks} and 

\textbf{Distributional alignment.} For categorical (\textit{yes/no} and \textit{count}) questions that are naturally narrow in answer space, we measure alignment using Jensen--Shannon divergence ($\djs$) and a modal-match rate (\textit{Mode}).

Let $\hat{P}_{H}^{s}$ and $\hat{P}_{M}^{s}$ denote the empirical answer distributions of humans and model $M$, pooled over all questions $q\!\in\!\mathcal{Q}_{s}$ and control variants within answer-type subgroup $s\!\in\!S=\{\text{yes/no},\,\text{count}\}$; these estimate the subgroup-averaged priors $\mathbb{E}_{q}[P_{H}(a\mid q)]$ and $\mathbb{E}_{q}[P_{M}(a\mid q)]$ (or $\mathbb{E}_{q}[P_{M}(a\mid q,v_{0})]$ for VLMs).
Divergence per subgroup is
\begin{equation}
  \djs[M,s] = \djs\!\bigl(\hat{P}_{H}^{s} \,\|\, \hat{P}_{M}^{s}\bigr),
  \label{eq:js}
\end{equation}
averaged over answer-type subgroups $s$, where $\mathcal{Q}_s$ is the set of questions of type $s$.
% This captures group-level biases rather than per-question agreement, for which modal-match rate is the more direct measure.

Mode complements JS by revealing per-question majority alignment. 
For each (question, variant) pair, Mode checks whether the model's answer matches the human majority answer for that specific question, then averages over all pairs under the same sub-group.
% Unlike yes-rate---the aggregate proportion of yes answers---Mode is sensitive to \textit{which} questions the model gets right: a model with a matching aggregate yes-rate can still score low on Mode if it answers yes on the questions where humans predominantly say no, and vice versa.
% Mode thus captures per-question majority alignment rather than aggregate distributional tendency. % : a model can match the aggregate yes-rate while scoring poorly on Mode if it answers yes on the wrong questions ; conversely, a no-biased model can achieve high Mode by correctly identifying which specific questions humans predominantly answer no to. 

As a calibration reference, we report a leave-one-out (LOO) baseline: each participant's pooled distribution within $s$ is compared against the remaining participants', then averaged over participants. The resulting 95\% bootstrap CI serves as a target range---a model within this range matches human distributional behaviour.
We refer to this interval as the \textit{human range} throughout.
For $\djs$, a model below the lower bound is closer to the pooled human distribution than our human set are, indicating an majority-biased response pattern rather than stronger human-likeness.% ; above the upper bound indicates weaker alignment to the pooled human distribution.

\textbf{Semantic alignment.} For open-ended questions we compute pairwise Sentence-BERT (\citealt{reimers2019sentence}) cosine similarity using the \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} encoder \citep{song2020mpnet}. % , denoted as SBERT ($S$)

Let $\phi(\cdot)$ denote the encoder and $H$ the set of participants.
For model $m$ over question instances $Q$:
\begin{equation}
  \sHM = \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}\cos\!\bigl(\phi(\hat{a}_{m}(q)),\,\phi(a_{h}(q))\bigr),
  \label{eq:sbert}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{a}_{m}(q)$ is the model's answer and $a_{h}(q)$ is participant $h$'s answer to question $q$.
The human--human analogue $\sHH$ is computed by treating each participant as the model in turn---substituting $a_{h}(q)$ for $\hat{a}_{m}(q)$ and scoring against the remaining participants---then averaging over $h$; the 95\% CI is obtained via bootstrap over this leave-one-out set.

\textbf{Anchoring sensitivity.} We use two complementary views of anchor dependence. First, $\Delta S \equiv S_{\vOrig} - S_{\vPron}$ measures the drop induced by pronominalization per operation or entity subgroup. Larger values indicate that responses are more tightly bound to the specific referent. % The \emph{over-degradation gap} $\Delta S_m - \Delta S_{\text{human}}$ then measures how much more (or less) a model degrades relative to humans on the same subgroup. % ; positive values indicate the model loses alignment faster than human agreement drops when the visual anchor is removed. % ( \S\ref{sec:alignment_main}; Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:op_degradation_per_model}--\ref{tab:ent_degradation_per_model};

% \textbf{Difficulty tracking.}  
Secondly, for each model $m$, we also compute Spearman $\rho$ between its per-question scores $\sHM[m,q]$ and the pooled human reference $\sHH[q]$ across all question instances. %  ($N{=}183$ 
% We use Spearman $\rho$ rather than Pearson $r$ because the relationship between $\sHM$ and $\sHH$ is monotone but not necessarily linear, and $\rho$ is robust to skewed score distributions.
A strong correlation indicates that the model's difficulty profile co-moves with the human semantic scoring on the questions. % ; a low $\pr$ indicates the model fails on structurally different questions than humans do.
% The human LOO reference is the median $\rho$ obtained by correlating each participant's leave-one-out scores against the remaining participants' pooled reference.

\subsection{Human Priors Patterns}
\label{sec:human_patterns}

\paragraph{Signal convergence and reference reliability.}
We assess the reliability of $\sHH$ as a difficulty measure by examining its correlation with two independent behavioral signals---response time (RT) and subjective confidence---collected alongside answers.
If $\sHH$ captures genuine question difficulty, harder questions (lower agreement) should take longer and elicit lower confidence.
Figure~\ref{fig:conf_rt_sbert_triangle} confirms this hypothesis: RT correlates strongly negatively with $\sHH$ across all control questions ($r{=}{-}0.854$ to $-0.860$; all $p{<}.001$), and confidence tracks positively in two of three variants ($r{=}0.228$--$0.299$; $p{<}.016$)---converging on the same latent difficulty dimension from three independent measures.

\paragraph{Structural patterns.}
Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation} shows human blind agreement variability across question types.
Within operation groups, \textit{identity} questions are the most anchor-sensitive ($\Delta\sHH{=}0.196$, $p{=}.012$, Wilcoxon), as the referent disappears under pronominalization and answers scatter. % {\equiv}\sHH(\vOrig)
For example, \textit{``What type of bear is this?''} \emph{(Identity; Animal)} elicits convergent bear-species responses under the original phrasing (\textit{brown bear}, \textit{polar bear}, \textit{asian black bear}; $\sHH{=}0.674$), disperses to broader animal categories when the anchor is weakened to \textit{``animal''} (\textit{mammal}, \textit{dog}, \textit{carnivore}; $\sHH{=}0.458$), and collapses to unrelated responses once fully ablated to \textit{``What type is this?''} (\textit{dog}, \textit{food}, \textit{electronic device}; $\sHH{=}0.341$, $\Delta{=}{-}0.333$).

\textit{Count} questions appear the most anchor-robust for humans ($\Delta\sHH{=}0.008$, $p{=}.52$, Wilcoxon), as numerical priors persist even after the object is removed. % (models, by contrast, collapse to a zero-default regardless of anchor presence (Section~\ref{sec:dist_alignment})).
However, question groups such as \textit{Count} and \textit{Existence} only consist of binary or number answer expectations (answer type distribution in Table~\ref{tab:q113_sample_composition}), which are restricted narrowly in SBERT answer space. % (\textit{e.g. }yes or no)  
Therefore, we report separately in more details for such categorical questions in \S\ref{sec:dist_alignment}.

\paragraph{Visual encoding.}
The model alignment in blank prior is generally better for VLMs without the image encoder. 
Let $P(a\mid q)$ denote the model's answer distribution over answers $a$ conditioned on question $q$.
In our task, LM computes this distribution from the question alone, while its VLM computes $P(a\mid q,v_0)$, where $v_0$ is the vision encoder's output for an uninformative image; critically, $v_0\!\neq\!\mathbf{0}$, so the encoder acts as a nuisance variable that shifts the output away from $P(a\mid q)$ without adding information. 
VLMs show particularly weaker semantic alignment on \textit{Identity} questions than its LMs (Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_sbert}). Example shows the image encoder triggers the VLMs \textit{``What condiments are on it?''} \emph{(Identity; Food; Pronominalized)} elicits \textit{ketchup and mustard} from both InternVL's VLM and LM---matching the human plurality (ketchup: 9 of 40)---while all other VLMs default to \textit{0} (the encoder triggers count-zero confusion).

Therefore, processing the blank image introduces an absence signal. It flips LLaVA VLMs from their backbone's yes-bias (42--81\%) to a no-default (15--39\%). %---a shift absent in SA-LLMs without an encoder: Qwen3-8B sits at 38.5\% and Vicuna at 57.7\%, reflecting their text-prior baselines rather than any encoder-driven push

\paragraph{Instruction compliance.}
When no instruction is given, humans would abstain and double check on the task. 
With instruction to imagine the scene, human abstention rate to our questions is $<\!0.4\%$ (49 of 13,560 responses) with our classifier. We treat this unique behavior of humans of context-switching as an alignment dimension. 
Some models under the explicit instruction, also reach near-zero abstention (LLaVA-1.5 and LLaVA-Vicuna backbone decoders: ${\approx}0.6\%$), superficially approaching human compliance. However, 
As seen in Table~\ref{tab:alignment_ranking_7b_ticks}, many of our models show minimal abstention even without instruction -- some even 0\%, and those ones sometimes gain in abstention with instruction, which is a clear reversal of human behavior. 
The most aligned model in this case is InternVL~(LM), showing the highest spontaneous abstention ($17.1\%$ without instruction) and reduction in abstention with instruction ($9.47\%$). Qwen3-VL VLM and LM also show this switch with small abstention reduction under instruction (VLM: $5.3\%{\to}3.9\%$; LM: $5.3\%{\to}3.3\%$), though at much lower absolute levels.
In sum, while humans abstain freely without instruction and comply cleanly with it, no model achieves both: models that abstain do so inconsistently, and models that comply do so regardless of context.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This work studies how models respond to visual questions in the absence of any image, and how closely that behavior resembles the blind priors that humans themselves express under the same condition. We find that backbone decoders---the language components of VLMs with the vision encoder bypassed---come closest to human blind prior structures, nearly reaching the human--human agreement ceiling and consistently outperforming both full VLMs and standalone LLMs.
This confirms the intuition that VLM language decoders carry rich world knowledge from multimodal training, while also showing that the vision encoder introduces a distributional pull on uninformative images that pushes responses away from those very priors.
The exception is InternVL, whose encoder preserves rather than disrupts the language prior under uninformative input (\S\ref{sec:ablation}).
Majority of backbone decoders and several VLMs fall within the human range on semantic similarity ($\sHM$). 
On categorical questions, models show relatively different behavior, most backbone decoders are over-concentrated on presence bias and anchored on zero-default places for counting questions.

The biggest divergence observed in the humans and models were \emph{context-switching} when scnario was given or not.
Humans modulate their epistemic mode to the task: answering confidently when grounded, speculating productively when prompted to imagine, abstaining when evidence is absent.  
Current models lack this toggle. On our task, model abstention rates are universally low (0--17\%).
% The exception is InternVL backbone decoder, which drops from $17.1\%$ to $9.5\%$ under instruction---a real behavioral shift---while simultaneously ranking among the top models on distributional alignment ($\djs$), semantic similarity ($\sHM$), and difficulty tracking ($\rho$), making it the most coherently aligned model across all four criteria.
This is not a capability gap but a training one: anti-hallucination objectives penalize uncertainty expression, locking models into a single confident-generation regime even when confidence is unwarranted.
We suggest that better alignment requires not removing safety constraints but adding deliberate modes---a grounded mode that surfaces uncertainty when evidence is absent, and an explicitly licensed creative mode where confident prior-driven generation is appropriate.
Whether such a distinction can be learned, instructed, or enforced architecturally is an open question that the human--model divergence documented here makes concrete.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Participants were recruited from university communities, limiting demographic scope.
Model evaluation uses greedy decoding, which may underestimate answer diversity. 
% Human data covers only the instruction-blind condition; % corpus-frequency correlation \citep{mccoy2024embers} are similarly left to future work.
Although benchmark contamination may also amplify blind performance (see \citet{chen2024are} show that VLMs can achieve non-trivial scores without visual input partly due to training-set leakage), we stress that our pronominalization test partially controls for this, since models that rely on memorized question--answer pairs should degrade more sharply when content nouns are removed.
% Whether blind accuracy and semantic alignment dissociate systematically across architectures---and what that reveals about the relationship between benchmark performance and human-like prior structure---remains an open question for future work.

We also note that the control variants of questions are not perfect due to large variability of preciseness of subject reference in the VQA questions. For exapmle, a few questions contain no clear nominal subject, so the abstraction ladder collapses across all three variants, removing the intended difficulty gradient, for example, ``What sport is being played?'' remains identical across all three variants.
%Additionally, the Weaker and Pronominalized steps do not always follow a strict specificity order: occasionally the Pronominalized variant drops its referent entirely while the Weaker version stays grounded, producing non-monotonic agreement patterns (Weaker $>$ Pronominalized).
% For example, ``How many zebras?'' $\to$ Weaker: ``How many animals?'' $\to$ Pronominalized: ``How many are there?'' — the Weaker version remains grounded but the Pronominalized version loses its referent entirely.
% Similarly, ``What is the blue thing on the ground?'' weakens to ``What is the blue object on the ground?'', which is no more abstract.
These cases motivate more controlled abstraction methods beyond single-step LLM rewriting.
