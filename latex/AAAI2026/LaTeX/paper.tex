\begin{abstract}
"How many people are in the photo?" is a typical visual question answering (VQA) task; but what if you had to answer this question \emph{without} an image? Humans would rely on linguistic priors and world knowledge to imagine a plausible scene. VLMs can likewise answer from learned priors alone. This blind setting therefore provides a useful probe of model--human prior alignment.
%Vision-Language Models (VLMs) can exploit statistical regularities invisual question answering (VQA)---question-answer co-occurrences, lexical cues, and world-knowledge patterns---to produce answers without seeing any image. Humans, too, rely on linguistic priors when answering visual questions: question wording alone carries expectations about likely answers even before any image is consulted. Do model priors mirror these human expectations, or do they instead reflect corpus shortcuts that diverge from genuine scene imagination?
Here, we probe this alignment by presenting both models and humans with VQA questions without any image, eliciting prior-driven answers from language alone. Each question carries two linguistic dimensions: a \emph{reasoning operation} and an \emph{entity-typed content noun} serving as the primary lexical anchor. We progressively remove these anchor words from VQA questions%s while preserving grammatical structure
, creating a controlled degradation axis over 113 questions annotated across 11 operation types and 9 entity types.
We collect short free-form answers from $N=40$ humans in this imagination scenario and characterize their prior structure using cosine-based semantic similarity and distributional alignment metrics. We then compare 18 model checkpoints across three architectural groups---full VLMs, backbone decoders, and standalone LLMs---finding that backbone decoders align more closely with human blind priors than their paired full VLMs or standalone counterparts.
Overall, alignment is structured by reasoning operation: among open-ended question types, \textit{Identity} and \textit{Spatial} show the widest human--model gaps---models fall further than humans when anchors are removed. Importantly, \textit{count} questions reveal a qualitatively distinct pattern: models converge on a zero-default that humans do not share.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) couple a vision encoder with a large language
model (LLM) backbone, jointly trained on massive paired image--text corpora to
perform cross-modal reasoning tasks \citep{liu2023visual,zhang2023multimodal}.
Visual question answering (VQA)---answering natural-language questions about
image content---has become the canonical benchmark for measuring how well these
models integrate visual features with language understanding, and modern VLMs have achieved near-human accuracy on standard splits \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}.

A blindspot of this success is that VLMs encode strong question--answer
co-occurrence statistics during training \citep{mccoy2024embers}: because VQA
benchmarks are drawn from naturalistic images \citep{torralba2011unbiased},
text-only baselines exploiting these regularities achieve non-trivial
accuracy even without any image \citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin,agrawal2018dont},
and benchmark audits confirm that language-only signals remain exploitable
even in vision-critical evaluations: \citet{chen2024are} shows that a substantial
fraction of VQA benchmark questions can be answered correctly
without any image, motivating the MMStar benchmark filtered for visual
indispensability \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
This is analogous to hypothesis-only baselines in natural language inference
\citep{poliak2018hypothesis}, where models predict entailment from the
hypothesis alone---here, VLMs produce answers from question-side priors without grounding them in the image.
This raises a question beyond benchmark accuracy: when visual grounding is
unavailable, what priors drive model answers, and are those priors shared
with human linguistic expectations?

% Systematic reviews of LLM safety evaluation similarly show that identifying such persistent behavioral biases requires going beyond standard performance metrics \citep{rottger2025safetyprompts}.
% Understanding the structure of these priors matters beyond the blind setting itself.or
VLMs integrate visual evidence with learned priors, and when those priors diverge
from human expectations, they can override rather than defer to visual input---a
mechanism well-documented as a source of hallucination in grounded settings
\citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object}.
Comparing model and human blind responses therefore reveals the prior structure
that visual input will interact with in grounded settings---reinforcing alignment
where priors are shared, and contending with it where they diverge.

In this work, to study human and model priors in visual questions, we construct progressively weaker variants of VQA questions that degrade object-denoting lexical
anchors. We collect blind responses from 40 participants under the same no-image condition as the models, establishing a human reference for linguistically grounded priors and revealing which lexical elements drive model priors and where those priors begin to diverge from human intuition.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Vision-Language Models and Thinking.}
Modern VLMs combine vision encoders with large language model backbones trained
jointly on paired image--text data \citep{liu2023visual,zhang2023multimodal}, and
visual question answering (VQA) has become a standard benchmark for evaluating their
visual grounding and language understanding capabilities \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}.
Recent \emph{thinking models}---such as DeepSeek-R1 \citep{deepseekai2025deepseekr1}
and the Qwen3 series---extend this with reinforcement-learning-trained extended
reasoning traces. Such traces improve calibration in grounded settings
\citep{yoon2025reasoningconfidence}, but can also induce systematic overconfidence
under long reasoning budgets \citep{lacombe2025dontthink}.
% We probe whether deliberate reasoning helps construct more human-like scene priors in the blind setting.

\paragraph{Language Shortcuts and Debiasing in VQA.}
Early analyses of VQA benchmarks revealed that text-only models achieved
non-trivial accuracy by exploiting question--answer regularities baked into
benchmark construction \citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin,agrawal2018dont},
motivating debiasing and counterfactual intervention methods
\citep{cadene2019rubi,clark2019dont,teney2020learning,niu2021counterfactual}. As VLMs scaled, the problem re-emerged in a
different form: rather than benchmark design flaws, shortcuts became embedded
through large-scale pretraining, and recent audits confirm that language-only
signals remain exploitable in modern evaluations \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
Hallucination and shortcut reliance have been widely documented in this context
\citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object,xiao2021hallucination}, with VLMs
exploiting spurious linguistic cues \citep{han2024instinctive,ye2024mmspubench}
and failing compositional visual-linguistic bindings \citep{yuksekgonul2023when}.
Beyond language shortcuts, vision encoder architecture independently constrains VLM behavior: \citet{tong2024eyes} show that CLIP's representational blind spots propagate to downstream VLM failures even when the language backbone is held fixed, and \citet{mckinzie2024mm1} demonstrate that encoder choice and pre-training recipe are primary determinants of VLM behavior---both complementary failure modes to the linguistic priors we study.
These audits diagnose \emph{whether} models rely on language priors---\citet{chen2024are}
filter for vision-indispensable items, \citet{lee2024vlind} test with counterfactual
images. We take a complementary approach to understand the structure of those priors or how they compare to human expectations: removing the image
entirely and comparing model answer distributions against those of humans under the same condition.

\paragraph{Human Reference and Answer Distributions.}
AmbigQA \citep{min2020ambigqa} showed that many questions admit multiple plausible
answers and introduced distributional evaluation---scoring against the full set of
valid human responses rather than a single gold label---a design we adopt directly,
comparing model answer distributions against those of 40 humans under the same blind condition.
From the human side, dual-process accounts of judgment under uncertainty
\citep{tversky1974judgment,kahneman2011thinking} and the proactive brain framework
\citep{bar2007proactive} suggest that linguistic cues activate structured scene
predictions even without full sensory input, grounding our interpretation of human
blind responses as meaningful prior-driven behavior rather than noise.
A growing body of work evaluates AI systems against this kind of human reference
directly: \citet{lan2025mind} show that human disagreement distributions over VQA answers
reveal systematic discrepancies between model predictions and human responses
that top-1 accuracy masks, \citet{lee2023visalign} study alignment between human and model visual
judgments, and \citet{bao2026imagination} show that AI outputs systematically diverge
from the divergent and negating hypotheses humans generate freely.
Behavioral auditing work further shows that standard benchmarks miss persistent
biases that only surface under diverse input conditions
\citep{rottger2025safetyprompts,kwon2025whatllms}.
Our work extends this human-reference paradigm to visual questions under controlled
referential degradation, using a taxonomy that decomposes prior structure by
reasoning operation and entity type.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset and Control Variants}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Question pool, taxonomy, and selection.}
We begin with 1{,}000 questions randomly sampled from the VQA v2.0 validation split
\citep{goyal2016making}, spanning \textit{yes/no}, numerical, and open-ended types.
Because the original VQA metadata groups questions by surface question form (e.g., \textit{how many}, \textit{what color}), we re-annotate each question along two orthogonal dimensions: \emph{operation} and \emph{entity} type (full taxonomy in Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy}). 
We then diversity-select 113 questions spanning all 11 operation types and 9 entity types (full composition in Table~\ref{tab:q113_sample_composition}). % ; question space coverage in Appendix~\ref{app:tsne} Appendix~\ref{app:sample_composition},  
For group-level anchoring analyses, operation and entity types with $n \leq 5$ questions are collapsed into an \emph{Other} category (\S\ref{sec:human_patterns}). % (\textit{operations}: World Knowledge, Text Reading, Temporal, Comparison, Causality; \textit{entities}: Text, Vehicle, Place, Product); 
All groups are retained at full granularity in the semantic alignment analysis (\S\ref{sec:alignment_main}).
% Yes/no questions were intentionally included to capture presence-bias and count-default behaviors, but they are not evenly distributed across operation groups---\textit{Existence} is 100\% yes/no, \textit{Action} 72\%, and \textit{World Knowledge} 50\%---so SBERT-based anchor sensitivity is only interpretable for predominantly open-ended groups (\textit{Identity}, \textit{Spatial}, \textit{Attribute}, \textit{Causality}); yes/no groups are analyzed distributional in \S\ref{sec:dist_alignment}.

\paragraph{Control question variants.}
\label{sec:control}
To test how strongly blind answers depend on entity-denoting lexical anchors,
we generate three progressively abstracted variants of each question,
indexed by $v\!\in\!\{\vOrig,\vW,\vPron\}$ (referred to as \emph{variants} throughout):
\textbf{Original} — the unmodified question;
\textbf{Weaker} — object nouns generalized to a higher-level category (e.g., \emph{dog} $\rightarrow$ \emph{animal});
\textbf{Pronominalized} — content noun phrases replaced with minimal pronouns (e.g., \emph{dog} $\rightarrow$ \emph{it}) while retaining grammatical structure.
The full four-step generation schema and transformation rules are in Appendix~\ref{app:ctl}.
% When an anchor is present the referent is unambiguous and answers tend to converge; removing it widens the plausible referent space, causing answer dispersion.
% A drop in $\sHH$ similarity across variants therefore reflects this increased dispersion rather than a change in question difficulty per se.

% These variants define a controlled abstraction hierarchy that progressively
% reduces entity-specific information while preserving the operation required to
% answer the question. This allows us to test whether human and model responses
% depend primarily on lexical cues or on broader semantic and commonsense priors.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

\paragraph{Models and Inference.}

We evaluate 18 checkpoints spanning scales of 0.6B--13B across three groups of models: \textbf{VLMs}, \textbf{backbone decoders (LM)}, and \textbf{standalone LLMs (SA-LLM)}. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:model_inventory} lists out all models we used for the analysis.

All evaluations are zero-shot at temperature~0 with a maximum of 512 output tokens, run on NVIDIA TITAN RTX GPU. Models receive the instruction \textit{``No image provided---imagine a typical scene and answer accordingly,''} which generally reduces refusals; we denote the instruction effect $\dinst \equiv (\text{w/inst})-(\text{w/o\,inst})$ (full analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:abstention}).

For VLMs, they require inputs of images so we provide a blank 224$\times$224 image, where backbone decoders run the same weights text-only with image-encoder bypassed.  %  (Qwen3 \citep{yang2025qwen3}, Qwen2.5-Instruct, Vicuna)
SA-LLMs are text-only; several share a family with our VLMs in use. Qwen3 is evaluated both with and without Chain-of-Thought reasoning traces (\textit{Think}).
We use 7-8B scale models for the main-text alignment tests. Two SA-LLM models were run but excluded: Phi-3.5-mini (3.8B) produced verbose multi-sentence answers throughout (mean 14--16 words across variants, vs.\ 1.1 for humans), while Mistral-7B combined even longer outputs (28--30 words) with substantially higher abstention, especially on categorical questions (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:comprehensive_inst_blind}; Table~\ref{tab:abstention_by_qtype}).

\subsection{Metrics and Evaluation Protocols}
\label{sec:eval_protocols}

\textbf{Preprocessing.} Before scoring, all answers are normalized with the standard VQA pipeline
\citep{antol2015vqa}, and stripped of generic wrappers so human and model
responses are compared on the same cleaned answer string.
Abstention responses are excluded from both human and model answers before all distributional and semantic alignment metrics (see Appendix~\ref{app:abstention} for exclusion criteria); uncertain but still committed answers (e.g., \textit{``maybe''}, \textit{``perhaps''}, \textit{``unsure''}) are retained.
Details are in Appendix~\ref{app:preprocessing}. % Full preprocessing, cleaning, and abstention analysis d 

\textbf{Distributional alignment.} For categorical (\textit{yes/no} and \textit{count}) questions, we measure alignment using Jensen--Shannon divergence ($\djs$) and a modal-match rate (\textit{Mode}: whether the model's answer matches the human modal answer).
Let $\hat{P}_{H}^{s}$ and $\hat{P}_{M}^{s}$ denote the empirical answer distributions of humans and model $M$, pooled over all questions $q\!\in\!\mathcal{Q}_{s}$ and control variants within answer-type subgroup $s$; these estimate the subgroup-averaged priors $\mathbb{E}_{q}[P_{H}(a\mid q)]$ and $\mathbb{E}_{q}[P_{M}(a\mid q)]$ (or $\mathbb{E}_{q}[P_{M}(a\mid q,v_{0})]$ for VLMs).
Divergence per subgroup is
\begin{equation}
  \djs[M,s] = \djs\!\bigl(\hat{P}_{H}^{s} \,\|\, \hat{P}_{M}^{s}\bigr),
  \label{eq:js}
\end{equation}
averaged over answer-type subgroups $s\!\in\!S=\{\text{yes/no},\,\text{count}\}$, where $\mathcal{Q}_s$ is the set of questions of type $s$.
% This captures group-level biases rather than per-question agreement, for which modal-match rate is the more direct measure.
As a calibration reference, we report a leave-one-out (LOO) baseline: each participant's pooled distribution within $s$ is compared against the remaining participants', then averaged over participants. The resulting 95\% bootstrap CI serves as a target range---a model within this range matches human distributional behaviour.
We refer to this interval as the \textit{human range} throughout.
For $\djs$, a model below the lower bound is closer to the pooled human distribution than our human set are, indicating an majority-biased response pattern rather than stronger human-likeness.% ; above the upper bound indicates weaker alignment to the pooled human distribution.

\textbf{Semantic alignment.} For open-ended questions we compute pairwise Sentence-BERT (\citealt{reimers2019sentence}) cosine similarity using the \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} encoder \citep{song2020mpnet}, denoted as SBERT ($S$).

Let $\phi(\cdot)$ denote the encoder and $H$ the set of participants.
Here, $q$ indexes a single question instance, i.e., one original question under one specific control variant.
For model $m$ and question instance $q$:
\begin{equation}
  \sHM[m,q] = \frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}\cos\!\bigl(\phi(\hat{a}_{m}(q)),\,\phi(a_{h}(q))\bigr),
  \label{eq:sbert}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{a}_{m}(q)$ is the model's answer and $a_{h}(q)$ is participant $h$'s answer.
The human--human (HH) analogue $\sHH[q]$ is the mean pairwise cosine similarity across all participant pairs for the same instance, serving as a per-question \emph{difficulty} measure (low $\sHH$ = high human disagreement).
Embedding-based agreement can be sensitive to answer length and response format; we therefore report a truncated robustness analysis in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:sbert_truncation_scatter}. % the appendix (Appendix~\ref{app:model_diagnostics},  
% We therefore report lexical-overlap robustness checks (chrF, ROUGE-1) in .

\textbf{Anchoring sensitivity.} We use two complementary views of anchor dependence. First, $\Delta S \equiv S_{\vOrig} - S_{\vPron}$ measures the drop induced by pronominalization per operation or entity subgroup. Larger values indicate that responses are more tightly bound to the specific referent. % The \emph{over-degradation gap} $\Delta S_m - \Delta S_{\text{human}}$ then measures how much more (or less) a model degrades relative to humans on the same subgroup. % ; positive values indicate the model loses alignment faster than human agreement drops when the visual anchor is removed. % ( \S\ref{sec:alignment_main}; Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:op_degradation_per_model}--\ref{tab:ent_degradation_per_model};
Second, we measure how well the model tracks which question instances elicit more versus less consistent human responses via the Pearson correlation $\pr = \mathrm{Corr}\!\bigl(\mathbf{s}_{\text{HM}},\,\mathbf{s}_{\text{HH}}\bigr)$, where $\mathbf{s}_{\text{HM}}, \mathbf{s}_{\text{HH}} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Q}|}$ are SBERT score vectors for all questions $\mathcal{Q}$, where $\mathbf{s}_{\text{HH}}$ is the mean score for each question $q \in $ $\mathcal{Q}$ for 40 participants. The human reference band for $r$ is then computed separately with a leave-one-out procedure over raters. % (N=339)

\subsection{Human Priors Patterns}
\label{sec:human_patterns}

\paragraph{Signal convergence and reference reliability.}
We assess the reliability of $\sHH$ as a difficulty measure by examining its correlation with two independent behavioral signals---response time (RT) and subjective confidence---collected alongside answers.
If $\sHH$ captures genuine question difficulty, harder questions (lower agreement) should take longer and elicit lower confidence.
Figure~\ref{fig:conf_rt_sbert_triangle} confirms this hypothesis: RT correlates strongly negatively with $\sHH$ across all control questions ($r{=}{-}0.854$ to $-0.860$; all $p{<}.001$), and confidence tracks positively in two of three variants ($r{=}0.228$--$0.299$; $p{<}.016$)---converging on the same latent difficulty dimension from three independent measures.

\paragraph{Structural patterns.}
Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation} shows human blind agreement variability across question types.
Within operation types, \textit{identity} questions are the most anchor-sensitive ($\Delta\sHH{=}0.33$), as the referent disappears under pronominalization and answers scatter. % {\equiv}\sHH(\vOrig)
For example, \textit{``What type of bear is this?''} \emph{(Identity; Animal)} sees humans converge on specific
species when the anchor is present, but shift to uncoordinated responses without it.
\textit{Count} questions are the most anchor-robust for humans ($\Delta\sHH{=}0.008$, $p{=}.52$, Wilcoxon), as numerical priors persist even after the object is removed, which we further analyze in Section~\ref{sec:dist_alignment}. % (models, by contrast, collapse to a zero-default regardless of anchor presence (Section~\ref{sec:dist_alignment})).
However, some question groups are naturally narrow in answer space (distributional breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:yesno_distribution_all}). An extreme case is \textit{Existence} group, where they appear near-ceiling and stable across variants because they consist of only \textit{yes/no} answer expectations so $\sHH$ remains high regardless of actual agreement. % ---rather than a genuine prior, 
For alignment, we separately report for categorical questions in \S\ref{sec:dist_alignment} % yes/no and count questions are analyzed distributionally via JS divergence , while free-text questions use SBERT-based semantic alignment (\S\ref{sec:alignment_main}); the per-question Pearson $\pr$ is computed across all types jointly.

\textit{``What type of bread does this appear to be?''} \emph{(Attribute; Food;} Weaker: \textit{``food''};

(\textit{baguette}, \textit{sandwich bread}) under the original phrasing, but shift

\paragraph{Presence bias in humans and models.}
Humans show a strong positive-world prior, answering \textit{yes} 65\% of the time even though ground truth leans \textit{no} (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:yesno_distribution_all}).
Among backbone decoders, four of five are yes-biased (54--81\%); LLaVA-Mistral~(LM) is the exception at 42.3\%.
Among full VLMs, only InternVL mirrors the human prior (69.2\%); the three LLaVA VLMs flip to a no-default (15--31\%) and Qwen3-VL is also no-biased (38.5\%)---both reversals of their backbone decoders, analyzed in \S\ref{sec:ablation}.
SA-LLMs split: Vicuna-7B is yes-biased (57.7\%) while Qwen3-8B is no-biased (38.5\%); Qwen3~(Think) shifts slightly toward yes ($+7.7$\,pp to 46.2\%).

On count questions, all VLMs except for InternVL collapse toward ``0'' (94--99\%; Figure~\ref{fig:count_transition_all}).
InternVL instead spreads answer mass across small counts (0--3), similar to our human distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:count_transition}) and being the closest backbone decoder to the human count distribution ($\djs{=}.575$; Table~\ref{tab:hm_grouped_distribution_7b_compact_filtered}). % Qwen3~(Think) the closest SA-LLM ($\djs{=}.610$).
However, InternVL~(LM)'s human-likeness comes at the expense of an unusually high count-question abstention rate (26.9\%), which is absent in its paired VLM and other question types (Table~\ref{tab:abstention_by_qtype}).

\paragraph{Can instruction restore scene imagination?}
In most models, instruction reduces refusals but does not resolve the underlying zero-default: for 4 of 5 VLMs, count $\djs$ actually worsens under instruction as abstentions are replaced by zero-answers (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:hm_distribution_blind_vs_inst}).

\paragraph{Models replicate only part of the human anchor-sensitivity pattern.}
Models track the human difficulty ordering closely ($\pr{=}0.95$ for VLMs and backbone decoders)---but do they degrade in the \emph{same} question groups when referent nouns are removed?
Applying the same two-sided Wilcoxon test as Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation} to models (full results in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:degradation_wilcoxon}), \textit{Identity} is the only op group with significant human degradation ($\Delta\sHH{=}+0.196$, $p{=}.012$), and all three model groups replicate this ($p{\leq}.012$): backbone decoders match the human magnitude almost exactly ($\Delta\sHM{=}+0.197$), while VLMs and SA-LLMs over-degrade slightly ($+0.220$ and $+0.251$). %  $\Delta\sHM$ 

Among entity groups, humans show significant degradation in two groups -- \textit{Animal} ($p{=}.045$) and \textit{Food} ($p{=}.021$). % Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation} marks  
\textit{Food} is replicated by VLMs and SA-LLMs ($p{=}.003$ and $p{<}.001$), with SA-LLMs over-degrading most sharply ($\Delta{=}+0.129$ vs.\ human $+0.064$).
However, \textit{Animal} is not replicated by any model group ($p{>}.08$ for all three)---humans lose alignment when the species noun is removed, but models do not.
\textit{``What breed of dog is on the left?''} \emph{(Attribute; Animal)} illustrates this: on the \textit{Pron} variant (\textit{``What breed is on the left?''}), human answers scatter across species (\textit{tiger}, \textit{amphibian}), while 9 of 14 models maintain a specific breed prior (\textit{labrador}, \textit{golden retriever}, \textit{poodle})---dog identity is not encoded in the question, yet the breed prior fires regardless.

The clearest model-specific departure is \textit{Spatial}: all three model groups degrade significantly ($p{<}.01$) while humans do not ($\Delta\sHH{=}+0.040$, $p{=}.18$), with tracking fidelity dropping correspondingly ($\pr{=}0.94{\to}0.70$ for VLMs; $0.88{\to}0.66$ for LMs)---the spatial noun anchors model responses but not human ones.
This two-directional asymmetry (humans anchor on \textit{Animal}; models anchor on \textit{Spatial}) shows that the over-degradation is genuinely group-specific and not a systematic metric artifact.

\paragraph{Visual encoding.}
The backbone decoder computes $P(a\mid q)$ from the question alone---a distribution shaped by VQA co-training that closely mirrors human linguistic priors. The VLM computes $P(a\mid q,v_0)$, where $v_0$ is the vision encoder's output for an uninformative image; critically, $v_0\!\neq\!\mathbf{0}$, so the encoder acts as a nuisance variable that shifts the output away from $P(a\mid q)$ without adding information.
This is borne out empirically: in four of five matched families except for InternVL, removing the vision encoder \emph{improves} $\sHM$ (Table~\ref{tab:matched_family_alignment_tests}); difficulty concordance ($r$) follows the same pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:alignment_summary}). % by $+0.017$--$+0.069$ (LLaVA families: $+0.041$--$+0.069$; ---showing the encoder distorts both \emph{how well} a model answers and \emph{which} questions it finds easier

Processing a blank image introduces an absence signal that flips LLaVA VLMs from their backbone's yes-bias (42--81\%) to a no-default (15--39\%). %---a shift absent in SA-LLMs without an encoder: Qwen3-8B sits at 38.5\% and Vicuna at 57.7\%, reflecting their text-prior baselines rather than any encoder-driven push
InternVL's encoder preserves rather than disrupts this, maintaining the backbone's yes-bias (78.2\% vs.\ 80.8\% for its LM).
To test how much VLMs are affected by uninformative inputs, we probe four blank-image variants (black, gray, noise, white; Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}, Table~\ref{tab:image_ablation_main}): Qwen3-VL spans $\sHM{=}0.490$--$0.628$ and LLaVA-Mistral $0.590$--$0.626$, with none recovering LM-level alignment, while InternVL remains robust ($0.639$--$0.660$), sometimes exceeding its own backbone decoder.
Since InternVL and Qwen3-VL share the same Qwen3-8B backbone yet behave oppositely, the prior-disrupting vs.\ prior-preserving split traces to encoder architecture and training recipe \citep{tong2024eyes,mckinzie2024mm1}.

\textit{Identity} questions make this most concrete: \textit{``What condiments are on it?''} \emph{(Identity; Food; Pron)} elicits \textit{ketchup and mustard} from both InternVL's VLM and LM---matching the human plurality (ketchup: 9 of 40)---while all four other VLMs default to \textit{0} (the encoder triggers count-zero confusion), and backbone decoders recover to \textit{ketchup and mustard} once the encoder is removed. % N ($\sHM{=}0.621$ vs.\ $0.261$ for VLMs).

% \textit{Identity} questions reveal a complementary noun-anchoring effect: \textit{``What type of bread does this appear to be?''} \emph{(Identity; Food; Variant~C)} yields diverse but plausible answers (\textit{wheat}, \textit{white bread}, \textit{sourdough}), yet abstracting one level to \textit{``What type of food does this appear to be?''} \emph{(Variant~B)} causes every model---VLMs, backbone decoders, and SA-LLMs alike---to snap to \textit{pizza}, the dominant food category in VQA corpora, regardless of architecture.

\paragraph{Image pretraining.}
% Comparing matched backbone-decoder--SA-LLM pairs isolates the effect of image co-training in the language component itself, independent of the encoder.
We can see the effects of image co-training by comparing LM to SA-LLM. % , with encoder contribution held fixed.
Two VLMs in our set---Qwen3-VL and InternVL---share the Qwen3-8B language backbone.
Both backbone decoders outperform the SA-LLM by $+0.087$ and $+0.103$ in $\sHM$ (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:matched_family_alignment_tests}). 
\textit{Attribute} questions show the largest co-training benefit ($\sHM{=}0.61$ for both LMs vs.\ $0.49$ for Qwen3-8B). We see that SA-LLMs lack the grounded perceptual associations formed through visual co-training---defaulting to corpus-frequent object attributes (\textit{e.g. }\textit{black}) where LMs reflect visually-grounded distributions.
\textit{Identity} questions, by contrast, show near-zero co-training benefit ($\sHM{\approx}0.55$ for LMs and SA-LLM): text-only pretraining already captures the same distributional knowledge as VQA co-training for position- or type-naming questions. \textit{``What is this player's position called?''} \emph{(Identity; Sports)} draws diverse sports positions from both backbone decoders (\textit{goalkeeper}, \textit{goalie}, \textit{outfielder}) and SA-LLMs (\textit{striker}, \textit{forward})---mirroring the dispersed human distribution (striker=9, goalkeeper=6, pitcher=4 of 40).

\paragraph{Extended reasoning.} % Qwen3 vs. Qwen3 (Think) with CoT 
Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:alignment_summary} reveals a sharp class separation: VLMs and backbone decoders cluster tightly within the human concordance band ($r{=}0.88$--$0.95$), while SA-LLMs span a much wider range ($r{=}0.59$--$0.77$) and fall almost entirely below it.
Extended reasoning partially closes this gap: Qwen3-8B (think) reaches $r{=}0.827$, standing apart from all other SA-LLMs and approaching---but not entering---the human band.
Count calibration and degradation-group concordance follow the same pattern---extended reasoning closes gaps that text-only pretraining leaves open (Appendix~\ref{app:qwen3_op_breakdown}).%, \ref{app:thinking}
The benefit is selective: count and structured inference gain most ($+.125$ $\sHM$), while world-knowledge and action operations regress---reasoning traces cannot replicate the alignment gains on world-knowledge and action questions that vision co-training provides.
The two routes to human-like alignment---multimodal training and extended reasoning---are therefore complementary: each closes a different gap left by text-only pretraining.

\paragraph{Scale.}
Scaling VLMs does not reliably improve alignment: Qwen3-VL $\sHM$ peaks at 4B ($0.568$) and falls at 8B ($0.561$), with backbone decoders following the same non-monotonic pattern (Appendix~\ref{app:scale_variant}).
The most consequential scale effect is indirect: InternVL's backbone $\sHM$ decreases from 1B to 8B ($0.551{\to}0.469$ on pronominalized), driven by growing count abstention (20.5\% at 8B; Table~\ref{tab:abstention_by_qtype})---which the vision encoder suppresses, partially explaining the reversed VLM--backbone ordering for InternVL.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Participants were recruited from university communities, limiting demographic scope.
Model evaluation uses greedy decoding, which may underestimate answer diversity, which should be explored in future research. 
Human data covers only the instruction-blind condition; corpus-frequency correlation
\citep{mccoy2024embers} and broader image ablations are similarly left to future work.
Although benchmark contamination may also amplify blind performance (see \citet{chen2024are} show that VLMs can achieve non-trivial scores without visual input partly due to training-set leakage), we stress that our pronominalization test partially controls for this, since models that rely on memorized question--answer pairs should degrade more sharply when content nouns are removed.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

% Table~\ref{tab:alignment_ranking_7b} confirms that no single architecture provides human-like blind priors across all criteria.
We decompose human-like blind-prior alignment into 4 components---answer-distribution matching ($\djs$), semantic alignment ($\sHM$), per-question difficulty tracking ($r$), and appropriate uncertainty expression under instruction (Abst\%). % : VQA co-training, extended reasoning, and instruction tuning respectively.

Backbone decoders occupy the top three average-rank positions, driven by strong $\sHM$ and per-question concordance, but most over-concentrate on yes/no answers or over-align in the semantic alignment. % , reflecting an inherited VQA affirmative bias.
CoT LLM achieves the best per-question difficulty concordance ($r$) and strong count alignment, partially compensating for the absence of multimodal co-training---but offers no recovery on yes/no calibration or instruction abstention. % Extended reasoning (Qwen3~think)  (rank~3)(rank~11)(rank~5) 
SA-LLMs lead on instruction abstention: Qwen2.5-Instruct and Qwen3 reduce abstention with our instruction to imagine a scene. % (rank~1, 2 )  
InternVL stands apart: its VLM outperforms its own backbone decoder on average rank and achieves the best count alignment, consistent with its encoder preserving rather than disrupting the language prior (\S\ref{sec:ablation}).

Robustness to visual uncertainty therefore depends not only on whether a model can answer, but on which combination of priors its architecture and decoding mode activate when it cannot see.
The same encoder prior disruption that limits VLM alignment on uninformative images may also matter on genuinely difficult real images \citep{ye2024mmspubench, chen2024are}, suggesting blind-prior alignment is informative about visual-uncertainty robustness more broadly.
InternVL is the notable exception: its prior-preserving encoder confers a form of neutral-mode robustness to uninformative visual input, sustaining close alignment with humans across most metrics---while backbone decoders, freed from encoder interference entirely, remain the strongest overall alignment achievers.
