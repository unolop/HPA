\begin{abstract}
Analogous to hypothesis-only baselines that exposed annotation artifacts in
Natural Language Inference \citep{poliak2018hypothesis}, we investigate how
much of VQA behavior is explainable without any visual information.
Across multiple open-source Vision-Language Models (VLMs), blind inference
reveals characteristic answer distribution biases shaped by the training
distribution \citep{mccoy2024embers}: models collapse to ``no'' for yes/no
questions and ``0'' for count questions at rates far exceeding human baselines.
To characterize how these priors depend on lexical cues, we introduce
\emph{control question variants}: four progressively weaker reformulations
that remove entity anchors while preserving question type.
A single instruction further reveals a spectrum from \emph{instruction-gated}
prior use to unconditional hallucination. Blind outputs are also highly
overconfident: their token-confidence distributions remain high even for wrong
committed answers, a calibration failure invisible to accuracy-only evaluations.
Using a human blind-answer dataset (N=40 participants, 113 shared questions,
IRB-approved), we show that the model group closest to human blind priors is
not the full VLM but the VLM backbone decoder: removing the vision encoder
yields the highest human-model semantic agreement, while full VLMs and
standalone LLMs exhibit distinct failure regimes. Question-type analysis
reveals where this overlap comes from: yes/no and action questions show strong
human-model prior sharing, while count and grounded-reference questions are
strongly anti-aligned.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) achieve impressive performance on visual question
answering (VQA) benchmarks \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}, yet a basic
question remains: how much of this performance reflects genuine visual
grounding, and how much is explained by linguistic priors inherited from
large-scale pretraining? This question is sharpened by the benchmark's own
design history: VQA~v2.0 was explicitly constructed to reduce language bias
by pairing each question with complementary images that force different correct
answers---yet models still answer 35--49\% of questions correctly when given
no image at all, suggesting the benchmark remains meaningfully exploitable
through language alone.

This question parallels a well-known episode in Natural Language Inference
(NLI): \citet{poliak2018hypothesis} showed that a model trained on hypotheses
alone substantially exceeded the random baseline across ten NLI datasets,
revealing annotation artifacts in the benchmarks themselves
\citep{torralba2011unbiased}. The same diagnostic was applied early in VQA:
\citet{jabri2016revisiting} showed that text-only baselines on VQA~v1.0
achieved substantial accuracy without visual input, and \citet{zhang2016yin}
documented a pronounced yes-bias that motivated the construction of VQA~v2.0.
We extend this diagnostic tradition to modern VLMs: the \emph{image} plays
the role of the premise, and the question is the hypothesis. A model that
answers correctly without the image exploits a hypothesis-only shortcut.

\citet{mccoy2024embers} provide the mechanistic explanation: large language
models are shaped by next-word prediction over internet-scale text and produce
outputs proportional to the probability of sequences in the training corpus.
When a VQA question has a high-frequency answer in training data, a VLM will
produce it regardless of the image. Blind VQA accuracy thus directly measures
corpus-frequency exploitation.

What distinguishes our study from prior diagnostics is the human reference
dataset. We recruited 40 participants to answer the same VQA questions under
the same blind condition, providing a natural baseline for \emph{linguistically
plausible} uncertainty. This reveals a counterintuitive finding: the backbone
decoder---the VLM's language component with the vision encoder removed---is
the model group most closely aligned with human blind priors. Full VLMs,
despite incorporating the vision pathway during pretraining, are \emph{less}
human-like in their priors. The vision encoder appears to introduce its own
training-distribution biases that diverge from linguistically natural behavior.

We conduct a diagnostic study across multiple open-source VLMs, organized
around four questions: what do blind model priors look like compared to human
blind reasoning? How does prior-driven behavior vary with model architecture
and scale? How does prior reliance change as lexical anchors are progressively
removed? And how do instruction and token-level confidence relate to prior
expression? To answer these, we combine blind evaluation on 1,000 VQA
questions, a control-variant perturbation suite, token-confidence analysis,
and a human blind-answer dataset (N=40, 113 shared questions). A critical
failure mode we document is that blind outputs are not merely wrong---they
are \emph{confidently} wrong: token confidence distributions remain high even
for incorrect committed answers, making this failure invisible to
confidence-based filtering at inference time \citep{guo2017calibration}.

\paragraph{Main findings.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item VLMs answer 35--50\% of VQA questions correctly without any image
    input and exhibit characteristic biases (no-bias, zero-bias)
    that are structurally opposite to human blind behavior.

    \item VLM backbone decoders (same weights, vision encoder bypassed)
    are the model group closest to human blind priors across both accuracy
    and semantic agreement. Scale does not close this gap; architecture does.

    \item As entity anchors are progressively stripped (Original $\to$ Weaker
    $\to$ Pronominalized), model accuracy and human-model semantic agreement
    both drop monotonically, while the human-human ceiling barely moves,
    confirming that blind success is carried by entity-specific lexical signals.

    \item A single instruction collapses 60--70\% of model soft-abstentions
    into committed answers without improving accuracy; those answers are
    severely overconfident (ECE: VLM 0.337, standalone LLM 0.642).

    \item Human-model alignment is question-type-specific: yes/no and action
    questions show near-complete prior overlap; count and grounded-reference
    questions are anti-aligned, identifying which priors are genuinely
    human-like and which are model-specific artifacts.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Methodological contributions.}
\textbf{(i)} A semantic control-question extraction pipeline generating four
progressively weaker reformulations via GPT-4.1-mini, forming a controlled
degradation axis that isolates which lexical elements carry prior information.
\textbf{(ii)} An operation--entity taxonomy annotating each of 1,000 VQA
questions with operation type and entity type, enabling fine-grained
prior-structure diagnosis.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Hypothesis-Only and Shortcut Baselines.}
Dataset bias and hypothesis-only shortcuts have been recognized as a
fundamental challenge across vision and language benchmarks
\citep{torralba2011unbiased}. In NLI, \citet{poliak2018hypothesis} demonstrated
that classifiers achieve non-trivial accuracy using only the hypothesis,
exposing annotation artifacts; \citet{gururangan2018annotation} characterized
these through systematic analysis. In VQA, the dynamic was recognized early:
\citet{jabri2016revisiting} showed text-only baselines on VQA~v1.0 performed
surprisingly well without visual input, and \citet{zhang2016yin} identified
a pronounced yes-bias motivating the VQA~v2.0 redesign. Subsequent models remained competitive on VQA~v2.0 without genuine grounding \citep{agrawal2018dont}, and counterfactual intervention methods have attempted to remove these biases \citep{niu2021counterfactual}. Our work extends this diagnostic tradition to modern VLMs, using
a human reference dataset to calibrate which shortcuts are linguistically
natural versus model-specific artifacts.

\paragraph{LMs as Corpus Frequency Models.}
\citet{mccoy2024embers} showed that LLM failure patterns are predicted by the
probability of task, input, and output in the training corpus. \citet{kalai2025hallucinate}
provide a complementary theoretical account: hallucinations arise as statistically
natural errors under corpus-frequency pressure, and benchmarks should credit abstention
rather than penalizing uncertainty. We operationalize both perspectives: blind VQA
accuracy directly measures the probability of question-answer pairs in the pretraining
distribution, and our overconfidence finding shows that current VLMs suppress abstention
under visual deprivation.

\paragraph{VLM Hallucination and Shortcut Learning.}
Hallucination in VLMs has been widely documented
\citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object,xiao2021hallucination}.
\citet{li2023evaluating} introduced POPE, a yes/no probing benchmark showing
that VLMs systematically hallucinate objects; our finding that VLMs default to
``no'' at 64\% under blind conditions provides a mechanistic complement,
identifying the prior source of yes/no hallucination bias.
\citet{yuksekgonul2023when} showed that VLMs often behave like bags-of-words,
losing compositional binding between visual and linguistic tokens; our blind
results show this failure extends to effectively ignoring the image entirely.
\citet{chen2024are} proposed MMStar to filter questions solvable without
images; VLind-Bench \citep{lee2024vlind} measures language prior influence
across benchmarks; \citet{han2024instinctive} identified visual shortcuts
through spurious image features; \citet{ye2024mmspubench} systematically
benchmark these spurious biases across nine categories in multimodal LLMs;
and \citet{lu2025mitigating} propose zero-shot debiasing of VLM text-vision
embeddings to mitigate the resulting correlations. Our work provides the
diagnostic complement: characterizing the linguistic prior that \emph{drives}
spurious success when visual input is absent.
In parallel, \citet{kim2025subtlebench} show VLMs fail
at fine-grained visual discrimination even when images are present---bounding
the visual grounding gap from the opposite direction.

\paragraph{Human-Model Alignment in VQA.}
\citet{lan2025mind} show that VLM confidence distributions diverge from human
uncertainty distributions (measured via TVD, KL, and entropy calibration error),
and that calibrating toward accuracy can \emph{widen} this gap: the higher-accuracy
BEiT3 is less aligned with human distributions than the lower-accuracy LXMERT.
This directly parallels our SBERT--accuracy dissociation: VLMs outperform humans
by $+19$\% on blind accuracy yet are less semantically aligned with humans than
backbone decoders. Both findings challenge the assumption that accuracy improvement
implies human-alignment improvement. \citet{lee2023visalign} similarly measure
AI-human alignment in visual perception; we focus specifically on the linguistic
prior regime where visual input is absent. Our work differs from both in providing
control-variant and architectural ablations that diagnose \emph{why} divergence occurs.

\paragraph{Model Calibration and Overconfidence.}
\citet{guo2017calibration} showed that modern neural networks are
systematically overconfident---predicted probabilities do not reflect true
correctness likelihoods. We find the same pathology in the generative VLM
setting: blind outputs remain in a high token-confidence regime even for wrong
committed answers, making this failure invisible to accuracy-only evaluation.
\citet{kadavath2022language} showed LLMs can express calibrated uncertainty
when asked to predict their own correctness; under our explicit no-image
instruction, model confidence \emph{increases} instead---indicating committed
hallucination rather than acknowledged uncertainty.

\paragraph{Human Cognition Under Uncertainty.}
Dual-process theory \citep{tversky1974judgment,kahneman2011thinking} describes
how System~1 heuristics rely on representativeness and availability. Humans do
not share the corpus-frequency bias of pretrained LLMs: their yes-bias (65\%
``yes'') reflects pragmatic plausibility rather than training-distribution
statistics, making human blind behavior the natural reference for linguistically
natural uncertainty.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Models and Dataset}

We evaluate VLMs from the Qwen3-VL \citep{bai2025qwen3vl}, LLaVA
\citep{liu2023visual,liu2024llavanext}, and InternVL \citep{zhu2025internvl3}
families (InternVL results in Appendix~\ref{app:inst}), plus Qwen3
\citep{yang2025qwen3} LLMs as text-only baselines. All evaluations are
zero-shot at temperature~0. Models are organized into four groups:
\textbf{VLM} (full vision-language stack), \textbf{Backbone decoder} (same
checkpoint, vision encoder bypassed), \textbf{Standalone LLM (SA-LLM)}, and
\textbf{SA-LLM (think)} (chain-of-thought mode). A 7/8B matched subset is
used for fair scale-controlled comparisons.

Our evaluation dataset is 1,000 questions sampled from the VQA v2.0 validation
split \citep{goyal2016making}, spanning yes/no, numerical, and open-ended
types. The subset covers the full semantic range of the pool, confirmed by
t-SNE embedding visualization (Appendix~\ref{app:tsne}).

\subsection{Blind Inference Conditions}

Three conditions: (1)~\textbf{Standard}: original image with question;
(2)~\textbf{Blind}: blank 224$\times$224 black PNG replacing the image;
(3)~\textbf{Blind+Inst}: blank image plus the instruction
\textit{``Note: No images are provided. For each question, imagine an
appropriate image exists and answer based on the most common or universal
scenario.''}
Image-content ablations (gray and noise images) confirm that observed
behavior is driven by linguistic priors rather than pixel statistics
(Appendix~\ref{app:image_ablation}).

\subsection{Control Question Variants and Semantic Taxonomy}
\label{sec:control}

\textbf{Control question extraction.}
We generate four progressively weaker reformulations of each question using
GPT-4.1-mini (temperature~0, seed~42, JSON schema mode):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{deictic\_removed}: Deictic markers and spatial qualifiers removed.
    \item \textbf{object\_removed}: Object noun replaced with ``object.''
    \item \textbf{weaker\_object} (variant~B): Noun replaced by one-level-up
    hypernym (dog $\to$ animal, car $\to$ vehicle).
    \item \textbf{pronominalized} (variant~A): All content nouns replaced by
    minimal pronouns.
\end{enumerate}
Original~(C) $\to$ Weaker~(B) $\to$ Pronominalized~(A) forms a monotonically
decreasing axis of lexical prior cues. The prompt and schema are in
Appendix~\ref{app:ctl}.

\textbf{Operation--entity taxonomy.}
Each question is annotated with (1)~\emph{operation type}: yes/no-existence,
count, attribute, action, world knowledge, spatial, identity, temporal,
text/OCR, comparison, causal; and (2)~\emph{entity type}: person, animal,
object, food, vehicle, place, other.

\subsection{Human Data Collection}

N=40 participants were recruited via online university community forums under
an IRB-approved protocol (10,000~KRW/hour). Participants answered up to 134
questions in a text-only Blind+Inst setting in Korean or English; responses
were translated and normalized. We retained participants with $\geq$348
responses and restricted to the 113 question IDs answered by all qualifying
participants ($h$=40). Human--model comparisons use this 113-question subset
under the inst\_blind model condition; semantic-agreement analyses use the 88
free-text questions within it. Full protocol in Appendix~\ref{app:human}.

\subsection{Metrics}

\textbf{Task accuracy} uses the standard VQA soft evaluation
\citep{antol2015vqa}. \textbf{Semantic agreement} uses Sentence-BERT cosine
\citep{wang2020minilm} as the primary metric and SimCSE \citep{gao2021simcse}
as robustness check; BERTScore is excluded due to length bias on short answers.
\textbf{Difficulty correlation} is Pearson~$r$ between per-question human
accuracy and per-model accuracy. \textbf{Krippendorff's $\alpha$} (nominal)
measures within- and cross-group reliability. \textbf{Token confidence} is
mean token probability from output logprobs.

\section{Answer Distribution Biases}
\label{sec:prior}

We first characterize what blind model answers \emph{look like}: what
distributions models produce and how they differ from humans and ground truth.
Blind accuracy ranges from 35--50\% for VLMs and 3--6\% for standalone LLMs
(full breakdown in Appendix~\ref{app:accuracy}). The near-zero accuracy of
standalone LLMs confirms that blind performance is driven by the
vision-language co-training objective, not text-only pretraining.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{inst_blind_vC_yn_q26_h40_7b}%
\hfill%
\includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{inst_blind_vC_number_q26_h40_7b}
\caption{Answer distribution biases under Blind+Inst conditions, 7/8B model
subset ($q=26$ yes/no, $q=26$ number; $h=40$ participants).
\textbf{Left (yes/no):} VLMs default to ``no'' (64\%)---the opposite direction
from human 65\% yes-bias and ground-truth 65\% no. Backbone decoders revert
to 59\% yes, approaching the human prior. Standalone LLMs show $\sim$67\%
abstention (``others'').
\textbf{Right (number):} VLMs collapse to ``0'' (72\%), while humans
distribute across small counts (modal: 2--3). Standalone LLMs show 60\%
abstention. These are not calibration gaps in the same direction---they are
qualitatively different priors.}
\label{fig:dist}
\end{figure*}

Three systematic biases emerge from Figure~\ref{fig:dist}:

\textbf{No-bias in yes/no questions.} VLMs default to ``no'' at 64\%, opposite
to the human 65\% yes-bias, yet accidentally aligned with the ground-truth
distribution (65\% ``no'' in the VQA~v2.0 sample). This alignment is spurious:
it arises from a training-distribution prior, not visual reasoning. Backbone
decoders revert toward 59\% yes---closer to humans---suggesting that vision
co-training introduces the no-bias rather than inheriting it from the language
backbone.

\textbf{Zero-bias in count questions.} 72\% of VLM numeric answers are ``0,''
while humans distribute across small counts. This is the model's null
hypothesis under missing evidence. Backbone decoders distribute more broadly
(49\% ``0''), again closer to the human pattern.

\textbf{Abstention in standalone LLMs.} Without visual co-training, standalone
LLMs abstain on $\sim$60--67\% of both question types, confirming they lack
the VQA answer distribution that drives blind accuracy in VLMs.

\section{Human-Model Alignment at Scale}
\label{sec:alignment}

\subsection{Accuracy and Semantic Agreement Profiles}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{inst_blind_groups_vC_q113_h40_yesno}%
\hfill%
\includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{inst_blind_groups_sbert_vC_q113_h40_yesno}
\caption{Scale and human-alignment profiles across model groups ($q=113$,
$h=40$, variant~C, inst\_blind).
\textbf{Left:} Blind VQA accuracy versus parameter count. VLMs (red) and
backbone decoders (orange) consistently exceed the human baseline (dashed);
standalone LLMs fall far below. Accuracy does not improve monotonically
with scale.
\textbf{Right:} Human--model SBERT cosine versus parameter count. Backbone
decoders approach the human--human ceiling (dashed) at 7--8B and sit above
full VLMs at equivalent scale. Architecture determines alignment; scale
does not.}
\label{fig:scale}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:scale} presents the central architectural result.
On accuracy (left), VLMs and backbone decoders both exceed the human baseline
across all parameter counts, while standalone LLMs fall far below---confirming
the co-training effect from Section~\ref{sec:prior}.
On SBERT agreement (right), backbone decoders approach the human--human ceiling
at 7--8B, while full VLMs at equivalent scale consistently score lower.
This is the backbone decoder paradox: removing the vision encoder does not
hurt human alignment; it improves it. Vision co-training introduces
distributional biases (the no-bias and zero-bias) that push answers away
from the human prior, while the language backbone alone recovers closer to
human blind behavior.

\subsection{Pairwise Answer Similarity}
\label{sec:model_similarity}

We compute pairwise SBERT cosine across all answer pairs on the 88 free-text
questions. The human-human ceiling is
$\overline{\text{SBERT}}_{\text{HH}} = 0.510$.
Backbone decoders reach $0.509$---essentially at the ceiling---while full
VLMs score $0.478$, standalone LLMs $0.377$, and SA-LLM (think) $0.392$.

High SBERT proximity to humans co-occurs with a large accuracy \emph{advantage}
over humans: VLMs outperform humans by $+19$\% on average, backbone decoders
by $+24$\%. This SBERT--accuracy dissociation is not evidence of visual
grounding; it indicates that VLMs produce answers in the same short, concrete
linguistic register as humans while being better calibrated to the VQA answer
frequency distribution. Standalone LLMs show both lower SBERT (0.38) and a
\emph{negative} accuracy gap ($-9$\%), confirming they fail to exploit
the same distribution.

\subsection{Inter-Rater Structure}
\label{sec:interrater}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{vC_sbert_groups}
\caption{Group-mean SBERT cosine inter-rater agreement (variant~C,

Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} shows group-mean SBERT across all pairings.
Humans are most similar to backbone decoders (0.440), less so to full VLMs
(0.398), and least to standalone LLMs (0.366/0.312). Within-group model

Krippendorff's~$\alpha$ (from 0.554 to 0.535--0.543), with the smallest
decrease for backbone decoders, confirming structural consistency with
the human group.

\subsection{Prior Alignment by Question Type}
\label{sec:prior_types}

Operation type is the primary moderator of human-model alignment.
Yes/no and action questions show the strongest overlap: both humans and
backbone decoders converge on the same plausibility prior without visual
evidence.
Count and grounded-reference questions show the sharpest divergence:
the model zero-bias is not a corpus-frequency prior shared with humans---it
is an epistemic null that humans do not produce.
Attribute and spatial questions occupy a mixed regime where apparent
blind competence is carried by surface form rather than shared prior knowledge.

Human difficulty estimates are highly reliable: ICC$(2,40)=0.963$.
Pearson~$r$ between per-question human and model accuracy is 0.50--0.62 for
VLMs, 0.58--0.68 for backbone decoders, and 0.35--0.55 for standalone LLMs.
Entity-level structure (Appendix~\ref{app:entity}) provides a complementary
view: animal and vehicle questions show near-perfect human--model difficulty
correlation ($r=0.90$, $0.88$), while product questions are anti-aligned
($r=-0.12$).

\section{Lexical Prior Structure: Control Variants}
\label{sec:degradation}

The control variants isolate which lexical elements carry prior information.
As entity anchors are removed---from original question~(C) to
hypernym-weakened~(B) to fully pronominalized~(A)---we track how accuracy
and human-model agreement degrade.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{inst_blind_vlm_backbone_q113_h40_7b}%
\hfill%
\includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{inst_blind_sbert_q88_h40_7b}
\caption{Variant degradation under Blind+Inst conditions, 7/8B subset.
\textbf{Left:} Per-model accuracy across control variants
(Original~C $\to$ Weaker~B $\to$ Pronominalized~A; $q=113$, $h=40$).
Human accuracy (dashed) remains stable; model accuracy drops 15--20
percentage points as entity anchors are removed.
\textbf{Right:} Group-mean human--model SBERT cosine across variants
($q=88$ free-text). Backbone decoders (orange) sit closest to the
human--human ceiling (blue star, dashed) throughout. All model groups
lose alignment as anchors are stripped; the human ceiling drops only slightly,
confirming model-specific dependence on entity-specific lexical signals.}
\label{fig:variants}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:variants} confirms that prior exploitation is entity-anchor
dependent. The accuracy drop from C to A is substantial for all model groups
($\sim$15--20 pp) while human accuracy barely changes. The SBERT lineplot
shows the same degradation semantically: backbone decoders maintain the
highest alignment throughout, while standalone LLMs collapse most steeply
(0.37 $\to$ 0.29). The human--human ceiling drops only slightly (0.51
$\to$ 0.47), confirming that human blind reasoning does not depend on
entity anchors in the same way.

Within-group model-model SBERT coherence (Appendix~\ref{app:model_coherence})
follows the same pattern: models become less internally consistent as anchors
are removed, indicating that hallucinated answers diversify when entity
content is stripped from questions.

\section{Instruction Gating and Confidence Calibration}
\label{sec:gating}

\subsection{Instruction as a Permission Signal}

We classify blind outputs as \textit{hard-abstained} (explicit refusal),
\textit{soft-abstained} (implicit hedging: ``nothing,'' ``none''),
\textit{committed-correct}, or \textit{committed-wrong}.
Hard abstentions are near-zero across all models (0.0--0.3\%): instruction-
tuning suppresses explicit refusals. Soft abstention is 10--20\% for VLMs
and backbone decoders, and 30--45\% for standalone LLMs.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{merged_abstention_vC_q113}
\vspace{0.4em}
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{inst_blind_vC_group_reliability_accuracy_q113_h40}
\caption{\textbf{Top:} Soft and hard abstention rates (blind $\to$
inst\_blind) per model ($q=113$). Standalone LLMs (green) show large
shifts---soft abstentions collapse into committed answers---while VLMs and
backbone decoders (red, orange) are nearly unchanged, indicating their
priors are expressed unconditionally.
\textbf{Bottom:} Reliability diagram (mean token probability vs.\ empirical
accuracy, inst\_blind). All groups lie far below the diagonal:
ECE = 0.337 (VLM), 0.366 (backbone), 0.642 (SA-LLM), 0.641 (SA-LLM think).
VLMs and backbones are overconfident at every confidence level; standalone
LLMs are additionally erratic.}
\label{fig:gating}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:gating} (top) shows the instruction's primary effect:
collapsing soft abstentions into committed answers.
Standalone LLMs show the largest collapse (Mistral-7B shifts from $\sim$30\%
soft abstention to near zero), while VLMs and backbone decoders barely change.
This asymmetry reveals an architectural difference: VLM priors are expressed
\emph{unconditionally}---they commit regardless of instruction---while
standalone LLMs require explicit permission to activate the same priors.
In neither case does accuracy improve (Appendix~\ref{app:inst}), confirming
that instruction unlocks committed hallucination rather than grounded reasoning.

\subsection{Confidence Calibration Failure}
\label{sec:confidence}

Figure~\ref{fig:gating} (bottom) shows that all model groups are severely
overconfident. All curves lie far below the diagonal across the full
confidence range, with ECEs of 0.337--0.642. VLMs and backbone decoders
cluster at high token probabilities (0.8--0.95) regardless of whether
their committed answer is correct---high token confidence is not a reliable
signal of correct prior recall.

Standalone LLMs show higher ECE (0.642) and erratic reliability curves:
their occasional high-confidence outputs are especially poorly calibrated,
reflecting incoherent prior activation rather than coherent but wrong priors.
This calibration failure is invisible to accuracy-only evaluation, which would
rank VLMs as best-performing without flagging the overconfidence.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{The backbone decoder paradox.}
Removing the vision encoder from a VLM improves blind human-model alignment
and reverts answer distributions toward the human prior. Vision co-training
does not simply add a visual processing pathway---it also suppresses parts of
the language prior and introduces new biases tied to the image-conditioned
training objective. Blind VQA diagnosis should therefore test backbone
decoders separately.

\paragraph{Implications for benchmark design.}
Any VQA benchmark can be partially solved by linguistic priors, and the
magnitude varies predictably with question type, answer distribution, and
lexical anchoring. We propose that blind VQA evaluation become a standard
diagnostic for new benchmarks: blind accuracy above 40\% is a warning sign
of over-indexing on knowledge-accessible question types.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Human participants were recruited from university communities in Korean/English
contexts, limiting demographic generalizability. Model evaluation uses greedy
decoding (temperature~0), which may underestimate answer diversity. Control
variants use a best-effort seed (advisory in the Responses API). Human data
was collected under Blind+Inst only, so comparison with model blind (no
instruction) behavior relies on the assumption that the instruction has
comparable normalizing effects on humans and models. A direct corpus-frequency
correlation---testing whether per-question blind accuracy predicts answer
frequency in the VQA training distribution---would strengthen the
mechanistic account \citep{mccoy2024embers} and is deferred to future work.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

We present a human-calibrated diagnostic study of linguistic prior exploitation
in Vision-Language Models. Blind VQA reveals that VLMs produce characteristic
answer distribution biases---no-bias and zero-bias---that are
qualitatively opposite to human blind priors. Control question variants
show these biases are carried by entity-specific lexical anchors. The
instruction-gated spectrum reveals that VLMs express priors unconditionally
while standalone LLMs require permission, and all groups are severely
overconfident regardless.

The central alignment finding is architectural: backbone decoders achieve
near-human SBERT similarity (0.509 vs.\ ceiling 0.510) while full VLMs score
lower (0.478) and standalone LLMs diverge further. This gap is stable across
scales. Question-type decomposition shows where alignment holds (yes/no,
action) and where it breaks (count, OCR, grounded reference), providing a
principled diagnostic vocabulary for studying linguistic prior exploitation
in any multimodal benchmark.

\section{Human Study Protocol}
\label{app:human}

A total of 40 participants were included. Participants were recruited via
online student community forums at universities. All procedures were conducted
under an IRB-approved protocol adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki.
Informed consent was obtained prior to participation.

Participants answered up to 134 VQA-style questions in a text-only Blind+Inst
setting. The analysis retains participants with $\geq$348 responses and
restricts to the 113 question IDs answered by all qualifying participants.
Participants were compensated at 10,000~KRW per hour. Responses in Korean or
English were translated and processed using the aggregation strategy in
Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation}.

\section{Answer Aggregation}
\label{app:aggregation}

We cluster semantically equivalent answers (e.g., ``3'' and ``three'') using
GPT-4o mini \citep{openai2025gpt4o}. Human responses are aggregated into a
confidence-weighted distribution:
\begin{equation}
H(a_k^*) = \frac{\sum_{i:\phi(a_i)=a_k^*} c_i}{\sum_i c_i}
\end{equation}
where $c_i$ is participant $i$'s Likert confidence (normalized to $[0,1]$)
and $\phi$ maps raw answers to canonical forms.

\section{Control Question Extraction Prompt}
\label{app:ctl}

Control variants are extracted using GPT-4.1-mini-2025-04-14 (temperature~0,
seed~42, JSON schema mode) in batches of 50 questions per API call.
The output schema enforces
\texttt{\{deictic\_removed, object\_removed, weaker\_object, pronominalized\}}
as required string fields with \texttt{additionalProperties:~false}.
The system prompt is reproduced verbatim below:

\begin{quote}\small
Generate 4 control variants for each VQA question.
Return ONLY a JSON object \texttt{\{"results":[...]\}}; preserve input order.

\textbf{Rules:}

1) \textit{deictic\_removed}: Remove deictic words (this/that/these/those/here/there)
and spatial markers (left/right/top/bottom/foreground/background) while preserving
grammar. Restructure the sentence if needed to remain fluent.

2) \textit{object\_removed}: Replace the specific object noun with neutral placeholder
``object''. Do not replace pronouns or generic nouns already present.

3) \textit{weaker\_object}: Replace the specific object noun with a broader hypernym
one level up (dog$\to$animal, car$\to$vehicle, drink$\to$beverage, shirt$\to$clothing,
apple$\to$fruit, knife$\to$utensil, sofa$\to$furniture). If no clear hypernym
exists, use ``object''.

4) \textit{pronominalized}: Replace ALL content noun phrases with minimal pronouns.
Grammar-aware rules: attribute (``What color is the dog?'') $\to$ ``What color is it?'';
count (``How many cats are there?'') $\to$ ``How many are there?'';
location (``Where is the phone?'') $\to$ ``Where is it?'';
action (``What is the man doing?'') $\to$ ``What is it doing?'';
yes/no (``Is the dog affectionate?'') $\to$ ``Is it affectionate?'';
multi-NP (``between the girl and the giraffe'') $\to$ ``between them''.
Keep ONLY question words, copula, prepositions, predicate/attribute, and pronouns.
Use ``it''/``they''/``them''---never ``the object''.
\end{quote}
