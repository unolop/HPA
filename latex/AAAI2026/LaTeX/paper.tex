\begin{abstract}
"How many people are in the photo?" is a typical visual question answering (VQA) task; but what if you had to answer this (visual) question \emph{without} an image? Humans would imagine a (typical) scene, trying to answer based on linguistic priors and world knowledge. Likewise, in this situation Vision-Language Models (VLMs) would use statistical regularities reflecting their internal learned priors to produce answers without seeing any image. Therefore we use this implicit testing of prior knowledge task as an important lens into VLM-to-human alignment. 
Here, we probe this alignment for the first time by presenting both models and humans with VQA questions without any image, eliciting prior-driven answers from language alone. Each question carries two linguistic dimensions: a \emph{reasoning operation} and an \emph{entity-typed content noun} serving as the primary lexical anchor. We progressively remove these anchor words from VQA questions, creating a controlled degradation axis over 339 questions annotated across 11 operation types and 9 entity types.
We first analyze the answers of $N=40$ humans in this imagination scenario in terms of semantic similarity and answer distributions. We next compare 18 model checkpoints across three architectural groups---full VLMs, backbone decoders, and standalone LLM references. We probe alignment for different question types (yes/no, numerical, and open-ended) and in terms of instruction-following using a variety of metrics.
We find that while models (especially their backbone decoders) fall within the human similarity range for open-ended questions, there is broad misalignment across all model families and versions for both yes/no and numerical questions as well as for instruction-following.
Our results therefore point to a core difference in how priors are activated in the absence of visual information.

\section{Introduction}

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) couple a vision encoder with a large language
model (LLM) backbone, jointly trained on massive paired image--text corpora to
perform cross-modal reasoning tasks \citep{liu2023visual,zhang2023multimodal}.
Visual question answering (VQA)---answering natural-language questions about
image content---has become the canonical benchmark for measuring how well these
models integrate visual features with language understanding, and modern VLMs have achieved near-human accuracy on standard splits \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}. Newer benchmarks have expanded VQA with more diverse and challenging settings \citep{hudson2019gqa,marino2019okvqa,yue2024mmmu}.

A blindspot of this success is that the language components of VLMs encode strong question--answer co-occurrence statistics from pretraining \citep{mccoy2024embers}. Since VQA benchmarks are drawn from naturalistic images \citep{torralba2011unbiased},
text-only baselines exploiting these regularities achieve non-trivial
accuracy even without any image \citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin,agrawal2018dont},
and benchmark audits confirm that language-only signals remain exploitable
even in vision-critical evaluations. \citet{chen2024are} shows that a substantial
fraction of VQA benchmark questions can be answered correctly
without any image. \citet{lee2024vlind} substitute counterfactual images to directly measure how much models ignore visual input.
This is analogous to hypothesis-only baselines in natural language inference \citep{poliak2018hypothesis}, where models predict entailment from the
hypothesis alone---here, VLMs produce answers from question-side priors without grounding them in the image.

This raises a question beyond benchmark accuracy: when visual grounding is
unavailable, what priors drive model answers, and are those priors shared
with human linguistic expectations?

% Systematic reviews of LLM safety evaluation similarly show that identifying such persistent behavioral biases requires going beyond standard performance metrics \citep{rottger2025safetyprompts}.

VLMs integrate visual evidence with learned priors, and when those priors diverge
from human expectations, they can override rather than defer to visual input---a
mechanism well-documented as a source of hallucination in grounded settings
\citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object}.
Comparing model and human blind responses therefore reveals the prior structure
that visual input will interact with in grounded settings---reinforcing alignment
where priors are shared, and contending with it where they diverge. This is important because models whose priors are aligned with human expectations can build on a shared cognitive base when evidence arrives; misaligned priors, by contrast, compound rather than correct hallucination even under clear visual input \citep{bar2007proactive,ji2023survey}.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Vision-Language Models and Thinking.}
Modern VLMs combine vision encoders with large language model backbones trained
jointly on paired image--text data \citep{liu2023visual,zhang2023multimodal}, and
visual question answering (VQA) has become a standard benchmark for evaluating their
visual grounding and language understanding capabilities \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}.
Recent \emph{thinking models}---such as DeepSeek-R1 \citep{deepseekai2025deepseekr1}
and the Qwen3 series---extend this with reinforcement-learning-trained extended
reasoning traces. Such traces improve calibration \citep{yoon2025reasoningconfidence}, but can also induce systematic overconfidence under long reasoning budgets \citep{lacombe2025dontthink}.
% We probe whether deliberate reasoning helps construct more human-like scene priors in the blind setting.

\paragraph{Language Shortcuts and Debiasing in VQA.}
Early analyses of VQA benchmarks revealed that text-only models achieved
non-trivial accuracy by exploiting question--answer regularities baked into
benchmark construction \citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin,agrawal2018dont},
motivating debiasing and counterfactual intervention methods
\citep{cadene2019rubi,clark2019dont,teney2020learning,niu2021counterfactual}. As VLMs scaled, the problem re-emerged in a
different form: rather than benchmark design flaws, shortcuts became embedded
through large-scale pretraining, and recent audits confirm that language-only
signals remain exploitable in modern evaluations \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
Hallucination and shortcut reliance have been widely documented in this context
\citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object,xiao2021hallucination,li2023evaluating,huang2024opera}, with VLMs
exploiting spurious linguistic cues \citep{han2024instinctive,ye2024mmspubench}
and failing compositional visual-linguistic bindings \citep{yuksekgonul2023when}.
Beyond language shortcuts, vision encoder architecture independently constrains VLM behavior: \citet{tong2024eyes} show that CLIP's representational blind spots propagate to downstream VLM failures even when the language backbone is held fixed, and \citet{mckinzie2024mm1} demonstrate that encoder choice and pre-training recipe are primary determinants of VLM behavior---both complementary failure modes to the linguistic priors we study.
These audits diagnose \emph{whether} models rely on language priors---\citet{chen2024are}
filter for vision-indispensable items, \citet{lee2024vlind} test with counterfactual
images. We take a complementary approach to understand the structure of those priors or how they compare to human expectations: removing the image
entirely and comparing model answer distributions against those of humans under the same condition.

\paragraph{Human Reference and Answer Distributions.}
AmbigQA \citep{min2020ambigqa} showed that many questions admit multiple plausible
answers and introduced distributional evaluation---scoring against the full set of
valid human responses rather than a single gold label---a design we adopt directly. % , comparing model answer distributions against those of 40 humans under the same blind condition.
From the human side, dual-process accounts of judgment under uncertainty
\citep{tversky1974judgment,kahneman2011thinking} and the proactive brain framework
\citep{bar2007proactive} suggest that linguistic cues activate structured scene
predictions even without full sensory input, grounding our interpretation of human
blind responses as meaningful prior-driven behavior rather than noise.
A growing body of work evaluates AI systems against this kind of human reference
directly: \citet{lan2025mind} show that human disagreement distributions over VQA answers
reveal systematic discrepancies between model predictions and human responses that top-1 accuracy masks. \citet{lee2023visalign} study alignment between human and model visual judgments, and \citet{bao2026imagination} show that AI outputs systematically diverge from the divergent and negating hypotheses humans generate freely.
Behavioral auditing work further shows that standard benchmarks miss persistent
biases that only surface under diverse input conditions
\citep{kwon2025whatllms}.
Our work extends this human-reference paradigm to visual questions under controlled
referential degradation, using a taxonomy that decomposes prior structure further. %  by reasoning operation and entity type.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset and Control Variants}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Question pool, taxonomy, and selection.}
We begin with 1{,}000 questions randomly sampled from the VQA v2.0 validation split
\citep{goyal2016making}, spanning \textit{yes/no}, numerical, and open-ended answer types.
% The original VQA metadata question types are grouped by their surface question form (e.g., \textit{how many}, \textit{what color}), 
We annotate each question along two orthogonal dimensions: \emph{operation} type (Attribute, Count, Spatial, Identity, Action, Existence, Text Reading, World Knowledge, Temporal, Comparison, Causality) and \emph{entity} type (Object, Person, Animal, Food, Place, Product, Vehicle, Text, Other). Details are in Appendix~\ref{tab:taxonomy}. 

We then diversity-select 113 questions spanning all 11 operation types and 9 entity types (taxonomy, embedding coverage, and subset composition in Appendix~\ref{app:pool_char}).
For group-level analyses, operation and entity types with $n \leq 5$ questions are collapsed into an \emph{Other} category (\S\ref{sec:human_patterns}). % (\textit{operations}: World Knowledge, Text Reading, Temporal, Comparison, Causality; \textit{entities}: Text, Vehicle, Place, Product); 
All groups are retained in semantic alignment analysis (\S\ref{sec:alignment_main}). %  at full granularity 
% Yes/no questions were intentionally included to capture presence-bias and count-default behaviors, but they are not evenly distributed across operation groups---\textit{Existence} is 100\% yes/no, \textit{Action} 72\%, and \textit{World Knowledge} 50\%---so SBERT-based anchor sensitivity is only interpretable for predominantly open-ended groups (\textit{Identity}, \textit{Spatial}, \textit{Attribute}, \textit{Causality}); yes/no groups are analyzed distributional in \S\ref{sec:dist_alignment}.

\paragraph{Control question variants.}
\label{sec:control}
To test how strongly blind answers depend on entity-denoting lexical anchors,
we generate three progressively abstracted variants of each question (referred to as \emph{variants}): % ,indexed by $v\!\in\!\{\vOrig,\vW,\vPron\}$  
\textbf{Original} — the unmodified question;
\textbf{Weaker} — object nouns generalized to a higher-level category (e.g., \emph{dog} $\rightarrow$ \emph{animal});
\textbf{Pronominalized} — content noun phrases replaced with minimal pronouns (e.g., \emph{dog} $\rightarrow$ \emph{it}) while retaining grammatical structure.
The full generation schema and transformation rules are in Appendix~\ref{app:ctl}. 

We note that although benchmark contamination may amplify blind performance \citep{chen2024are}, the pronominalization variants partially control for this, since models cannot rely on "memorization".
% When an anchor is present the referent is unambiguous and answers tend to converge; removing it widens the plausible referent space, causing answer dispersion.
% A drop in $\sHH$ similarity across variants therefore reflects this increased dispersion rather than a change in question difficulty per se.

% These variants define a controlled abstraction hierarchy that progressively
% reduces entity-specific information while preserving the operation required to
% answer the question. This allows us to test whether human and model responses
% depend primarily on lexical cues or on broader semantic and commonsense priors.

% dependence: if blind behavior degrades sharply when content-noun anchors are

\subsection{Metrics and Evaluation Protocols}
\label{sec:eval_protocols}

\paragraph{Preprocessing and abstention.} Before scoring, all answers are normalized with the standard VQA pipeline
\citep{antol2015vqa}, and stripped of generic wrappers so human and model
responses are compared on the same cleaned answer string.
We then use a regex classifier to detect abstention responses, separating these into another alignment analysis
%  before all distributional and semantic alignment metrics  % ; uncertain but still committed answers (e.g., \textit{``maybe''}, \textit{``perhaps''}, \textit{``unsure''}) are retained 
%In our study, participants were instructed to provide an answer in all cases, yielding a near-zero human abstention rate ($<\!0.4\%$). % ; we assume that without such instruction, humans would naturally abstain on most questions when no image is available.
% We therefore treat blind-condition abstention (w/o) as alignment with this hypothetical uninstructed human behavior---higher is better---and instruction-condition abstention (w/) as alignment with observed human behavior---lower is better, approaching the human near-zero rate.
, reporting abstention rate of models ($\mathrm{Abst}(\%)$) as a human-alignment signal measuring the degree of instruction-following. %  in Appendix~\ref{app:abstention}. % and instruction-effect and~\ref{app:inst}  Table~\ref{tab:alignment_ranking_7b_ticks} and 
More preprocessing details and human statistics are in Appendix~\ref{app:preprocessing}. 

\paragraph{Distributional alignment.} For categorical questions that are naturally narrow in answer space, we measure alignment using Jensen--Shannon divergence ($\djs$) and a modal-match rate (\textit{Mode}). Let $\hat{P}_{H}^{q}$ and $\hat{P}_{M}^{q}$ denote the empirical answer distributions of humans and model $M$ for question $q$. In answer-type subgroup $s\!\in\!S=\{\text{yes/no},\,\text{numerical}\}$: % Both metrics are computed per question-variant pair and averaged over 

\begin{equation}
  \djs = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}_s|}\sum_{q\in Q_s}\djs\!\bigl(\hat{P}_{H}^{q} \,\|\, \hat{P}_{M}^{q}\bigr),
  \label{eq:js}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Q}_s$ is the set of questions of type $s$.

\textit{Mode} complements $\djs$ by using a harder majority-vote criterion: for each question--variant pair it checks whether the model's answer matches the human majority answer, then averages over pairs.
% Unlike yes-rate---the aggregate proportion of yes answers---Mode is sensitive to \textit{which} questions the model gets right: a model with a matching aggregate yes-rate can still score low on Mode if it answers yes on the questions where humans predominantly say no, and vice versa.
% Mode thus captures per-question majority alignment rather than aggregate distributional tendency. % : a model can match the aggregate yes-rate while scoring poorly on Mode if it answers yes on the wrong questions ; conversely, a no-biased model can achieve high Mode by correctly identifying which specific questions humans predominantly answer no to. 

To determine a human calibration reference, we report a leave-one-out (LOO) baseline: for each question within $s$, each participant's answer is treated as a point distribution and compared against the remaining participants' pooled distribution; JS values are averaged per participant, and the 95\% bootstrap CI of the mean serves as the target reference.
We refer to this interval as the \textit{human range} throughout.
For $\djs$, a model below the lower bound is closer to the pooled human distribution than our human set are (note that this does not make it "more" human-like, but just less variable than humans, as it concentrates on the consensus answer). % ; above the upper bound indicates weaker alignment to the pooled human distribution. 

\paragraph{Semantic alignment.} For open-ended questions we compute pairwise Sentence-BERT (\citealt{reimers2019sentence}) cosine similarity using the \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} encoder \citep{reimers2019sentence}. % , denoted as SBERT ($S$)

Let $\phi(\cdot)$ denote the encoder and $H$ the set of participants.
For model $m$ over question instances $Q$:
\begin{equation}
  \sHM = \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}\cos\!\bigl(\phi(\hat{a}_{m}(q)),\,\phi(a_{h}(q))\bigr),
  \label{eq:sbert}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{a}_{m}(q)$ is the model's answer and $a_{h}(q)$ is participant $h$'s answer to question $q$.
The human--human (HH) analogue $\sHH$ is computed by treating each participant as a "model" in turn---substituting $a_{h}(q)$ for $\hat{a}_{m}(q)$ and scoring against the remaining participants---then averaging over $h$; the 95\% CI is obtained via bootstrap over this leave-one-out set. Likewise, the models that are above this range are closer to the pooled average but not necessarily human-aligned. 

\paragraph{Anchoring sensitivity.} We use two complementary views of anchor dependence. First, $\Delta S \equiv S_{\vOrig} - S_{\vPron}$ measures the drop induced by pronominalization per operation or entity subgroup. Larger values indicate that responses are more tightly bound to the specific referent. 
% The \emph{over-degradation gap} $\Delta S_m - \Delta S_{\text{human}}$ then measures how much more (or less) a model degrades relative to humans on the same subgroup. % ; positive values indicate the model loses alignment faster than human agreement drops when the visual anchor is removed. % ( \S\ref{sec:alignment_main}; Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:op_degradation_per_model}--\ref{tab:ent_degradation_per_model};

% \textbf{Difficulty tracking.}  
Secondly, we acknowledge that $\sHM$ is partially inflated by shared question semantics---both human and model answers are conditioned on the same questions. % ---and by the diversity of human answers creating a broad target, absolute $\sHM$ differences across models are real but compressed 
We therefore additionally compute Spearman correlation ($\rho$) between per-question $\sHM$ and the pooled human reference $\sHH$ on the particular question, which is invariant to this inflation and tests whether a model tracks the same difficulty gradient as humans. We again compute bootstrap 95\% CI to obtain the target human range. 
We include categorical questions in this computation: human consensus varies substantially even on yes/no and numerical questions---some are near-unanimous, others are evenly split---so $\rho$ across all question types tests whether models recover this difficulty structure broadly. % , not only on free-text items 
% A strong $\rho$ indicates the model's per-question difficulty profile co-moves with the human reference; a weak $\rho$ indicates the model fails on structurally different questions than humans do, even if its mean $\sHM$ appears adequate.

\paragraph{Auxiliary robustness metrics.} 
Embedding-based agreement can be sensitive to answer length and response format; we therefore report a truncated robustness comparison, pairwise metric correlations, alternative embedding comparisons (SimCSE, BERTScore), and additional blind VQA accuracy and lexical-overlap metrics (chrF, ROUGE-1) as supplementary diagnostics in Appendix~\ref{app:preprocessing} (\S\ref{app:chrf_human}--\ref{app:embed_compare}). % . % the appendix (Appendix~\ref{app:model_diagnostics},   Figure~\ref{fig:sbert_truncation_scatter in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:comprehensive_inst_blind}.

\subsection{Human Priors Patterns}
\label{sec:human_patterns}

\paragraph{Signal convergence and reference reliability.}
We first assess the reliability of $\sHH$ as a difficulty measure by examining its correlation with two independent behavioral signals---response time (RT) and subjective confidence---collected alongside answers.
If $\sHH$ captures genuine question difficulty, harder questions (lower agreement) should take longer and elicit lower confidence.
Figure~\ref{fig:conf_rt_sbert_triangle} confirms this hypothesis: RT negatively correlates with $\sHH$ across all control questions ($r{=}{-}0.854$ to $-0.860$; all $p{<}.001$), and confidence tracks positively in two of three variants ($r{=}0.228$--$0.299$; $p{<}.016$)---converging on the same latent difficulty dimension from three independent measures.

\paragraph{Structural patterns.}
\label{sec:human_patterns_st}

\paragraph{Visual encoding.}

Let $P(a\mid q)$ denote the model's answer distribution over answers $a$ conditioned on question $q$.
In our task, LM computes this distribution from the question alone, while its VLM computes $P(a\mid q,v_0)$, where $v_0$ is the vision encoder's output for an uninformative image; critically, $v_0\!\neq\!\mathbf{0}$, so the encoder acts as a nuisance variable that shifts the output away from $P(a\mid q)$ without adding information.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}
This paper studies how humans and models answer visual questions when no image is available, probing blind priors triggered by questions alone. 
While semantic similarity is often within the range of humans for most models (although the detailed pattern is less well reproduced), the models often fall outside the human range in categorical questions. A summary of our overall alignment results is shown in Table~\ref{tab:alignment_ranking_7b_ticks}. % We find misalignment in humans and models biases on categorical questions ---either over-committing to \textit{yes} or flipping to \textit{no} under encoder influence.  
% we discussed that models exhibit more sensitivity to different types of question anchors. Importantly,  

Our analyses established that backbone decoders align most closely with human response patterns. On numerical questions, however, no model reproduces the human answer distribution, owing to a very different response behavior in the absence of visual information. 
% , their full VLMs are often pulled away by the active vision encoder. 
% In order to align this, one could, for example, use learned input-uncertainty gating---the model should modulate how much it defers to the visual pathway based on estimated input quality, falling back on its language prior when the visual signal is uninformative. % , and extended reasoning helps only selectively rather than uniformly. 
This counting bias, in which models do not follow the humans distribution of guessed quantities, is consistent with corpus-frequency accounts \citep{mccoy2024embers} and may connect to broader findings on numerosity priors in language models \citep{zhang2020language} and vision language models \citep{paiss2023teaching}---an interesting avenue for further studies.
%future work could probe whether this gap closes with explicit numerical grounding or chain-of-thought elicitation \citep{wei2022chain} 
More broadly, characterizing the structure of blind priors offers a principled diagnostic for visual grounding quality: models whose priors are already close to human expectations may require less corrective visual evidence, while those with strong divergent priors may exhibit systematic hallucination even under clear visual input \citep{rohrbach2018object, li2023evaluating, huang2024opera, ji2023survey}---making prior alignment a potential upstream signal for grounding reliability.

Another human--model divergence is \emph{context-switching}: humans follow the task instructions almost to 100\%, giving an answer even without visual evidence.  
We find that our models largely lack this ability -- abstention remains even when it would be appropriate and explicitly instructed (see Table~\ref{tab:alignment_ranking_7b_ticks}). 
The reason seems to be that the current objectives favor a single confident-generation regime even when uncertainty or abstention would be more appropriate. Better alignment may therefore require not just stronger priors, but explicit behavioral modes: a grounded mode that surfaces uncertainty when evidence is absent, and a licensed imaginative mode where prior-driven generation is appropriate.
Whether such a distinction can be learned, instructed, or enforced architecturally is an open question that the human--model divergence documented here makes concrete.
