\begin{abstract}
"How many humans are shown here?" is a typical visual question answering (VQA) task; but what if you had to answer this (visual) question \emph{without} an image? Humans would imagine a (typical) scene, trying to answer based on linguistic priors and world knowledge. Likewise, when posing this question to Vision-Language Models (VLMs), these would use statistical regularities reflecting their internal learned priors to produce answers without seeing any image. This task is therefore an implicit test of prior knowledge and thus can be used as an important lens into VLM-to-human alignment.   
%Vision-Language Models (VLMs) can exploit statistical regularities invisual question answering (VQA)---question-answer co-occurrences, lexical cues, and world-knowledge patterns---to produce answers without seeing any image. Humans, too, rely on linguistic priors when answering visual questions: question wording alone carries expectations about likely answers even before any image is consulted. Do model priors mirror these human expectations, or do they instead reflect corpus shortcuts that diverge from genuine scene imagination?
Here, we probe this alignment for the first time by presenting both models and humans with VQA questions without any image, eliciting prior-driven answers from language alone. Each question carries two linguistic dimensions: a \emph{reasoning operation} and an \emph{entity-typed content noun} serving as the primary lexical anchor. We progressively remove these anchor words from VQA questions%s while preserving grammatical structure
, creating a controlled degradation axis over 113 questions annotated across 11 operation types and 9 entity types.
Using a semantic similarity metric, we first analyze the short free-form answers of $N=40$ humans in this imagination scenario. We next compare 18 model checkpoints across three architectural groups---full VLMs, backbone decoders, and standalone LLMs---finding that backbone decoders align more closely with human blind priors than their paired full VLMs or standalone counterparts.
Overall, alignment is structured by reasoning operation: \textit{Existence} and \textit{Action} questions carry stable priors for both humans and models, while \textit{Identity} and \textit{Spatial} questions show the widest human--model gaps. Importantly, models diverge most sharply on \textit{count} questions, converging on a zero-default that humans do not share.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) couple a vision encoder with a large language
model (LLM) backbone, jointly trained on massive paired image--text corpora to
perform cross-modal reasoning tasks \citep{liu2023visual,zhang2023multimodal}.
Visual question answering (VQA)---answering natural-language questions about
image content---has become the canonical benchmark for measuring how well these
models integrate visual features with language understanding, and modern VLMs have achieved near-human accuracy on standard splits \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}.

A blindspot of this success is that VLMs encode strong question--answer
co-occurrence statistics during training \citep{mccoy2024embers}: because VQA
benchmarks are drawn from naturalistic images \citep{torralba2011unbiased},
text-only baselines exploiting these regularities achieve non-trivial
accuracy even without any image \citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin,agrawal2018dont},
and benchmark audits confirm that language-only signals remain exploitable
even in vision-critical evaluations: \citet{chen2024are} shows that a substantial
fraction of VQA benchmark questions can be answered correctly
without any image, motivating the MMStar benchmark filtered for visual
indispensability \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
Analogous to hypothesis-only baselines in natural language inference
\citep{poliak2018hypothesis}, where models predict entailment from the
hypothesis alone, showing VLMs can exploit shortcut priors in the question text without grounding them in the image.
This raises a question beyond benchmark accuracy: when visual grounding is
unavailable, what priors drive model answers, and are those priors shared
with human linguistic expectations?

% Systematic reviews of LLM safety evaluation similarly show that identifying such persistent behavioral biases requires going beyond standard performance metrics \citep{rottger2025safetyprompts}.
% Understanding the structure of these priors matters beyond the blind setting itself.or
VLMs integrate visual evidence with learned priors, and when those priors diverge
from human expectations, they can override rather than defer to visual input---a
mechanism well-documented as a source of hallucination in grounded settings
\citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object}.
Comparing model and human blind responses therefore reveals the prior structure
that visual input will interact with in grounded settings---reinforcing alignment
where priors are shared, and contending with it where they diverge.

In this work, to study human and model priors in visual questions, we construct progressively weaker variants of VQA questions that degrade object-denoting lexical
anchors. We collect blind responses from 40 participants under the same no-image condition as the models, establishing a human reference for linguistically grounded priors and reveal which lexical elements drive model priors and where those priors begin to diverge from human intuition.

We find that human--model misalignment is structured by reasoning operation:
where questions draw on stable commonsense priors (\textit{Existence}, \textit{Action}), humans
and models remain in step even as lexical anchors are removed; but \textit{Identity}
and \textit{Spatial} questions show the widest gaps---both humans and models
become less consistent without anchors, but models fall further.
\textit{Count} questions expose a qualitatively different failure: models converge on a
zero-default that humans do not share, regardless of anchor presence.
Importantly, backbone decoders---the language component of a VLM with its vision
encoder bypassed---align more closely with human priors than either full VLMs or
standalone LLMs, suggesting that vision encoder processing actively suppresses rather than
complement the language prior.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Vision-Language Models and Thinking.}
Modern VLMs combine vision encoders with large language model backbones trained
jointly on paired image--text data \citep{liu2023visual,zhang2023multimodal}, and
visual question answering (VQA) has become a standard benchmark for evaluating their
visual grounding and language understanding capabilities \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}.
Recent \emph{thinking models}---such as DeepSeek-R1 \citep{deepseekai2025deepseekr1}
and the Qwen3 series---extend this with reinforcement-learning-trained extended
reasoning traces. Such traces improve calibration in grounded settings
\citep{yoon2025reasoningconfidence}, but can also induce systematic overconfidence
under long reasoning budgets \citep{lacombe2025dontthink}.
% We probe whether deliberate reasoning helps construct more human-like scene priors in the blind setting.

\paragraph{Language Shortcuts and Debiasing in VQA.}
Early analyses of VQA benchmarks revealed that text-only models achieved
non-trivial accuracy by exploiting question--answer regularities baked into
benchmark construction \citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin,agrawal2018dont},
motivating debiasing and counterfactual intervention methods
\citep{cadene2019rubi,clark2019dont,teney2020learning,niu2021counterfactual}. As VLMs scaled, the problem re-emerged in a
different form: rather than benchmark design flaws, shortcuts became embedded
through large-scale pretraining, and recent audits confirm that language-only
signals remain exploitable in modern evaluations \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
Hallucination and shortcut reliance have been widely documented in this context
\citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object,xiao2021hallucination}, with VLMs
exploiting spurious linguistic cues \citep{han2024instinctive,ye2024mmspubench}
and failing compositional visual-linguistic bindings \citep{yuksekgonul2023when}.
Beyond language shortcuts, vision encoder architecture independently constrains VLM behavior: \citet{tong2024eyes} show that CLIP's representational blind spots propagate to downstream VLM failures even when the language backbone is held fixed---a complementary failure mode to the linguistic priors we study.
These audits diagnose \emph{whether} models rely on language priors---\citet{chen2024are}
filter for vision-indispensable items, \citet{lee2024vlind} test with counterfactual
images. We take a complementary approach to understand the structure of those priors or how they compare to human expectations: removing the image
entirely and comparing model answer distributions against those of humans under the same condition.

\paragraph{Human Reference and Answer Distributions.}
AmbigQA \citep{min2020ambigqa} showed that many questions admit multiple plausible
answers and introduced distributional evaluation---scoring against the full set of
valid human responses rather than a single gold label---a design we adopt directly,
comparing model answer distributions against those of 40 humans under the same blind condition.
From the human side, dual-process accounts of judgment under uncertainty
\citep{tversky1974judgment,kahneman2011thinking} and the proactive brain framework
\citep{bar2007proactive} suggest that linguistic cues activate structured scene
predictions even without full sensory input, grounding our interpretation of human
blind responses as meaningful prior-driven behavior rather than noise.
A growing body of work evaluates AI systems against this kind of human reference
directly: \citet{lan2025mind} show that human disagreement distributions over VQA answers
reveal systematic discrepancies between model predictions and human responses
that top-1 accuracy masks, \citet{lee2023visalign} study alignment between human and model visual
judgments, and \citet{bao2026imagination} show that AI outputs systematically diverge
from the divergent and negating hypotheses humans generate freely.
Behavioral auditing work further shows that standard benchmarks miss persistent
biases that only surface under diverse input conditions
\citep{rottger2025safetyprompts,kwon2025whatllms}.
Our work extends this human-reference paradigm to visual questions under controlled
referential degradation, using a taxonomy that decomposes prior structure by
reasoning operation and entity type.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset and Control Variants}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Question pool, taxonomy, and selection.}
We begin with 1{,}000 questions randomly sampled from the VQA v2.0 validation split
\citep{goyal2016making}, spanning \textit{yes/no}, numerical, and open-ended types.
Because the original VQA metadata uses surface question forms that do not cleanly separate reasoning demand from semantic content, we annotate each question along two orthogonal dimensions: \emph{operation} (e.g., \textit{Count}, \textit{Attribute}, \textit{Action}, \textit{Spatial}, \textit{Identity}) and \emph{entity} (e.g., \textit{Person}, \textit{Animal}, \textit{Object}, \textit{Vehicle}, \textit{Food}, \textit{Place}; full taxonomy in Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy}).
We then diversity-select 113 questions, targeting at least 7 questions per entity type across all 11 operation types (full composition in Appendix~\ref{app:sample_composition}, Table~\ref{tab:q113_sample_composition}; question space coverage in Appendix~\ref{app:tsne}).

\paragraph{Control question variants.}
\label{sec:control}
To test how strongly blind answers depend on entity-denoting lexical anchors,
we generate three progressively abstracted variants of each question
(referred to as \emph{variants} throughout):
\textbf{Original} — the unmodified question;
\textbf{Weaker} — object nouns generalized to a higher-level category (e.g., \emph{dog} $\rightarrow$ \emph{animal});
\textbf{Pronominalized} — content noun phrases replaced with minimal pronouns (e.g., \emph{dog} $\rightarrow$ \emph{it}) while retaining grammatical structure.
The full four-step generation schema and transformation rules are in Appendix~\ref{app:ctl}.
For a small number of questions with no clear nominal subject (e.g., existential or
weather questions), the abstraction hierarchy partially collapses; these are retained
as they do not introduce systematic bias.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

\paragraph{Models and Inference.}

We evaluate 18 checkpoints (0.6B--13B) across three groups---full VLMs, backbone decoders, and standalone LLMs (Table~\ref{tab:model_inventory}). We mainly use the matched 7--8B scale for main alignment tests (full scale in Appendix~\ref{app:scale_variant}).
\textbf{VLMs} (Qwen3-VL \citep{bai2025qwen3vl}, LLaVA \citep{liu2023visual,liu2024llavanext}, InternVL \citep{zhu2025internvl3}) receive a blank 224$\times$224 image with the instruction \textit{``No image provided---imagine a typical scene and answer accordingly,''} which reduces refusals and shifts answers toward the human prior (See instruction effect in Appendix~\ref{app:abstention}. 
\textbf{Backbone decoders} run the same weights text-only with image-encoder bypassed. % , isolating the language prior.
\textbf{Standalone LLMs} (Qwen3 \citep{yang2025qwen3}, Qwen2.5-Instruct, Mistral, Vicuna) are text-only; several share a family with a paired VLM backbone, enabling direct comparisons.
\textbf{Thinking models} refer to Qwen3 checkpoints decoded with reasoning traces enabled (labeled \textit{think}); the underlying weights are unchanged, and only the decoding mode differs from the paired non-thinking model.
All evaluations are zero-shot at temperature~0.
Phi-3.5-mini (3.8B) was run but excluded as it produced exclusively verbose multi-sentence outputs incompatible with short-answer alignment scoring.

\paragraph{Human Data Collection.}\label{sec:human_data}

40 participants (online university forums, IRB-approved) answered the 113 questions across all three control variants under the same blind-awareness instruction as the models---\textit{``No image provided---imagine a typical scene and answer accordingly''}---yielding 13,560 total responses ($N{=}339$ question--variant pairs $\times$ 40 participants).
Instruction effects on abstention and alignment are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:abstention}.
Full protocol details and statistics are in Appendix~\ref{app:human}.

\subsection{Metrics and Evaluation Protocols}
\label{sec:eval_protocols}

\textbf{Preprocessing.} Before scoring, all answers are normalized with the standard VQA pipeline
\citep{antol2015vqa}, and stripped of generic wrappers so human and model
responses are compared on the same cleaned answer string.
Abstentions are then excluded and non-standard committed answers (e.g., \textit{``maybe''}) are mapped to the \textit{other} bin; this filtering is applied uniformly to both human and model responses before all distributional and semantic alignment metrics, so comparisons are always over the same committed-answer subset.
Full preprocessing, cleaning, and abstention analysis details are in Appendix~\ref{app:preprocessing} and~\ref{app:abstention}.

\textbf{Distributional alignment.} For categorical questions such as \textit{yes/no}
and \textit{count}, we compare each model response (a single answer per question--variant
at temperature~0) against the per-question human answer distribution using
Jensen--Shannon (JS) divergence and a modal-match rate
(\textit{Mode}: whether the model's answer matches the human modal answer for that question).
All comparisons are per-question: the model's answer is treated as a point mass and
evaluated against the empirical distribution of 40 human responses for the \emph{same} question--variant; scores are then averaged across questions.
% As a human ceiling, we report a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) baseline in which each human answer is evaluated against the remaining 39 human responses for the same question.
% Answer bins for yes/no are \{yes, no, other\}; for count, they are the observed numeric values.

\textbf{Semantic alignment.} For answer-level semantic agreement, we compute pairwise
Sentence-BERT \citep{reimers2019sentence} cosine similarity between responses.
Specifically, we use the \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} encoder
\citep{song2020mpnet}. For each question--variant pair, we compare each model answer against
the set of human answers and average over humans to obtain a per-question
human--model score.
% Human--human (HH) agreement is computed analogously from distinct human pairs.
% For model $m$, question $q$, and variant $v$,
% $\mathrm{HM}_{m,q,v}$ is the mean SBERT cosine between the model answer and
% all individual human answers, and $\mathrm{HH}_{q,v}$ is the mean SBERT cosine
% across distinct human--human pairs for the same question and variant. For
% grouped views such as the HH--HM scatterplots, we average these per-question
% scores over the questions and variants in slice $g$ to obtain
% $\overline{\mathrm{HM}}_{m,g}$ and $\overline{\mathrm{HH}}_g$.
Embedding-based agreement can be sensitive to answer length; some standalone LLMs produce longer explanatory responses even after preamble stripping.
We therefore report lexical-overlap robustness checks (chrF, ROUGE-1) in Appendix~\ref{app:chrf_human} and a length-normalized evaluation truncating answers to $\leq$5 words (Figure~\ref{fig:sbert_truncation_scatter}).
Group-level concordance between HH and HM SBERT is quantified via Spearman $\rho$ over group$\times$question variant means (Appendix~\ref{tab:per_model_r}). % ; a high $\rho$ means the model ranks question groups by alignment quality in the same order as humans.

\textbf{Task Performance.} We report blind VQA accuracy as a secondary behavioral
signal indicating how strongly models leverage question-side priors and shortcuts
\citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}; degradation profiles and human--model accuracy
correlations are in Appendix~\ref{app:model_diagnostics}
(Figures~\ref{fig:combined_accuracy},~\ref{fig:scatter_accuracy}).

\subsection{Human Priors Patterns}
\label{sec:human_patterns}

\paragraph{Signal convergence and reference reliability.}
We assess the reliability of HH (Human-to-human) SBERT as a difficulty measure by examining its correlation with two independent behavioral signals---response time (RT) and subjective confidence---collected alongside answers.
If HH SBERT captures genuine question difficulty, harder questions (lower agreement) should take longer and elicit lower confidence.
This is confirmed (Figure~\ref{fig:conf_rt_sbert_triangle}): RT correlates strongly negatively with HH SBERT across all variants ($r{=}{-}0.854$ to $-0.860$; all $p{<}.001$), and confidence tracks positively in two of three variants ($r{=}0.228$--$0.299$; $p{<}.016$)---converging on the same latent difficulty dimension from three independent measures.
SBERT is insensitive to directional bias within narrow answer spaces (\textit{yes/no}, \textit{count}), where scores remain artificially high regardless of anchor removal; we address this with distributional alignment in \S\ref{sec:dist_alignment}.
% We therefore complement SBERT with categorical distribution alignment for binary and count questions (Section~\ref{sec:dist_alignment}).

\paragraph{Structural patterns.}
Human blind agreement varies selectively across question types (Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation}).
\textit{Existence} questions appear near-ceiling and stable across control variants, but this reflects the narrow \textit{yes/no} answer space rather than a genuine prior---yes and no are close in embedding space, so HH SBERT remains high regardless of actual agreement (see Table~\ref{tab:q113_sample_composition} for the full question-type breakdown).
Within operation types, \textit{identity} questions are the most anchor-sensitive: HH SBERT
drops from $0.67$ to $0.34$ under pronominalization, as the referent disappears
and answers scatter.
For example, \textit{``What type of bear is this?''} \emph{(Identity; Animal)} sees humans converge on specific
species when the anchor is present, but shift to uncoordinated responses without it.
\textit{Count} questions are the most anchor-robust for humans ($\Delta_{\text{HH}}{=}{+}0.008$), as numerical priors persist even after the object is removed (models, by contrast, collapse to a zero-default regardless of anchor presence (Section~\ref{sec:dist_alignment})).
Within entity types, \textit{food} and \textit{animal} questions show the steepest degradation:
\textit{``What type of bread does this appear to be?''} \emph{(Attribute; Food;} Weaker: \textit{``food''};
Pronominalized: \textit{``it''}) sees humans converge on specific bread types
(\textit{baguette}, \textit{sandwich bread}) under the original phrasing, but shift
to less well-defined, broader categories once the anchor is removed (HH SBERT $0.53 \to 0.32$).

\paragraph{Presence prior in humans, absence default in models.}
Figure~\ref{fig:dist_human_vs_model} shows the ground truth of our actual \textit{yes/no} VQA questions (when presented \textit{with} image) leans \textit{no} (34.6\% \textit{yes}). When prompted with questions and instruction, humans answer \textit{yes} on 65\% of questions---a positive-world bias that diverges sharply from the benchmark dataset.
Importantly, most full VLMs are not aligned to the human pattern: LLaVA and Qwen3-VL models usually stay between 15.4\% and 38.5\% \textit{yes}, far below the human 65.4\% (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:yesno_distribution_all} for full values). This suggests that, for these families, the vision encoder treats the black image as a visually empty scene rather than an unspecified one; InternVL, however, is the main exception: the full VLM reaches 69.2\% \textit{yes}, compared with 65.4\% for humans and 80.8\% for its paired decoder. 

The same split appears on \textit{count} questions: LLaVA and Qwen3-VL collapse toward ``0'', whereas full InternVL remains the main exception (see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:count_transition_all} for all models distributions). Figure~\ref{fig:count_transition} shows that full InternVL and its decoder spread mass across small counts (``1'', ``2--3'') rather than collapsing as sharply to ``0'', unlike the other full VLMs and the standalone Qwen3 models, including the thinking model. At the pooled distribution level, InternVL~(LM) is also among the closest models to the human count distribution (JS$=.599$; Table~\ref{tab:hm_grouped_distribution_7b_compact_filtered}). However, this comes with unusually high count-question abstention of 29.5\%, which is already reduced by instruction from roughly 38.5\% (See Table~\ref{tab:abstention_by_qtype} for all abstention changes).
% Across both \textit{yes/no} and \textit{count} questions, standalone LLMs show a different failure: they often exit the answer space entirely, replacing content answers with refusals, topic-noun extractions, or hedged reasoning (Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_calibration}).

\paragraph{Can instruction restore scene imagination?}
Given the VLM zero-default established above, can explicit instructions to \emph{imagine a scene} actually activate scene imagination?
The answer is 'no'---not for models whose gap stems from a learned zero-default. Instruction only recovers human-like counts when the deficit is driven by abstention.
InternVL (VLM) is already the closest to the human count distribution before instruction (JS$=.559$, the lowest of any model), yet instruction slightly worsens alignment ($\Delta\mathrm{JS}{=}{+}.027$): the instruction shifts mass \emph{toward} ``0'' (12\%$\to$27\%; Appendix~Figure~\ref{fig:count_transition_all}), revealing that ``imagining a scene'' activates the zero-default rather than restoring genuine count imagination. On well-anchored questions such as \emph{``How many stripes does the zebra have?''} InternVL answers ``10'' with or without the prompt, staying within the human spread of 2--50+ (Figure~\ref{fig:count_transition}; see \S\ref{sec:ablation})---but this does not generalize across count questions.
For Qwen3 models, where count-question abstention reaches 23.1\% without instruction, the prompt substantially reduces refusals: Qwen3~(Think) shows the largest reduction ($\Delta\mathrm{Abs}{=}{-}20.5\%$), closing the largest distributional gap of any model ($\Delta\mathrm{JS}{=}{-}.146$) and coming closest to the human count distribution overall.
For LLaVA-Mistral and LLaVA-Vicuna, instruction moves answers off ``0'' but into implausible values (50 and 100 on the zebra question); count JS remains above $.78$ ($\Delta\mathrm{JS}{=}{-}.048$ and $+.007$), showing that breaking the zero-default is not sufficient to reach the human range.

\subsection{Semantic Alignment}
\label{sec:alignment_main}

\paragraph{Anchor-robust and anchor-sensitive groups: do models track the human pattern?}
Section~\ref{sec:human_patterns} established that humans are anchor-robust on \textit{Existence} and \textit{Action} questions and anchor-sensitive on \textit{Identity} and \textit{Spatial} questions.
Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_sbert} shows mean HM SBERT per group and architecture class; groups whose model points fall below the diagonal indicate systematic under-alignment relative to the human baseline.

\textit{Anchor-robust groups.}
\textit{Existence} and \textit{Action} show near-zero $\Delta$ for both humans and all model classes---models maintain stable priors on these groups even as anchors are progressively removed.
The same flatness holds under accuracy (Appendix~\ref{fig:combined_accuracy}), confirming this is not a SBERT artifact.

\textit{Anchor-sensitive groups and model over-degradation.}
\textit{Spatial} is the most over-degraded group across all architectures: human $\Delta{=}{-}0.039$ while VLMs reach $\Delta{=}{-}0.115$ (excess ${=}{-}0.075$) and backbone decoders $\Delta{=}{-}0.084$ (excess ${=}{-}0.045$).
Once the referent noun is removed, model responses disperse far more than human responses do, revealing a stronger surface-cue dependence than the human baseline.
\textit{Identity} shows an architecture split: backbone decoders nearly match human degradation ($\Delta{=}{-}0.192$; human ${=}{-}0.193$), while VLMs and SA-LLMs over-degrade ($\Delta{=}{-}0.220$ and ${-}0.246$ respectively).
Accuracy profiles confirm the same collapse pattern across all families (Appendix~\ref{fig:combined_accuracy}).

\textit{Count as a distinct failure mode.}
\textit{Count} does not follow the over-degradation pattern.
Humans are slightly \emph{more} concordant without the anchor ($\Delta{=}{+}0.010$), as removing the specific referent causes convergence on generic quantity terms.
VLMs are flat ($\Delta{\approx}0$)---the zero-default suppresses any response to anchor removal---while SA-LLMs show the opposite, collapsing sharply ($\Delta{=}{-}0.132$).
This is a qualitatively different failure mode from Identity and Spatial: models are not over-sensitive to the anchor but instead override it entirely with a corpus shortcut.

\textit{Operation vs.\ entity concordance.}
We quantify whether models rank groups by anchor-sensitivity the same way humans do via Spearman $\rho$ between HH $\Delta$ and model $\Delta$ across all groups (Appendix~\ref{fig:delta_bar}).
On the operation dimension, backbone decoders show the strongest concordance ($\rho{=}0.69$, $p{<}.001$); VLMs are moderate ($\rho{=}0.48$, $p{<}.05$); SA-LLMs are non-significant ($\rho{=}0.25$, $p{=}.27$), reflecting their divergent Count and World Knowledge profiles.
The entity dimension shows stronger concordance overall: backbone $\rho{=}0.95$, VLM $\rho{=}0.76$, SA-LLM $\rho{=}0.56$ (all $p{<}.05$).
Backbone decoders rank entity groups most faithfully, with InternVL as the exception---its encoder selectively enriches \emph{visual} entity priors (\textit{Place}, \textit{Vehicle}, \textit{Animal}), making the VLM less anchor-dependent than its backbone on those groups (Appendix~\ref{tab:internvl_anchoring}; \S\ref{sec:ablation}).

\section{What Modulates Alignment}
\label{sec:ablation}

\paragraph{Visual encoding.}
% The backbone decoder advantage is not a black-image artifact.
To test the VLMs on their resilience (impact) against the image input, we use black (default blank image), gray, noise, and white on Qwen3-VL, LLaVA-Mistral, and InternVL (Full ablation results are in Table~\ref{tab:image_ablation_main} in Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}). %  reveals a clear architectural split 
For Qwen3-VL and LLaVA-Mistral, no image condition recovers backbone-level alignment:
Qwen3-VL SBERT ranges from $0.490$ (noise) to $0.628$ (white), never reaching the backbone's $0.651$;
LLaVA-Mistral is more stable ($0.590$--$0.626$, backbone $0.662$).
InternVL reverses this pattern: its VLM alignment under any image condition ($0.639$--$0.660$) exceeds its own backbone ($0.575$), with a SBERT range of only $0.021$ across all image types---the narrowest of the three models.
% This suggests that InternVL's encoder enriches, rather than suppresses, the language prior when
% processing uninformative visual input, making the image type essentially irrelevant to alignment.

Notably, InternVL and Qwen3-VL share the same Qwen3 language backbone,
making their divergence a direct comparison of vision encoder joint-training effect: Qwen3-VL's
encoder \emph{reduces} semantic similarity relative to its backbone across all variants (SBERT: $0.561{\to}0.539$ on pronominalized; $0.593{\to}0.572$ on original), while InternVL's encoder \emph{raises} it ($0.519{\to}0.571$; $0.609{\to}0.620$).
This divergence suggests the difference originates in the vision encoder's architecture and joint training rather than the shared language backbone~\citep{tong2024eyes,mckinzie2024mm1}---though the precise mechanism remains open, raising the question of what qualitatively distinguishes InternVL's encoder response to uninformative visual input.

\paragraph{Scale.}
Scaling up VLMs does not reliably improve human--model alignment, and the
relationship between scale and accuracy also varies by family
(Table~\ref{tab:comprehensive_inst_blind} in Appendix~\ref{app:scale_variant}).
For Qwen3-VL, both VLM accuracy and SBERT peak at 4B ($0.501$; $0.568$) and return
near 2B levels at 8B ($0.473$; $0.561$)---neither improves monotonically with scale.
The paired backbone decoders follow the same non-monotonic SBERT pattern,
peaking at 4B ($0.595$) and falling at 8B ($0.588$)---indicating that
larger backbones absorb more VQA-style training signal without producing
a more human-like prior distribution.
For LLaVA-Vicuna (7B--13B), VLM accuracy and SBERT both rise with scale
($0.516{\to}0.524$; $0.544{\to}0.563$), while the backbone gains accuracy
($0.445{\to}0.472$) with a marginal SBERT decrease ($0.593{\to}0.588$).
InternVL presents the clearest example of accuracy--alignment decoupling driven by abstention: its backbone SBERT \emph{decreases} with scale ($0.551{\to}0.534{\to}0.469$, 1B to 8B on pronominalized); larger backbones also abstain more on count questions (29.5\% at 8B; Table~\ref{tab:abstention_by_qtype}).
The vision encoder eliminates this abstention entirely (0.0\% across all sizes), recovering SBERT above the backbone---so the encoder's apparent alignment benefit is partly an abstention-suppression effect rather than genuine prior enrichment (see also \S\ref{sec:ablation}).
Yet InternVL's VLM also leads its backbone on anchor-rich original questions (Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_sbert}), where abstention is negligible for both, suggesting genuine prior enrichment may also be present---distinguishing InternVL architecturally from the other families.

confined to 1.7B ($\Delta{=}{+}0.110$, $p{<}.001$), while 0.6B shows no
significant gain ($\Delta{=}{+}0.000$, $p{=}.317$); the pooled effect
($\Delta{=}{+}0.048$, $p{<}.001$; Table~\ref{tab:thinking_wilcoxon},
Appendix~\ref{app:thinking}).
Thinking therefore behaves more like a size-dependent decoding intervention
than a general route to more human-like blind priors
(Figure~\ref{fig:think_abs_vs_length}).
Longer traces also correlate with higher final-answer token confidence

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Backbone decoders carry the most human-calibrated prior.}
% Vision co-training exposes the language backbone to dense, scene-descriptive language---image captions that encode how often things are present, what typical counts look like, and how objects co-occur---so the backbone acquires a prior over visual world states that standalone LLMs, trained on more varied and abstract text, do not develop to the same degree.
% This co-training embeds rich, human-like linguistic priors in the language backbone, but the vision encoder introduces distributional biases that push responses away from those priors when no meaningful visual content is present.
Let $P(a \mid q)$ denote the language-only answer distribution given question $q$, and $P(a \mid q, v_0)$ the VLM distribution conditioned on both the question and an uninformative (blank) image $v_0$.
A backbone decoder approximates $P(a \mid q)$ directly, while a full VLM produces $P(a \mid q, v_0)$, which may differ from the pure language prior if the encoder treats $v_0$ as a meaningful signal.
We assume this finding is because the backbone decoder---approximating $P(a \mid q)$ rather than $P(a \mid q, v_0)$---best reproduces human blind priors not because it is more capable, but because the vision encoder actively suppresses the language prior when processing an uninformative image, treating the blank input as a meaningful null signal rather than ignoring it.
This reframes the problem: the limitation is not that VLMs are ``too visual,'' but that they lack a reliable way to distinguish informative from uninformative visual states \citep{lee2024vlind, han2024instinctive}.
The same suppression likely operates on genuinely difficult real images---low contrast, heavy occlusion, unusual viewpoints---where the visual signal is present but weak \citep{ye2024mmspubench, chen2024are}, suggesting that blind-prior alignment is a diagnostic for how well a model will behave under visual uncertainty, not only under complete absence.
In order to improve the alignment, one could, for example, use learned input-uncertainty gating---the model should modulate how much it defers to the visual pathway based on estimated input quality, falling back on its language prior when the visual signal is uninformative.

\paragraph{Lexical anchors as prior activators, structured by operation type.}
The control-variant degradation reveals that blind model success is
predominantly carried by content-noun lexical cues. When these are stripped,
models diverge from human priors while humans barely change. This effect
is not uniform, however: 
Commonsense-grounded operations (\textit{Existence}, \textit{Action}, \textit{World Knowledge}) are
anchor-robust as both humans and models draw on stable scene priors that survive
pronominalization.
In contrast, referent-specific operations (\textit{Identity}, \textit{Spatial}) are
anchor-sensitive as once the content noun is removed, both human agreement and
model alignment collapse, but models fall further (Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_sbert}).
This shows that model
answers in these categories are driven more by lexical surface cues than by
robust world knowledge.
Crucially, the operation-level anchoring hierarchy is architecture-invariant (Spearman $\rho$ between HH and model $\Delta$: backbone $\rho{=}0.69$, $p{<}.001$; VLM $\rho{=}0.48$, $p{<}.05$; Appendix~\ref{fig:delta_bar}), whereas entity-type anchor sensitivity is architecture-dependent ($\rho$: backbone $0.95$, VLM $0.76$, SA-LLM $0.56$): backbone decoders rank entity groups by anchor-sensitivity more faithfully than most VLMs, reflecting that the vision encoder selectively suppresses entity-level priors.
This distinction suggests a concrete target for benchmark design: pronominalization of \textit{identity} and \textit{spatial} questions acts as a stress test that separates genuine prior-driven behaviour from entity-anchored lexical pattern-matching.

\paragraph{Vision co-training enriches language priors.}
The backbone decoder advantage over standalone LLMs is arguably more revealing than
its advantage over full VLMs.
Backbone decoders and their matched standalone counterparts share the same underlying
architecture. %  and training objective---the only difference is that backbone decoders were trained jointly on image--caption pairs.
Yet backbone decoders substantially outperform matched standalone LLMs
($\Delta{=}{+}0.087$ to ${+}0.337$ HM SBERT), despite never processing any image at test time.
We assume that this gap is the signature of vision co-training enriching the language prior: exposure
to dense, scene-descriptive caption language embeds structured world knowledge---what
things look like, how often they appear, how they co-occur---that text-only pretraining
does not develop to the same degree.
The vision encoder temporarily suppresses these priors on uninformative input,
but the priors themselves seem to be a product of multimodal training.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Participants were recruited from university communities, limiting demographic scope.
Model evaluation uses greedy decoding, which may underestimate answer diversity, which should be explored in future research. 
Human data covers only the instruction-blind condition; corpus-frequency correlation
\citep{mccoy2024embers} and broader image ablations are similarly left to future work.
Although benchmark contamination may also amplify blind performance (see \citet{chen2024are} show that VLMs can achieve non-trivial scores without visual input partly due to training-set leakage), we stress that our pronominalization test partially controls for this, since models that rely on memorized question--answer pairs should degrade more sharply when content nouns are removed.

\section{Conclusion}

This work studies how models respond to visual questions in the absence of any image, and how closely that behavior resembles the blind priors that humans themselves express under the same condition. We find that backbone decoders---the language components of VLMs with the vision encoder bypassed---come closest to human blind prior structures, nearly reaching the human--human agreement ceiling and consistently outperforming both full VLMs and standalone LLMs.
This confirms the intuition that VLM language decoders carry rich world knowledge from multimodal training, while also showing that the vision encoder introduces a distributional pull on uninformative images that pushes responses away from those very priors.
This pattern holds across four of five matched families; the exception is InternVL, whose full VLM slightly leads its backbone decoder---a reversal we attribute to architectural differences in its training recipe (dynamic-resolution tiling, contrastive alignment against a large InternViT encoder) that may better preserve language priors under uninformative visual input.
How such architectural choices interact with blind-prior alignment more broadly is left to future work.

% High blind accuracy does not accompany this: the models with the strongest shortcut
% behavior score well on VQA metrics while diverging from human response distributions,
% underscoring that accuracy alone is an unreliable proxy for whether a model is
% doing what humans do.
We also find that the alignment varies by the reasoning demand of the question.
  rather than its semantic domain
\textit{Existence} and \textit{Action} questions carry stable priors that transfer across control variants---they ask whether something is present or occurring, which humans and models like LLaVA-Mistral and InternVL can answer from general scene expectations alone.
\textit{Identity} and \textit{Spatial} questions, however, require knowing \emph{what} or \emph{where} a specific referent is: without the content noun, the question loses its grounding entirely, and model alignment collapses more sharply than human agreement does.
This reveals that the backbone decoder's advantage is itself referent-dependent---
co-training instills strong priors for commonsense operations but cannot substitute
for the visual specificity that questions like \textit{Identity} and \textit{Spatial} types demand.
