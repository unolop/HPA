\begin{abstract}
When Vision-Language Models (VLMs) answer VQA questions without seeing any
image, how closely do their ``imagined'' answers match those of humans under
the same condition? Using a human blind-answer dataset (N=40, 113 shared
questions, IRB-approved) and 37 models spanning four architectural groups, we
measure semantic agreement rather than ground-truth accuracy---separating
linguistic prior alignment from training-set leakage.
A counterintuitive finding emerges: the model group most aligned with human
blind priors is not the full VLM but the \emph{backbone decoder}---the same
weights with the vision encoder bypassed. Image-condition ablations
(blank, gray, noise, white) confirm that the vision encoder has no neutral
mode: even on uninformative input it actively modulates the language model's
output distribution, pushing answers away from the human linguistic prior.
To isolate which lexical cues carry prior information, we introduce
\emph{control question variants}: progressive reformulations that strip
content-noun anchors (Original $\to$ Weaker $\to$ Pronominalized). Model agreement
drops monotonically while the human--human ceiling barely moves, confirming
lexical-anchor dependence. Question-type decomposition shows where alignment
holds (yes/no, action) and where it breaks (count, grounded reference),
providing a diagnostic vocabulary for linguistic prior exploitation in any
multimodal benchmark.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) achieve impressive performance on visual question
answering (VQA) benchmarks \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}, yet a basic
question remains: how much of this performance reflects genuine visual
grounding, and how much is explained by linguistic priors inherited from
large-scale pretraining? This question is sharpened by the benchmark's own
design history: VQA~v2.0 was explicitly constructed to reduce language bias
by pairing each question with complementary images that force different correct
answers \citep{goyal2016making}. Yet recent evaluation audits show that visual
content is often still unnecessary in practice: \citet{chen2024are} report
substantial no-image performance and benchmark-level leakage effects across
current multimodal evaluations. Consistent with this, in our blind VQA setting,
modern VLMs still answer 35--49\% of questions correctly without seeing any image.

This question parallels a well-known episode in Natural Language Inference
(NLI): \citet{poliak2018hypothesis} showed that a model trained on hypotheses
alone substantially exceeded the random baseline across ten NLI datasets,
revealing annotation artifacts in the benchmarks themselves
\citep{torralba2011unbiased}. The same diagnostic was applied early in VQA:
\citet{jabri2016revisiting} showed that text-only baselines on VQA~v1.0
achieved substantial accuracy without visual input, and \citet{zhang2016yin}
documented a pronounced yes-bias that motivated the construction of VQA~v2.0.
We extend this diagnostic tradition to modern VLMs: the \emph{image} plays
the role of the premise, and the question is the hypothesis. A model that
answers correctly without the image exploits a hypothesis-only shortcut.

\citet{mccoy2024embers} provide the mechanistic explanation: large language
models are shaped by next-word prediction over internet-scale text and produce
outputs proportional to the probability of sequences in the training corpus.
When a VQA question has a high-frequency answer in training data, a VLM will
often produce it regardless of the image. Blind VQA performance therefore
provides a practical diagnostic of corpus-frequency exploitation, though not a
pure measure of it.

What distinguishes our study from prior diagnostics is the human reference
dataset. We recruited 40 participants to answer the same VQA questions under
the same blind condition, providing a natural baseline for \emph{linguistically
plausible} uncertainty. This reveals a counterintuitive finding: the backbone
decoder---the VLM's language component with the vision encoder removed---is
the model group most closely aligned with human blind priors. Full VLMs,
despite incorporating the vision pathway during pretraining, are \emph{less}
human-like in their priors. Image-condition ablations confirm this is not
because the blank image is adversarial: the vision encoder actively modulates
outputs on \emph{any} uninformative image (blank, gray, noise, white),
shifting the language backbone away from the human prior it would otherwise
express.

We study 37 models from four architectural groups around three questions:
how closely do blind model priors align with human blind reasoning, how does
that alignment change as lexical anchors are removed, and what role does the
vision encoder play in shaping or distorting the resulting prior? We answer
these with blind evaluation on 1,000 VQA questions, a control-variant
perturbation suite, image-condition ablations, and a human blind-answer
dataset (N=40, 113 shared questions).

\paragraph{Main findings.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The backbone decoder paradox.} VLM backbone decoders (same
    weights, vision encoder bypassed) are the model group closest to human
    blind priors in semantic agreement. Architecture determines alignment;
    scale does not close the gap.

    \item \textbf{Lexical-anchor dependence.} As content-noun anchors are
    progressively stripped (Original $\to$ Weaker $\to$ Pronominalized),
    model--human agreement drops monotonically while the human--human ceiling
    barely moves, confirming that blind success is carried by
    lexical anchor signals.

    \item \textbf{The vision encoder has no neutral mode.} Image-condition
    ablations show that VLM outputs change substantially across uninformative
    images (blank, gray, noise, white). The backbone decoder, free from this
    modulation, falls back on the pure linguistic prior---which is the most
    human-like.

    \item \textbf{Question-type specificity.} Backbone decoders lead full
    VLMs across major operation types; count and grounded-reference questions
    are the largest and most persistent human--model mismatch regions.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Methodological contributions.}
\textbf{(i)} A semantic control-question extraction pipeline generating four
progressively weaker reformulations via GPT-4.1-mini, forming a controlled
degradation axis that isolates which lexical elements carry prior information.
\textbf{(ii)} An operation--entity taxonomy annotating each of 1,000 VQA
questions with operation type and entity type, enabling fine-grained
prior-structure diagnosis.
\textbf{(iii)} A human blind-answer dataset (N=40, 113 shared questions across
three control variants) providing a natural ceiling for linguistically
plausible prior alignment.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Hypothesis-Only and Shortcut Baselines.}
Dataset bias and hypothesis-only shortcuts have been recognized as a
fundamental challenge across vision and language benchmarks
\citep{torralba2011unbiased}. In NLI, \citet{poliak2018hypothesis} demonstrated
that classifiers achieve non-trivial accuracy using only the hypothesis,
exposing annotation artifacts; \citet{gururangan2018annotation} characterized
these through systematic analysis. In VQA, the dynamic was recognized early:
\citet{jabri2016revisiting} showed text-only baselines on VQA~v1.0 performed
surprisingly well without visual input, and \citet{zhang2016yin} identified
a pronounced yes-bias motivating the VQA~v2.0 redesign. Subsequent models remained competitive on VQA~v2.0 without genuine grounding \citep{agrawal2018dont}, and counterfactual intervention methods have attempted to remove these biases \citep{niu2021counterfactual}. Our work extends this diagnostic tradition to modern VLMs, using
a human reference dataset to calibrate which shortcuts are linguistically
natural versus model-specific artifacts.

\paragraph{LMs as Corpus Frequency Models.}
\citet{mccoy2024embers} showed that LLM failure patterns are predicted by the
probability of task, input, and output in the training corpus. \citet{kalai2025hallucinate}
provide a complementary theoretical account: hallucinations arise as statistically
natural errors under corpus-frequency pressure, and benchmarks should credit abstention
rather than penalizing uncertainty. We operationalize both perspectives: blind VQA
accuracy directly measures the probability of question-answer pairs in the pretraining
distribution, and our overconfidence finding shows that current VLMs suppress abstention
under visual deprivation.

\paragraph{VLM Hallucination and Shortcut Learning.}
Hallucination in VLMs has been widely documented
\citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object,xiao2021hallucination}.
\citet{li2023evaluating} introduced POPE, a yes/no probing benchmark showing
that VLMs systematically hallucinate objects; our finding that VLMs default to
``no'' at 64\% under blind conditions provides a mechanistic complement,
identifying the prior source of yes/no hallucination bias.
\citet{yuksekgonul2023when} showed that VLMs often behave like bags-of-words,
losing compositional binding between visual and linguistic tokens; our blind
results show this failure extends to effectively ignoring the image entirely.
\citet{chen2024are} proposed MMStar to filter questions solvable without
images; VLind-Bench \citep{lee2024vlind} measures language prior influence
across benchmarks; \citet{han2024instinctive} identified visual shortcuts
through spurious image features; \citet{ye2024mmspubench} systematically
benchmark these spurious biases across nine categories in multimodal LLMs;
and \citet{lu2025mitigating} propose zero-shot debiasing of VLM text-vision
embeddings to mitigate the resulting correlations. Our work provides the
diagnostic complement: characterizing the linguistic prior that \emph{drives}
spurious success when visual input is absent.
In parallel, \citet{kim2025subtlebench} show VLMs fail
at fine-grained visual discrimination even when images are present---bounding
the visual grounding gap from the opposite direction.

\paragraph{Human-Model Alignment in VQA.}
\citet{lan2025mind} show that VLM confidence distributions diverge from human
uncertainty distributions (measured via TVD, KL, and entropy calibration error),
and that calibrating toward accuracy can \emph{widen} this gap: the higher-accuracy
BEiT3 is less aligned with human distributions than the lower-accuracy LXMERT.
This directly parallels our SBERT--accuracy dissociation: VLMs outperform humans
by $+19$\% on blind accuracy yet are less semantically aligned with humans than
backbone decoders. Both findings challenge the assumption that accuracy improvement
implies human-alignment improvement. \citet{lee2023visalign} similarly measure
AI-human alignment in visual perception; we focus specifically on the linguistic
prior regime where visual input is absent. Our work differs from both in providing
control-variant and architectural ablations that diagnose \emph{why} divergence occurs.

\paragraph{Model Calibration and Overconfidence.}
\citet{guo2017calibration} showed that modern neural networks are
systematically overconfident---predicted probabilities do not reflect true
correctness likelihoods. We find the same pathology in the generative VLM
setting: blind outputs remain in a high token-confidence regime even for wrong
committed answers, making this failure invisible to accuracy-only evaluation.
\citet{kadavath2022language} showed LLMs can express calibrated uncertainty
when asked to predict their own correctness; under our explicit no-image
instruction, model confidence \emph{increases} instead---indicating committed
hallucination rather than acknowledged uncertainty.

\paragraph{Human Cognition Under Uncertainty.}
Dual-process theory \citep{tversky1974judgment,kahneman2011thinking} describes
how System~1 heuristics rely on representativeness and availability. Humans do
not share the corpus-frequency bias of pretrained LLMs: their yes-bias (65\%
``yes'') reflects pragmatic plausibility rather than training-distribution
statistics, making human blind behavior the natural reference for linguistically
natural uncertainty.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Models and Dataset}

We evaluate VLMs from the Qwen3-VL \citep{bai2025qwen3vl}, LLaVA
\citep{liu2023visual,liu2024llavanext}, and InternVL \citep{zhu2025internvl3}
families, plus standalone LLM baselines (Qwen3 \citep{yang2025qwen3},
Qwen2.5-7B(-Instruct), Mistral-7B, Vicuna-7B/13B, Phi-3.5-mini).
All evaluations are zero-shot at temperature~0. Models are organized into four groups:
\textbf{VLM} (full vision-language stack), \textbf{Backbone decoder} (same
checkpoint, vision encoder bypassed), \textbf{standalone LLM}, and
\textbf{standalone LLM (think)} (Qwen3 think variants). A 7/8B matched subset is
used for fair scale-controlled comparisons.
Importantly, \textbf{Backbone decoder} runs are weight-identical to their paired
VLM checkpoints and differ only by removing image input at inference, whereas
\textbf{standalone LLM} runs use separate family-matched checkpoints (e.g.,
LLaVA-Mistral$\leftrightarrow$Mistral-7B, InternVL-8B$\leftrightarrow$Qwen3-8B)
rather than decoders extracted from the VLM weights.

Our evaluation dataset is 1,000 questions sampled from the VQA v2.0 validation
split \citep{goyal2016making}, spanning yes/no, numerical, and open-ended
types. The subset covers the full semantic range of the pool, confirmed by
t-SNE embedding visualization (Appendix~\ref{app:tsne}).

\subsection{Blind Inference Conditions}

Three conditions: (1)~\textbf{Standard}: original image with question;
(2)~\textbf{Blind}: blank 224$\times$224 black PNG replacing the image;
(3)~\textbf{Blind+Inst}: blank image plus the instruction
\textit{``Note: No images are provided. For each question, imagine an
appropriate image exists and answer based on the most common or universal
scenario.''}
Image-content ablations (gray and noise images) confirm that observed
behavior is driven by linguistic priors rather than pixel statistics
(Appendix~\ref{app:image_ablation}).

\subsection{Control Question Variants and Semantic Taxonomy}
\label{sec:control}

\textbf{Control question extraction.}
We generate four progressively weaker reformulations of each question using
GPT-4.1-mini (temperature~0, seed~42, JSON schema mode):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{deictic\_removed}: Deictic markers and spatial qualifiers removed.
    \item \textbf{object\_removed}: Object noun replaced with ``object.''
    \item \textbf{weaker\_object} (variant~B): Noun replaced by one-level-up
    hypernym (dog $\to$ animal, car $\to$ vehicle).
    \item \textbf{pronominalized} (variant~A): All content nouns replaced by
    minimal pronouns.
\end{enumerate}
Original~(C) $\to$ Weaker~(B) $\to$ Pronominalized~(A) forms a monotonically
decreasing axis of lexical prior cues. Variant~A is not limited to the main
entity mention: it replaces all content noun phrases with minimal pronouns
while preserving question structure and predicate words. The prompt and schema are in
Appendix~\ref{app:ctl}.

\textbf{Operation--entity taxonomy.}
Each question is annotated with (1)~\emph{operation type}: yes/no-existence,
count, attribute, action, world knowledge, spatial, identity, temporal,
text/OCR, comparison, causal; and (2)~\emph{entity type}: person, animal,
object, food, vehicle, place, other.

\subsection{Human Data Collection}

N=40 participants were recruited via online university community forums under
an IRB-approved protocol (10,000~KRW/hour). Participants answered text-only
Blind+Inst questions in Korean or English; responses were translated and
normalized. We applied a raw-response qualification threshold ($\geq$348)
before subset intersection, then restricted analysis to the 113 shared
question IDs answered by all qualifying participants across variants
(A/B/C; 339 responses per participant, $h$=40). Human--model comparisons use this 113-question subset
under the inst\_blind model condition. Main semantic-agreement figures also
use this same $q=113$ subset; free-text-only slices are treated as robustness
checks. For instruction-effect HM deltas and common-set filtering, we use the
88-question open-ended slice within this subset to avoid yes/no lexical
ceiling effects. Full protocol in Appendix~\ref{app:human}.

\subsection{Metrics}

\textbf{Task accuracy} uses the standard VQA soft evaluation
\citep{antol2015vqa}. \textbf{Semantic agreement} uses Sentence-BERT cosine
\citep{wang2020minilm} as the primary metric and SimCSE \citep{gao2021simcse}
as robustness check; BERTScore is excluded due to length bias on short answers.
We additionally report lexical overlap metrics (token Jaccard, ROUGE-1, chrF,
exact match) to verify that semantic trends are not artifacts of any single
metric family. chrF scores are computed with the \texttt{sacrebleu}
implementation.
\textbf{Difficulty correlation} is Pearson~$r$ between per-question human
accuracy and per-model accuracy. \textbf{Krippendorff's $\alpha$} (nominal)
measures within- and cross-group reliability. \textbf{Token confidence} is
mean token probability from output logprobs.

\textbf{Answer normalization.}
All answers undergo VQA-standard preprocessing before scoring: stripping
articles (\textit{a, an, the}), normalizing punctuation, and converting
number words to digits \citep{antol2015vqa}. Answer length varies
substantially across model groups: VLMs and backbone decoders produce
median 1-word answers, while standalone LLMs average 10.1 words
(Mistral-7B: 29.7 words). Since embedding-based metrics penalize length
mismatch (SBERT $r=-0.33$ with word count; BERTScore $r=-0.53$), we
report both raw and length-normalized results (Eval-Normalized below).

\subsection{Evaluation Protocols and Confound Controls}
\label{sec:eval_protocols}

To separate content-level agreement from refusal/verbosity artifacts, we report
two complementary protocols for human--model (HM) agreement.
\textbf{Eval-Raw (inclusive)} evaluates all available responses and preserves
abstention behavior as part of model behavior. \textbf{Eval-Filtered
(substantive-common set)} filters to questions where models provided
substantive answers under Blind+Inst and compares groups on the same question
IDs. We use a tiered filter by abstention count per question (strict: 0
abstentions; relaxed: $\leq$1 abstention); the main robustness comparison uses
the relaxed shared set due to higher coverage.

Outputs are classified as \textit{hard-abstained}, \textit{soft-abstained},
\textit{committed-correct}, and \textit{committed-wrong}. For instruction
effects, we report three outcome families: (i) HM semantic agreement shift
(blind $\rightarrow$ inst\_blind), (ii) abstention shift, and (iii) calibration
shift (ECE and reliability curves). This decomposition prevents improvements in
agreement from being misread when they are caused only by reduced refusal.

\textbf{Eval-Normalized (length-normalized)} addresses the confound that
standalone LLMs produce substantially longer answers, which penalizes
embedding-based similarity metrics regardless of content overlap.
We apply a cleaning pipeline: (1)~strip explicit refusals using a regex
classifier; (2)~strip preamble patterns (e.g., \textit{``The answer is''},
\textit{``I think it is''}) on raw text; (3)~strip trailing elaboration
after sentence-final punctuation followed by discourse markers;
(4)~apply VQA answer normalization; (5)~truncate to $\leq$5 words.
SBERT embeddings are recomputed on the cleaned text. This protocol
isolates content-level agreement from format differences across model
groups.

\section{Human-Model Blind Alignment}
\label{sec:alignment}

We begin with the central question: when models answer VQA questions
without seeing any image, how closely do their ``imagined'' answers
match those of humans under the same condition? Rather than measuring
accuracy against ground truth---which conflates linguistic prior
exploitation with genuine visual reasoning---we measure \emph{semantic
agreement} between blind model outputs and blind human responses. The
results section proceeds in four steps: overall alignment, the role of
architecture versus scale, the question types on which agreement holds or
breaks, and finally the mechanism behind the backbone advantage.

\subsection{Alignment Across Control Variants}
\label{sec:degradation}

(Original~C $\to$ Weaker~B $\to$ Pronominalized~A; $q=113$, $h=40$).
Human accuracy (dashed) remains stable; model accuracy drops 15--20

Figure~\ref{fig:variants} tracks human-model agreement as content noun
phrases are weakened from original question~(C) to hypernym-weakened~(B)
to fully pronominalized~(A). Two findings stand out:

\textbf{The backbone decoder paradox.} Across all three variants,
backbone decoders (orange, dotted) achieve the highest human-model SBERT
agreement---consistently above full VLMs at equivalent scale and approaching
the human-human ceiling ($\overline{\text{SBERT}}_{\text{HH}} = 0.510$
on free-text). Removing the vision encoder does not hurt human alignment;
it \emph{improves} it. Vision co-training embeds human-like priors in the
language backbone, but the vision encoder active on a blank image introduces
distributional biases that push answers away from the human prior.

\textbf{Lexical-anchor dependence.} Model accuracy drops 15--20 pp from
C to A while human accuracy barely changes. The SBERT lineplot shows
the same degradation semantically: standalone LLM groups collapse most
steeply, while backbone decoders maintain the highest alignment throughout.
The human-human ceiling drops only slightly, confirming that human blind
reasoning does not depend on content-noun anchors in the same way as models.

Figure~\ref{fig:family} decomposes the group-level trend into individual
model families. In Row~1, backbone decoders consistently match or exceed

blind conditions. In Row~2, Qwen3 standalone LLMs show clear scale

consistent with its high verbosity (mean 29.7 words per answer).

\subsection{Architecture Dominates Scale}
\label{sec:scale}

\centering

\hfill%

\textbf{Left:} Blind VQA accuracy versus parameter count. VLMs (red) and

Figure~\ref{fig:scale} shows that the main alignment result is architectural,
not merely a scale artifact. On accuracy (left), VLMs and backbone decoders
both exceed the human baseline across parameter counts, while standalone LLMs
fall far below. On SBERT agreement (right), backbone decoders approach the
human-human ceiling at 7--8B, while full VLMs at equivalent scale remain
lower. The largest VLMs (32B) do not converge with their backbones, and the
scale $\times$ variant analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:scale_variant}) shows that
larger reasoning-oriented LLMs can become \emph{more} anchor-sensitive rather
than less. Scale changes the level of performance; it does not erase the
architecture gap.

\subsection{Pairwise Answer Similarity}
\label{sec:model_similarity}

As a free-text robustness slice, we compute pairwise SBERT cosine across the
88 open-ended questions within the human subset. The human-human ceiling is
$\overline{\text{SBERT}}_{\text{HH}} = 0.510$.
Backbone decoders reach $0.509$---essentially at the ceiling---while full
VLMs score $0.478$, standalone LLMs $0.377$, and standalone LLM (think) $0.392$.

High SBERT proximity to humans co-occurs with a large accuracy \emph{advantage}
over humans: VLMs outperform humans by $+19$\% on average, backbone decoders
by $+24$\%. This SBERT--accuracy dissociation is not evidence of visual
grounding; it indicates that VLMs produce answers in the same short, concrete
linguistic register as humans while being better calibrated to the VQA answer
frequency distribution. Standalone LLMs show both lower SBERT (0.38) and a
\emph{negative} accuracy gap ($-9$\%), confirming they fail to exploit
the same distribution. Under Eval-Normalized length normalization
(Section~\ref{sec:confounds}), the standalone LLM gap narrows by $\sim$26\% but
the ranking is preserved, confirming that prior-content differences---not
just format mismatch---drive the separation.

Within-group model-model SBERT coherence (Appendix~\ref{app:model_coherence})
follows the same pattern: models become less internally consistent as anchors
are removed, indicating that hallucinated answers diversify when entity
content is stripped from questions.

\section{Where Agreement Holds and Breaks}
\label{sec:drivers}

\subsection{Human Baseline by Question Type}
\label{sec:human_patterns}

To interpret model-human agreement, we first need the structure of human
blind agreement itself. We therefore measure pairwise human--human (HH)
SBERT per question across all three entity variants
(Original~C $\to$ Weaker~B $\to$ Pronominalized~A). All answers---human
and model alike---are passed through the same cleaning pipeline (refusal
stripping, preamble removal, VQA normalization, truncation to $\leq$5
words; \S\ref{sec:eval_protocols}) before computing SBERT similarity.

Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation} shows the distribution of
per-question HH SBERT by operation type and entity group across
variants. Operation type is the primary axis along which human
agreement varies: a one-way ANOVA on HH SBERT is significant in every
variant ($F{=}9.5$, $12.2$, $18.9$ for C/B/A; all $p{<}10^{-5}$),
and the effect \emph{amplifies} under entity abstraction (Kruskal-Wallis
$H{=}26.3 \to 37.2$, C$\to$A). Entity group, by contrast, is never
significant ($p{>}0.38$ in every variant).

Degradation under entity removal is not uniform. Paired $t$-tests
within each operation type (C vs.\ A) reveal that identity questions
collapse ($0.534 \to 0.337$, $p{=}0.002$), whereas count questions
are immune ($0.662 \to 0.671$, $p{=}0.55$). Among entity groups,
food questions degrade the most ($0.566 \to 0.470$, $p{=}0.017$),
with animal and object groups showing borderline drops ($p{\approx}0.06$);
person and other groups are stable ($p{>}0.28$).
This aligns with
qualitative inspection: questions with highest HH agreement
(e.g.,\ \emph{``How many bottles of water are on the table?''},
HH${=}$0.78) involve count or yes/no operations where linguistic
priors strongly constrain the answer space. Questions with lowest
agreement (e.g.,\ \emph{``What kind of information does the book
look like it contains?''}, HH${=}$0.18) require visual content to
disambiguate, producing scattered responses even among humans.

Human abstention is negligible: only 0.3\% of responses are explicit
abstentions (e.g.,\ ``unknown'', ``I don't know''), rising marginally
from 0.27\% (Original) to 0.40\% (Pronominalized). Unlike models---where
the cleaning pipeline strips refusals and truncates verbose
outputs---human answers pass through essentially unchanged (0.7\% of HH
answer tokens are affected, all by minor VQA normalization). The few
``unknown'' responses that remain are concentrated on questions that
genuinely require visual content (e.g.,\ brand identification,
ethnicity), confirming that they reflect true ambiguity rather than
participant disengagement. For human--model (HM) comparison, the shared
pipeline ensures that SBERT scores reflect semantic content rather than
format mismatch: model refusals and preambles are stripped before
embedding, so low HM SBERT indicates genuine disagreement rather than
verbosity artifacts.

These patterns are robust to entity manipulation: per-question HH SBERT
correlates $r{>}0.89$ across all variant pairs, and overall human agreement
decreases only modestly under pronominalization. This gives the reference
point for the model analysis below: humans do lose some agreement when
entity cues are stripped, but the loss is concentrated in a few operations
rather than being global.

\section{The Role of the Vision Encoder}
\label{sec:ablation}

The backbone decoder paradox raises a natural question: is the blank image
simply an adversarial input that misleads the vision encoder, or does the
encoder fundamentally lack a neutral mode? If the former, a different
uninformative image might recover alignment; if the latter, backbone
decoders should outperform VLMs under \emph{any} uninformative visual input.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{image_ablation_accuracy_by_condition_vlm_complete_q113_h40}%
\hfill%
\includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{image_ablation_answer_change_vs_blank_vlm_complete_q113_h40}

\textbf{Left:} VQA accuracy under four uninformative image conditions

Qwen3-VL-8B and InternVL-8B degrade sharply under noise (27.7\%, 35.1\%);

\textbf{Right:} Answer churn relative to blank. Qwen3-VL-8B and InternVL-8B

All families show non-zero sensitivity, confirming the vision encoder

We test this with four uninformative image conditions---blank (black),
uniform gray, random noise, and white---across five VLMs from all three
families: Qwen3-VL (4B, 8B), InternVL-8B, LLaVA-Mistral, and LLaVA-1.5
(Figure~\ref{fig:image_ablation}). Three findings emerge:

\textbf{The vision encoder actively processes uninformative images.}
Answer churn between conditions ranges from 0\% (Qwen3-VL-4B, which
produces identical outputs regardless) to 50\% (Qwen3-VL-8B under noise).
InternVL-8B shows a strikingly similar pattern (47\% churn under noise),
while LLaVA-1.5 is the most stable non-trivial model (12--16\% churn).
This is not failure to process---it is active processing of pixel-level
statistics that carries no semantic content.

\textbf{No uninformative image recovers backbone-level alignment.}
The backbone decoder (no image at all) achieves mean HM SBERT of 0.51,
approaching the human--human ceiling. Full VLMs score 0.48 under blank
and vary across other conditions, but none matches the backbone.
The vision encoder has no ``pass-through'' mode: even when its input
is uninformative, it produces an embedding that modulates the language
model's output distribution.

\textbf{The modulation is family-specific.}
Qwen3-VL-8B and InternVL-8B both degrade sharply under noise (47.8\%
$\to$ 27.7\% and 36.3\% $\to$ 35.1\%), while LLaVA models are
comparatively robust (LLaVA-1.5: 50.1\% $\to$ 48.4\%). This suggests
that different vision encoder architectures learn different ``default''
representations for uninformative inputs, with the sensitivity pattern
generalizing across families that share similar encoder designs.

Taken together, these results reframe the backbone decoder paradox:
vision co-training embeds useful visual-linguistic associations in the
language backbone, but the vision encoder itself has no mechanism to signal
\emph{``I have nothing useful to contribute.''} It always contributes,
and on uninformative input that contribution is noise that pushes the
language model away from the human-like linguistic prior it would otherwise
express. This explains why the backbone decoder---free from this
modulation---produces the most human-aligned blind responses.

\section{Instruction Effects and Confound Controls}
\label{sec:confounds}

The remaining question is whether the agreement pattern above is merely a
measurement artifact. We therefore separate three effects: instruction,
abstention, and answer format. Across all of them, the central ordering
(backbone $>$ VLM $>$ standalone LLM) is preserved.

\subsection{Instruction Raises Agreement but Not the Ordering}

Instruction (blind $\to$ Blind+Inst) increases HM agreement modestly, by
about $+$0.02 SBERT overall, with the largest gains on the original variant
and smaller gains as entity cues are removed (Appendix~\ref{app:inst}).
This shows that prompt framing can nudge models toward human-like blind
answers, but it does not overturn the architectural ordering or remove the
backbone advantage. Appendix~\ref{app:calibration} further shows that this
shift is accompanied by poor confidence calibration: all groups remain
severely overconfident, with VLMs and backbones assigning high token
probabilities regardless of correctness.

\subsection{Abstention Filtering}

Hard abstentions are near-zero (0.0--0.3\%). Soft abstention (``nothing,''
``none'') is 10--20\% for VLMs/backbones and 30--45\% for standalone LLMs.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{The vision encoder has no neutral mode.}
Our central finding is that removing the vision encoder from a VLM
\emph{improves} blind human-model alignment. Image-condition ablations
confirm this is not a blank-image artifact: the vision encoder actively
modulates outputs on any uninformative image, and no condition recovers
backbone-level alignment. Vision co-training embeds human-like
linguistic priors in the language backbone, but the encoder itself
introduces distributional biases that push answers away from those priors
when no meaningful visual content is present. This has practical
implications: blind VQA diagnosis should test backbone decoders
separately, and VLM architectures may benefit from a learned
``visual uncertainty'' signal that suppresses encoder influence when
input is uninformative.

\paragraph{An information-theoretic view.}
The backbone decoder computes $P(a \mid q)$ from the question alone---a
distribution shaped by the co-occurrence statistics of VQA fine-tuning
data, which closely mirrors human linguistic priors. The VLM computes
$P(a \mid q, v_0)$, where $v_0$ is the vision encoder's output for an
uninformative image. Critically, $v_0 \neq \mathbf{0}$: the encoder maps
any input---blank, gray, noise---to a non-trivial embedding that
carries its own distributional biases. This embedding acts as a
nuisance variable that shifts the output distribution away from
$P(a \mid q)$ without adding information.
Per-question entropy analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:case_study}) provides
three confirmations. First, human--model alignment correlates strongly
with shared uncertainty ($r = -0.85$ between human answer entropy and
HM SBERT): questions where humans agree are exactly the questions where
models align with humans. Second, models show systematically higher
consensus than humans---their answer entropy falls below the human
diagonal on the majority of questions, with the gap largest on
entity-grounded categories (identity, attribute) where the encoder's
``default image'' representation activates spurious visual associations.
Third, entropy mismatch is orthogonal to lexical-anchor dependence
($r = 0.12$, n.s.), ruling out answer consensus as a confound for the
control-variant findings: the mechanism by which $v_0$ distorts
$P(a \mid q)$ is separate from the mechanism by which content nouns
activate specific priors.

\paragraph{Entity anchors as prior activators.}
The control-variant degradation chain reveals that blind model success is
predominantly carried by content-noun lexical cues. When these are
stripped, models diverge from human priors while humans barely change.
This identifies a concrete mechanism---lexically anchored prior recall---that
benchmark design and debiasing interventions can explicitly target.

\paragraph{Implications for benchmark design.}
Any VQA benchmark can be partially solved by linguistic priors, and the
magnitude varies predictably with question type and lexical anchoring.
We propose that blind VQA evaluation become a standard diagnostic: blind
accuracy above 40\% signals over-indexing on knowledge-accessible
question types.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Human participants were recruited from university communities in
Korean/English contexts, limiting demographic generalizability. Model
evaluation uses greedy decoding (temperature~0), which may underestimate
answer diversity. Human data was collected under Blind+Inst only.
Image-condition ablations cover five VLMs from all three families
but use only four uninformative image types; a broader stimulus set
(e.g., task-irrelevant natural images) could further characterize encoder behavior.
A direct corpus-frequency correlation would strengthen the mechanistic
account \citep{mccoy2024embers} and is deferred to future work.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

We present a human-calibrated diagnostic study of linguistic prior
exploitation in Vision-Language Models. The central finding is
architectural: backbone decoders---the same VLM weights with the vision
encoder bypassed---achieve near-human semantic agreement (SBERT 0.509
vs.\ ceiling 0.510) while full VLMs score lower (0.478) and standalone
LLMs diverge further. Image-condition ablations show this is not a
blank-image artifact: the vision encoder has no neutral mode and actively
distorts the backbone's linguistic prior on any uninformative input.

Control question variants show that blind model success is carried by
content-noun lexical anchors: as these are stripped, model--human
agreement drops monotonically while the human ceiling barely moves. The
pattern is stable across scales and robust to confound controls
(abstention filtering, length normalization, instruction effects).
Question-type
decomposition identifies where alignment holds (yes/no, action) and
where it breaks (count, grounded reference), providing a diagnostic
vocabulary for linguistic prior exploitation in any multimodal benchmark.

\section{Human Study Protocol}
\label{app:human}

A total of 40 participants were included. Participants were recruited via
online student community forums at universities. All procedures were conducted
under an IRB-approved protocol adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki.
Informed consent was obtained prior to participation.

Participants answered text-only Blind+Inst questions across control variants
(A/B/C). The analysis applies a raw-response qualification threshold
($\geq$348) before subset intersection, then restricts to the 113 question IDs
answered by all qualifying participants (339 responses per participant).
Participants were compensated at 10,000~KRW per hour. Responses in Korean or
English were translated and processed using the aggregation strategy in
Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation}.

As a supplementary human-only diagnostic, we also examine the relationship
between per-question answer entropy and human VQA accuracy. Across variants,
the two are strongly negatively correlated ($r\approx -0.77$), indicating that
questions eliciting more diverse human blind answers are also the questions on
which humans are less likely to match VQA ground truth. We treat this as a
difficulty/ambiguity check rather than a primary result, since the main paper
focuses on semantic agreement and anchor sensitivity rather than accuracy.

\section{Answer Aggregation}
\label{app:aggregation}

We cluster semantically equivalent answers (e.g., ``3'' and ``three'') using
GPT-4o mini \citep{openai2025gpt4o}. Human responses are aggregated into a
confidence-weighted distribution:
\begin{equation}
H(a_k^*) = \frac{\sum_{i:\phi(a_i)=a_k^*} c_i}{\sum_i c_i}
\end{equation}
where $c_i$ is participant $i$'s Likert confidence (normalized to $[0,1]$)
and $\phi$ maps raw answers to canonical forms.

\section{Control Question Extraction Prompt}
\label{app:ctl}

Control variants are extracted using GPT-4.1-mini-2025-04-14 (temperature~0,
seed~42, JSON schema mode) in batches of 50 questions per API call.
The output schema enforces
\texttt{\{deictic\_removed, object\_removed, weaker\_object, pronominalized\}}
as required string fields with \texttt{additionalProperties:~false}.
The system prompt is reproduced verbatim below:

\begin{quote}\small
Generate 4 control variants for each VQA question.
Return ONLY a JSON object \texttt{\{"results":[...]\}}; preserve input order.

\textbf{Rules:}

1) \textit{deictic\_removed}: Remove deictic words (this/that/these/those/here/there)
and spatial markers (left/right/top/bottom/foreground/background) while preserving
grammar. Restructure the sentence if needed to remain fluent.

2) \textit{object\_removed}: Replace the specific object noun with neutral placeholder
``object''. Do not replace pronouns or generic nouns already present.

3) \textit{weaker\_object}: Replace the specific object noun with a broader hypernym
one level up (dog$\to$animal, car$\to$vehicle, drink$\to$beverage, shirt$\to$clothing,
apple$\to$fruit, knife$\to$utensil, sofa$\to$furniture). If no clear hypernym
exists, use ``object''.

4) \textit{pronominalized}: Replace ALL content noun phrases with minimal pronouns.
Grammar-aware rules: attribute (``What color is the dog?'') $\to$ ``What color is it?'';
count (``How many cats are there?'') $\to$ ``How many are there?'';
location (``Where is the phone?'') $\to$ ``Where is it?'';
action (``What is the man doing?'') $\to$ ``What is it doing?'';
yes/no (``Is the dog affectionate?'') $\to$ ``Is it affectionate?'';
multi-NP (``between the girl and the giraffe'') $\to$ ``between them''.
Keep ONLY question words, copula, prepositions, predicate/attribute, and pronouns.
Use ``it''/``they''/``them''---never ``the object''.
\end{quote}

\section{ChrF Agreement by Operation Type (Human--Human)}
\label{app:chrf_human}

Three systematic biases emerge (Figure~\ref{fig:dist}):
\textbf{No-bias} in yes/no: VLMs default to ``no'' at 64\%, opposite to

\textbf{Zero-bias} in count: 72\% of VLM numeric answers are ``0'';

Correlation with VQA~v2.0 training frequencies is weak or negative for VLMs
(Appendix~\ref{app:corpus_freq}), suggesting VLM blind biases are
uncertainty defaults shaped by uninformative visual input rather than
training-frequency parroting.

\section{Abstention and Calibration Ablations}
\label{app:abstention_calibration}

This section consolidates three related robustness analyses for the blind
setting: abstention behavior, instruction-induced abstention collapse, and
token-confidence calibration. Together they clarify that the backbone
advantage is not an artifact of refusal behavior, and that blind accuracy can
hide severe overconfidence.

\subsection{Instruction Gating Effect}
\label{app:inst_gating}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{merged_abstention_vC_q113}
\caption{Soft and hard abstention rates (blind $\to$ inst\_blind) per model.
Standalone LLMs show large shifts; VLMs/backbones are nearly unchanged.}
\label{fig:abstention_shift}
\end{figure}

Instruction (blind $\to$ Blind+Inst) increases HM agreement most on
original questions ($+$0.020 SBERT) and attenuates as lexical anchors are
removed (variant~A: $+$0.012). The behavioral mechanism is abstention
collapse: standalone LLMs shift from $\sim$30\% soft abstention to near
zero (Figure~\ref{fig:abstention_shift}), while VLMs and backbones barely
change---indicating their priors are expressed unconditionally. Accuracy
does not improve (Appendix~\ref{app:inst}), confirming that instruction
primarily converts refusal into committed hallucination rather than grounded
reasoning.

\subsection{Confidence Calibration}
\label{app:calibration}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{inst_blind_vC_group_reliability_accuracy_q113_h40_yesno}
\caption{Reliability diagram (inst\_blind). All groups lie far below the
diagonal. ECE: VLM 0.337, backbone 0.366, standalone LLM 0.642.}
\label{fig:reliability}
\end{figure}

All model groups are severely overconfident (Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}).
VLMs and backbones cluster at high token probabilities (0.8--0.95) regardless
of correctness. Standalone LLMs show higher ECE (0.642) and erratic curves,
reflecting incoherent prior activation. This calibration failure is invisible
to accuracy-only evaluation. Taken together with the abstention analysis
above, these results show two complementary failure modes: standalone LLMs
are more refusal-prone before instruction, while VLMs/backbones are more
likely to produce confident committed answers even under severe visual
deprivation.

Figure~\ref{fig:case_trajectories} shows per-question variant degradation
trajectories for six diagnostic questions (Table~\ref{tab:case_study}).
Lexically anchored questions exhibit steep HM drops under pronominalization,
while structure-prior questions are robust. The backbone decoder (orange)
consistently tracks the HH ceiling more closely than full VLMs (red).

Figure~\ref{fig:entropy} provides the information-theoretic complement.
Human and model answer entropies are positively correlated, confirming
that both are more uncertain on the same questions. However, models
systematically show lower entropy than humans---they are more
concentrated on a single answer even when humans distribute across
alternatives. This entropy gap is largest on entity-grounded questions
where models exploit a dominant corpus-frequency prior (e.g., ``sneaker''
for shoe type, ``0'' for count questions).
