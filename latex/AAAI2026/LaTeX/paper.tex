\begin{abstract}
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) can exploit statistical regularities in
visual question answering (VQA)---question-answer co-occurrences, lexical
cues, and world-knowledge patterns---to produce answers without seeing any
image. Humans, too, bring structured linguistic priors to visual tasks:
question wording activates scene expectations and prototypical associations
before an image is seen, a top-down cognitive prior well-documented in
psychology. Do model linguistic priors mirror these human scene expectations, or do
they reflect shortcut patterns that diverge from genuine prior-driven imagination?
Which linguistic dimensions of a question---its reasoning demand or its lexical
anchor---determine how much alignment survives?
We probe this by presenting both models and humans with VQA questions without
any image, eliciting prior-driven answers from language alone. Each question
carries two linguistic dimensions: a \emph{reasoning operation} and an
\emph{entity-typed content noun} serving as the primary lexical anchor. Three
progressively abstracted variants per question remove these anchors while
preserving grammatical structure, creating a controlled degradation axis over
339 question--variant pairs. We compare 36 models across three architectural
groups against a human reference ($N{=}40$), examining distributional and
semantic alignment with human answers.
We find that backbone decoders---VLM language components with the vision encoder
bypassed---align more closely with human blind priors than their paired full VLMs
or standalone language models, despite identical weights.
Alignment is structured by reasoning operation: Existence and Action questions carry
stable priors for both humans and models, while Identity, Comparison, and Causality
questions are most anchor-sensitive.
Models diverge most sharply on count questions, converging on a zero-default that
humans do not share.

\section{Introduction}

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) achieve impressive performance on visual question
answering (VQA) benchmarks \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}. A blindspot
of this success is that models learn to exploit statistical regularities in
the task alongside genuine visual grounding: text-only baselines achieve
non-trivial VQA accuracy without any image \citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin},
and benchmark audits confirm that language-only signals remain exploitable
even in vision-critical evaluations: \citet{chen2024are} find that a substantial
fraction of VQA benchmark questions can be answered correctly
without any image, motivating the MMStar benchmark filtered for visual
indispensability \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
Analogous to hypothesis-only baselines in natural language inference
\citep{poliak2018hypothesis}, where models predict entailment from the
hypothesis alone, VLMs can exploit shortcut priors in the question text
to produce plausible answers without grounding them in the image.
This raises a question beyond benchmark accuracy: when visual grounding is
unavailable, what priors drive model answers, and are those priors shared
with human linguistic expectations?
% Characterizing what models rely on is itself a safety and monitoring concern.
% Understanding LLM behavior across diverse settings is critical for reliable monitoring, finding that models produce characteristic default outputs shaped by training even under minimal prompting---priors that are persistent yet invisible to benchmark scores \citet{kwon2025whatllms}.
% Systematic reviews of LLM safety evaluation similarly show that identifying such persistent behavioral biases requires going beyond standard performance metrics \citep{rottger2025safetyprompts}.
\citet{lan2025mind} show that comparing full answer distributions between models and humans surfaces systematic disagreements that top-1 accuracy hides. Human blind responses serve this role in our setting, providing a window into what VLMs have learned to expect about the visual world---and establishing the baseline from which the marginal value of visual grounding can be measured: if a model's blind prior already diverges from human expectations, visual input must overcome that divergence before it can improve alignment.

In this work, to study human and model priors under referential
underspecification in visual questions, we construct progressively weaker variants of VQA questions that degrade object-denoting lexical
anchors. We collect blind responses from 40 participants under the same no-image condition as the models, establishing a human reference for linguistically grounded priors and reveal which lexical elements drive model priors and where those priors begin to diverge from human intuition. 
We use distributional alignment (JS divergence, modal-match rate) alongside semantic agreement (SBERT) to compare model and human answer distributions at the question-type level.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Vision-Language Models and Thinking.}
Modern VLMs combine vision encoders with large language model backbones trained
jointly on paired image--text data \citep{liu2023visual,zhang2023multimodal}, and
visual question answering (VQA) has become a standard benchmark for evaluating their
visual grounding and language understanding capabilities \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}.
Recent \emph{thinking models}---such as DeepSeek-R1 \citep{deepseekai2025deepseekr1}
and the Qwen3 series---extend this with reinforcement-learning-trained extended
reasoning traces. Such traces improve calibration in grounded settings
\citep{yoon2025reasoningconfidence}, but can also induce systematic overconfidence
under long reasoning budgets \citep{lacombe2025dontthink}.
% We probe whether deliberate reasoning helps construct more human-like scene priors in the blind setting.

\paragraph{Language Shortcuts and Debiasing in VQA.}
Early analyses of VQA benchmarks revealed that text-only models achieved
non-trivial accuracy by exploiting question--answer regularities baked into
benchmark construction \citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin,agrawal2018dont},
motivating debiasing and counterfactual intervention methods
\citep{niu2021counterfactual}. As VLMs scaled, the problem re-emerged in a
different form: rather than benchmark design flaws, shortcuts became embedded
through large-scale pretraining, and recent audits confirm that language-only
signals remain exploitable in modern evaluations \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
Hallucination and shortcut reliance have been widely documented in this context
\citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object,xiao2021hallucination}, with VLMs
exploiting spurious linguistic cues \citep{han2024instinctive,ye2024mmspubench}
and failing compositional visual-linguistic bindings \citep{yuksekgonul2023when}.
These audits diagnose \emph{whether} models rely on language priors---\citet{chen2024are}
filter for vision-indispensable items, \citet{lee2024vlind} test with counterfactual
images. We take a complementary approach to understand the structure of those priors or how they compare to human expectations: removing the image
entirely and comparing model answer distributions against those of humans under the same blind condition. 

\paragraph{Human Reference and Answer Distributions.}
AmbigQA \citep{min2020ambigqa} showed that many questions admit multiple plausible
answers, better represented as distributions than single gold labels---a point that
extends naturally to blind VQA, where both humans and models must rely on priors
rather than visual evidence.
From the human side, dual-process accounts of judgment under uncertainty
\citep{tversky1974judgment,kahneman2011thinking} and the proactive brain framework
\citep{bar2007proactive} suggest that linguistic cues activate structured scene
predictions even without full sensory input, grounding our interpretation of human
blind responses as meaningful prior-driven behavior rather than noise.
A growing body of work evaluates AI systems against this kind of human reference
directly: \citet{lan2025mind} compare VLM uncertainty against human uncertainty in
VQA, \citet{lee2023visalign} study alignment between human and model visual
judgments, and \citet{bao2026imagination} show that AI outputs systematically diverge
from the divergent and negating hypotheses humans generate freely.
Behavioral auditing work further shows that standard benchmarks miss persistent
biases that only surface under diverse input conditions
\citep{rottger2025safetyprompts,kwon2025whatllms}.
Our work extends this human-reference paradigm to visual questions under controlled
referential degradation, using a taxonomy that decomposes prior structure by
reasoning operation and entity type.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset and Control Variants}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Question pool and abstention filtering.}
We begin with 1{,}000 questions randomly sampled from the VQA v2.0 validation split \citep{goyal2016making}, spanning yes/no, numerical, and open-ended types. Abstention is highly model-dependent without the explicit no-image instruction: some standalone configurations refuse a large fraction of questions, whereas most VLMs and backbone decoders remain near zero (Table~\ref{tab:abstention_by_qtype}; Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_by_qtype}). We select questions on which all model groups give substantive answers (abstention $<$12\%; keyword criteria in Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_classifier}), spread across the broader question pool (Appendix~\ref{app:tsne}).

\paragraph{Entity coverage and diversity.}
Our dataset subset is designed to preserve variety while keeping the human--model comparison meaningful under blind prompting. The original VQA metadata is organized largely around surface question forms such as \textit{what}, \textit{how many}, or \textit{is there}, but those labels do not cleanly separate the reasoning demand from the semantic field of the anchor-bearing noun phrase. For analysis, we therefore annotate each question along two orthogonal dimensions: \emph{operation} (e.g., count, attribute, action, spatial, identity) and \emph{entity} (e.g., person, animal, object, vehicle, food, place). This two-axis taxonomy separates \emph{what kind of reasoning} the question requires from \emph{what semantic field} its lexical anchors evoke, so agreement patterns can be analyzed independently by operation and by entity type rather than conflated in a single label space.

% Within that taxonomy, we aim for relatively even coverage across \emph{entity} groupings. This matters for the control-variant analysis below: once lexical anchors are weakened or replaced by pronouns, some question types remain interpretable under broad commonsense priors while others become heavily anchor-dependent. Broad entity coverage therefore helps test that degradation pattern across a wider range of latent scene priors rather than concentrating it in a small number of repeated object domains. The resulting 113-question subset is also broadly spread across the larger question space (Appendix~\ref{app:tsne}, Figure~\ref{fig:tsne}).
The resulting 113-question subset spans three controlled reformulations (\S\ref{sec:control}), yielding 339 question--variant pairs (Appendix~\ref{app:taxonomy}; breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:q113_sample_composition}); annotation used a structured GPT-4.1-mini prompt (Appendix~\ref{app:sem_extraction}).


\paragraph{Control question variants.}
\label{sec:control}
To test how strongly blind answers depend on entity-denoting lexical anchors,
we generate three progressively abstracted variants of each question using GPT-4.1-mini:
\textbf{Original} (C) — the unmodified question;
\textbf{Weaker} (B) — object nouns generalized to a higher-level category (e.g., \emph{dog} $\rightarrow$ \emph{animal});
\textbf{Pronominalized} (A) — content noun phrases replaced with minimal pronouns while retaining grammatical structure.
The full four-step generation schema and transformation rules are in Appendix~\ref{app:ctl}; human and model comparisons use variants C, B, and A.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

\paragraph{Models and Inference.}

We evaluate three model groups. \textbf{VLMs} (Qwen3-VL \citep{bai2025qwen3vl}, LLaVA \citep{liu2023visual,liu2024llavanext}, InternVL \citep{zhu2025internvl3}) receive a blank 224$\times$224 image together with the instruction \textit{``Note: No images are provided. For each question, imagine an appropriate image exists and answer based on the most common or universal scenario,''} eliciting prior-driven answers while reducing refusal behavior. \textbf{Backbone decoders} are weight-identical to their paired VLMs but are run text-only (no image), isolating the language prior. \textbf{Standalone LLMs} include Qwen3 \citep{yang2025qwen3}, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Mistral-7B, Vicuna-7B/13B, and Phi-3.5-mini, all text-only; some share a family with VLM backbone decoders (e.g., Qwen3 with Qwen3-VL, Mistral-7B with LLaVA-Mistral), enabling direct comparisons.
All evaluations are zero-shot at temperature~0.
The instruction reduces soft abstention and drives models toward more committed answers (Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_calibration}).

\paragraph{Human Data Collection.}\label{sec:human_data}

40 participants (online university forums, IRB-approved) answered text-only
blind-instruction VQA questions in Korean or English, yielding 15,336 total responses
(339 question--variant pairs per participant).
Participants received the same blind-awareness instruction as the models.
Full protocol details and distributions are in Appendix~\ref{app:human}
and~\ref{app:human_stats}.

\subsection{Metrics and Evaluation Protocols}
\label{sec:eval_protocols}

\textbf{Preprocessing.} Before scoring, all answers are translated to English
when needed, normalized with the standard VQA pipeline
\citep{antol2015vqa}, and stripped of generic wrappers so human and model
responses are compared on the same cleaned answer string.
Abstentions are then excluded and non-standard committed answers (e.g., \textit{``maybe''}) are mapped to the \textit{other} bin; this filtering is applied uniformly before all alignment metrics for reliable comparisons.
Appendix~\ref{app:human} and
Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_classifier} provide the full preprocessing and
cleaning details.

\textbf{Distributional alignment.} For categorical questions such as \textit{yes/no}
and \textit{count}, we compare each model response (a single answer per question--variant
at temperature~0) against the per-question human answer distribution using
Jensen--Shannon (JS) divergence and a modal-match rate
(\textit{Mode}: whether the model's answer matches the human modal answer for that question).
All comparisons are per-question: the model's answer is treated as a point mass and
evaluated against the empirical distribution of 40 human responses for the \emph{same} question--variant; scores are then averaged across questions.
% Answer bins for yes/no are \{yes, no, other\}; for count, they are the observed numeric values.
The human distribution is always restricted to the questions each model committed to; per-model abstention rates by question type are in Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_by_qtype}.

\textbf{Semantic alignment.} For answer-level semantic agreement, we compute pairwise
Sentence-BERT \citep{reimers2019sentence} cosine similarity between responses.
Specifically, we use the \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} encoder
\citep{song2020mpnet}. For each question and variant, we compare each model
answer against the set of human answers and average over humans to obtain a
per-question human--model score.
% Human--human (HH) agreement is computed analogously from distinct human pairs.
% For model $m$, question $q$, and variant $v$,
% $\mathrm{HM}_{m,q,v}$ is the mean SBERT cosine between the model answer and
% all individual human answers, and $\mathrm{HH}_{q,v}$ is the mean SBERT cosine
% across distinct human--human pairs for the same question and variant. For
% grouped views such as the HH--HM scatterplots, we average these per-question
% scores over the questions and variants in slice $g$ to obtain
% $\overline{\mathrm{HM}}_{m,g}$ and $\overline{\mathrm{HH}}_g$.
Embedding-based agreement can be sensitive to answer length; some standalone LLMs produce longer explanatory responses even after preamble stripping.
We therefore report lexical-overlap robustness checks (chrF, ROUGE-1) and a length-normalized evaluation truncating answers to $\leq$5 words.
The main group ordering is preserved under all three checks
(Appendix~\ref{app:chrf_human}; Figure~\ref{fig:sbert_truncation_scatter}).

\textbf{Task Performance.} Although no image is provided at inference time,
we report blind VQA task accuracy as a complementary behavioral signal of how
models respond to question-only inputs. We include it as a secondary metric
because it also indicates how strongly models leverage question-side priors,
biases, and shortcuts rather than true visual grounding under blind answering
\citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.

\subsection{Human Priors Patterns}
\label{sec:human_patterns}

\paragraph{Signal convergence and reference reliability.}
We assess the reliability of HH SBERT as a difficulty measure by examining its correlation with two independent behavioral signals---response time (RT) and subjective confidence---collected alongside answers.
If HH SBERT captures genuine question difficulty, harder questions (lower agreement) should take longer and elicit lower confidence.
This is confirmed (Figure~\ref{fig:conf_rt_sbert_triangle}): RT correlates strongly negatively with HH SBERT across all variants ($r{=}{-}0.854$ to $-0.860$; all $p{<}.001$), and confidence tracks positively in two of three variants ($r{=}0.228$--$0.299$; $p{<}.016$)---converging on the same latent difficulty dimension from three independent measures.

Human blind agreement varies selectively across question types
(Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation}).
Within operation types, identity questions are the most anchor-sensitive: HH SBERT
drops from $0.67$ to $0.34$ under pronominalization, as the referent disappears
and answers scatter.
For example, \textit{``What type of bear is this?''} sees humans converge on specific
species when the anchor is present, but shift to uncoordinated responses without it.
Count questions are the most anchor-robust ($\Delta{=}{+}0.008$), as numerical
priors persist even after the object is removed.
Within entity types, food and animal questions show the steepest degradation:
\textit{``What type of bread does this appear to be?''} (Weaker: \textit{``food''};
Pronominalized: \textit{``it''}) sees humans converge on specific bread types
(\textit{baguette}, \textit{sandwich bread}) under the original phrasing, but shift
to generic categories once the anchor is removed (HH SBERT $0.53 \to 0.32$).
These within-group patterns are the interpretively meaningful signal.

\subsection{Categorical Answer Distribution Alignment}
\label{sec:dist_alignment}

SBERT measures whether a model's answer occupies the same semantic region as human answers, but not whether it is biased toward a particular response---and scores for categories with narrow answer spaces (yes/no, count) remain artificially high regardless of anchor removal.
A model that always answers \textit{no} and one that always answers \textit{yes} may score similarly under SBERT, yet diverge in opposite directions from the human distribution.
Table~\ref{tab:hm_grouped_distribution_7b_compact_filtered} quantifies this directly for binary and count questions.

\paragraph{Presence prior in humans, absence default in models.}
Under blind conditions, models and humans diverge in a consistent direction.
Humans lean toward presence: they answer \textit{yes} on 65\% of binary questions
and distribute count answers around small positive quantities (``2'' most frequent).
Full VLMs lean toward absence: they answer \textit{yes} on only 20--37\% of
binary questions (LLaVA family) and 0--41\% (Qwen3-VL), and collapse toward ``0''
on 81.4\% of count questions---treating a missing image as an empty scene rather
than an unspecified one.
Together these reflect a shared \emph{absence prior} that is the mirror image of
the human positive-world prior.
Backbone decoders partially recover the human pattern
(LLaVA-1.5~LM: 71\% yes, Qwen3-VL-8B~LM: 73\%).
InternVL-8B is the lone full VLM that exceeds rather than undershoots the human yes-rate---it overshoots to $\sim$80\%, placing it on the same side of the human baseline as backbone decoders (Figure~\ref{fig:dist_human_vs_model}), a tendency that carries through to its stronger semantic alignment in \S\ref{sec:alignment_main}.
On count questions the picture is more nuanced: LLaVA backbone decoders commit to numeric answers on every count question (predominantly ``0''), while InternVL-8B~(LM) refuses $\sim$62\% of count questions outright with verbose explanations---its count alignment metrics are therefore computed only over the 38\% where it gave a substantive answer.
When it does answer, it distributes across small counts (``1'', ``2--3'') rather than defaulting to ``0'', which is individually more human-like but makes it a less complete reference than the LLaVA decoders for this answer type.
Standalone LLMs show a separate failure: they often exit the answer space entirely,
replacing content answers with hedges or refusals
(Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_calibration}).

For \emph{``How many birds?''}, humans blind-answer with a spread of small

about a recall-from-memory question; VLMs that are stuck at ``0'' blind jump to
arbitrarily large values (50, 100) when instructed, overshooting the human
distribution rather than anchoring near it.

% unable to disengage from a null-image interpretation. [redundant with birds example]
The architecture ordering in how much instruction shifts answers is itself
revealing: standalone LLMs change most readily (68\%) because their language
backbone retains count priors from text-only pretraining; backbone decoders
follow at 38\%; full VLMs resist most strongly (14\%), as the vision encoder's
null-image signal suppresses the language prior even when instruction explicitly
asks the model to imagine a scene
(Table~\ref{tab:image_ablation_main}, \S\ref{sec:ablation};
full all-model count distributions in Appendix~\ref{app:count_dist},
Figure~\ref{fig:count_transition_all}).
This points to a specific failure mode of vision-language pretraining: the
language backbone defers count retrieval to the visual pathway, which atrophies
its independent linguistic count priors \citep{paiss2023teaching,parcalabescu2022valse}.
When the visual pathway receives no meaningful signal, that deference becomes a
liability---producing not an ``I cannot see'' abstention, but a committed
zero-default that instruction alone cannot dislodge.
The $\Delta$ values in Table~\ref{tab:hm_grouped_distribution_7b_compact_filtered} quantify this: think models recover most (yes/no $\Delta{=}{-}0.071$, count $\Delta{=}{-}0.146$), while full VLMs barely shift their yes/no distribution, confirming that the instruction triggers surface-level answer perturbation rather than coherent scene imagination.
Qwen3 and Qwen3 (think) share identical blind-condition distributions (JS$\,{=}\,.468$, Mode$\,{=}\,.568$), showing that the thinking mechanism affects instruction response but not the underlying blind prior.
At the semantic level, instruction improves HM SBERT for all models without exception ($\Delta{=}{+}0.089$--$+0.101$ per group; all $p{<}.001$; Table~\ref{tab:inst_effect_combined}), yet this gain does not translate into better distributional alignment for most VLMs on yes/no or count questions, pointing to a surface-level lexical shift rather than genuine prior recalibration.
This incomplete recovery is not driven by abstention changes: instruction reduces hard abstention for initially-refusing models (e.g., Mistral standalone), but VLMs and backbone decoders abstain at near-zero rates both with and without instruction (Table~\ref{tab:inst_effect_combined} in Appendix~\ref{app:inst}).
This distinction is especially visible on count questions.
Under instruction, thinking models often move away from blind defaults such as ``0'' and into plausible small counts, which makes the \emph{aggregate} count distribution look more human-like.
But at the \emph{question-specific} level this shift often remains misaligned: a model may move from ``0'' to ``1'' or ``3'' while humans concentrate on a different nearby number.
More human-like \emph{marginal} count distributions therefore do not necessarily translate into higher semantic alignment, a gap we quantify in the next section.

\paragraph{Commonsense operations align; identity and spatial questions expose the lexical gap.}
Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_sbert} reveals two distinct alignment regimes by operation type.

\textit{Anchor-robust.}
Existence, Action, and World Knowledge questions sit in the top-right of the
scatter, near or above the diagonal across all model classes.
Both humans and models converge on strong priors regardless of whether the anchor
noun is present---\textit{``What is the animal doing?''} and \textit{``What is
it doing?''} draw on the same locomotion prior, so the lexical cue adds little.

\textit{Anchor-sensitive.}
Comparison, Identity, and Spatial questions cluster in the bottom-left and fall
\emph{below} the diagonal: human agreement is already low because these questions
are inherently referent-specific, and models fall further below humans once the
anchor is removed.
Count questions are a different case---they sit \emph{above} the diagonal for
VLMs, but this reflects the zero-default shortcut rather than genuine alignment.

\paragraph{Backbone decoders are the most human-aligned.}
Removing the vision encoder \emph{improves} human--model alignment.
In four of five matched families, backbone decoders---weight-identical to their paired VLMs---raise HM SBERT by $+0.019$ to $+0.062$; collectively they average $0.595$ vs.\ $0.568$ for full VLMs and $0.459$ for standalone LLMs.
Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_sbert} makes this concrete: the backbone decoder row
sits \emph{above} the full VLM row across every operation and entity group.
Matched-family paired Wilcoxon tests confirm this in four of five decoder--VLM pairs
(Holm-corrected, $\Delta{=}{+}0.017$ to ${+}0.069$); InternVL is the lone
exception where the full VLM slightly leads the backbone decoder ($\Delta{=}{-}0.021$, $p{<}.001$; see visual encoding ablation below).
Against matched standalone LLMs, every decoder comparison is positive and
significant ($\Delta{=}{+}0.087$ to ${+}0.337$;
Appendix~\ref{app:family_alignment_tests}).
Within model classes, InternVL is the best-aligned full VLM across nearly all groups (consistent with its distributional advantage in Section~\ref{sec:dist_alignment}); Mistral-7B is the lowest standalone LLM.
Backbone decoders show the most uniform within-class scores (within-question SD: $0.048$ vs.\ $0.062$ for VLMs)---architecture dominates individual model variation, and alignment is determined first by architecture, then by the reasoning demand of the question.
All architecture comparisons use matched-family paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on per-question HM SBERT scores; all reported $p$-values are Holm-corrected.

\section{What Modulates Alignment}
\label{sec:ablation}

\paragraph{Visual encoding.}
The backbone decoder advantage is not a black-image artifact.
Testing blank, gray, noise, and white inputs on Qwen3-VL-8B, LLaVA-Mistral, and InternVL-8B
reveals a clear architectural split.
For Qwen3-VL-8B and LLaVA-Mistral, no image condition recovers backbone-level alignment:
Qwen3-VL-8B SBERT ranges from $0.490$ (noise) to $0.628$ (white), never reaching the backbone's $0.651$;
LLaVA-Mistral is more stable ($0.590$--$0.626$, backbone $0.662$).
InternVL-8B reverses this pattern: its VLM alignment under any image condition ($0.639$--$0.660$)
exceeds its own backbone ($0.575$), with a SBERT range of only $0.021$ across all image types---the narrowest of the three models.
This suggests that InternVL's encoder enriches, rather than suppresses, the language prior when
processing uninformative visual input, making the image type essentially irrelevant to alignment.
Full ablation results are in Appendix~\ref{app:ablation} (Table~\ref{tab:image_ablation_main}).

\paragraph{Scale.}
Scaling up increases blind VQA accuracy but does not reliably improve human-model
alignment---and the two metrics decouple differently across families
(Table~\ref{tab:comprehensive_inst_blind}, Appendix~\ref{app:scale_variant}).
For Qwen3-VL (2B--8B), VLM accuracy rises monotonically ($0.443{\to}0.478$)
while backbone decoder SBERT peaks at 4B ($0.514$) and falls at 8B ($0.492$):
larger backbones absorb more VQA-style training signal---improving ground-truth
accuracy---without producing a more human-like prior distribution.
For LLaVA-Vicuna (7B--13B), the same dissociation holds: backbone accuracy rises
sharply ($0.404{\to}0.531$) while SBERT falls for both backbone and VLM
($0.504{\to}0.488$; $0.450{\to}0.436$).
InternVL presents a contrasting pattern: the full VLM exceeds its own backbone
in alignment at every size (SBERT $0.658$ vs.\ $0.575$ at 8B), making it the only
family where the vision encoder does not degrade alignment relative to the
text-only backbone---consistent with the visual encoding ablation above showing
InternVL's encoder enriches rather than suppresses the language prior.
What varies across families is therefore not parameter count but how visual
training shapes the encoder--backbone interaction
(Table~\ref{tab:matched_family_alignment_tests}).

\paragraph{Extended reasoning.}
Thinking does not reliably improve human--model alignment.
Paired Wilcoxon tests across matched Qwen3 sizes show that the benefit is
confined to 1.7B ($\Delta{=}{+}0.110$, $p{<}.001$), while 0.6B shows no
significant gain ($\Delta{=}{+}0.000$, $p{=}.317$); the pooled effect
($\Delta{=}{+}0.048$, $p{<}.001$; Table~\ref{tab:thinking_wilcoxon},
Appendix~\ref{app:thinking}).
Thinking therefore behaves more like a size-dependent decoding intervention
than a general route to more human-like blind priors
(Figure~\ref{fig:think_abs_vs_length}).
Longer traces also correlate with higher final-answer token confidence
($r{=}0.26$, $p{<}.001$; Figure~\ref{fig:think_abs_vs_length}c),
consistent with overconfidence under extended reasoning budgets \citep{lacombe2025dontthink}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Backbone decoders carry the most human-calibrated prior.}
% Vision co-training exposes the language backbone to dense, scene-descriptive language---image captions that encode how often things are present, what typical counts look like, and how objects co-occur---so the backbone acquires a prior over visual world states that standalone LLMs, trained on more varied and abstract text, do not develop to the same degree.
% This co-training embeds rich, human-like linguistic priors in the language backbone, but the vision encoder introduces distributional biases that push responses away from those priors when no meaningful visual content is present.
The backbone decoder---approximating $P(a \mid q)$ rather than $P(a \mid q, v_0)$---best reproduces human blind priors not because it is more capable, but because the vision encoder actively suppresses the language prior when processing an uninformative image, treating the blank input as a meaningful null signal rather than ignoring it.
This reframes the problem: the limitation is not that VLMs are ``too visual,'' but that they lack a reliable way to distinguish informative from uninformative visual states.
The same suppression likely operates on genuinely difficult real images---low contrast, heavy occlusion, unusual viewpoints---where the visual signal is present but weak, suggesting that blind-prior alignment is a diagnostic for how well a model will behave under visual uncertainty, not only under complete absence.
A natural design response is learned input-uncertainty gating---the model should modulate how much it defers to the visual pathway based on estimated input quality, falling back on its language prior when the visual signal is uninformative.

\paragraph{Lexical anchors as prior activators, structured by operation type.}
The control-variant degradation chain reveals that blind model success is
predominantly carried by content-noun lexical cues. When these are stripped,
models diverge from human priors while humans barely change---but this effect
is not uniform.
Commonsense-grounded operations (Existence, Action, World Knowledge) are
anchor-robust: both humans and models draw on stable scene priors that survive
pronominalization.
Referent-specific operations (Identity, Spatial, Comparison) are
anchor-sensitive: once the content noun is removed, both human agreement and
model alignment collapse, but models fall further---revealing that model
answers in these categories are driven more by lexical surface cues than by
robust world knowledge.
This operation-level structure identifies a concrete diagnostic axis---\emph{which
question types expose shortcut reliance}---that benchmark design and debiasing
interventions can explicitly target: pronominalization of identity and spatial
questions acts as a stress test that separates genuine prior-driven behavior
from lexical pattern-matching.

\paragraph{Vision co-training enriches language priors.}
The backbone decoder advantage over standalone LLMs is arguably more revealing than
its advantage over full VLMs.
Backbone decoders and their matched standalone counterparts share the same underlying
architecture and training objective---the only difference is that backbone decoders
were trained jointly on image--caption pairs.
Yet backbone decoders substantially outperform matched standalone LLMs
($\Delta{=}{+}0.087$ to ${+}0.337$ HM SBERT), despite never processing any image at test time.
This gap is the signature of vision co-training enriching the language prior: exposure
to dense, scene-descriptive caption language embeds structured world knowledge---what
things look like, how often they appear, how they co-occur---that text-only pretraining
does not develop to the same degree.
The vision encoder temporarily suppresses these priors on uninformative input,
but the priors themselves are a product of multimodal training, not a failure of it.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Participants were recruited from university communities (Korean/English), limiting demographic scope.
Model evaluation uses greedy decoding, which may underestimate answer diversity.
Human data covers only the instruction-blind condition; corpus-frequency correlation
\citep{mccoy2024embers} and broader image ablations are left to future work.
Benchmark contamination may also amplify blind performance: \citet{chen2024are} show that VLMs can achieve non-trivial scores without visual input partly due to training-set leakage; our pronominalization test partially controls for this, since models that rely on memorized question--answer pairs should degrade more sharply when content nouns are removed.

The VLM-generated control variants are imperfect in two respects.
First, some questions contain no clear nominal subject, so the abstraction ladder collapses across all three variants, removing the intended difficulty gradient.
% e.g. ``What sport is being played?'' remains identical across all three variants.
Second, the Weaker and Pronominalized steps do not always follow a strict specificity order: occasionally the Pronominalized variant drops its referent entirely while the Weaker version stays grounded, producing non-monotonic agreement patterns (B $>$ A).
% e.g. ``How many zebras?'' $\to$ Weaker: ``How many animals?'' $\to$ Pronominalized: ``How many are there?'' — the Weaker version remains grounded but the Pronominalized version loses its referent entirely.
% Similarly, ``What is the blue thing on the ground?'' weakens to ``What is the blue object on the ground?'', which is no more abstract.
These cases motivate more controlled abstraction methods beyond single-step VLM rewriting.

\section{Conclusion}

This work studies how VLMs respond to visual questions in the absence of
any image, and how closely that behavior resembles the blind priors that
humans themselves express under the same condition. We find that backbone decoders---the language components of VLMs with the vision encoder bypassed---come
closest to human blind priors, nearly reaching the human--human agreement ceiling
and consistently outperforming both full VLMs and standalone LLMs despite identical weights.
% This confirms the intuition that VLM language decoders carry rich world knowledge
% from multimodal training, while also showing that the vision encoder introduces
% a distributional pull on uninformative images that pushes responses away from
% those very priors.
% High blind accuracy does not accompany this: the models with the strongest shortcut
% behavior score well on VQA metrics while diverging from human response distributions,
% underscoring that accuracy alone is an unreliable proxy for whether a model is
% doing what humans do.

Alignment varies by the reasoning demand of the question rather than its semantic domain.
Existence and Action questions carry stable priors that transfer across anchor variants---they
ask whether something is present or occurring, which humans and models like LLaVA-Mistral
and InternVL can answer from general scene expectations alone.
Identity and Spatial questions, however, require knowing \emph{what} or \emph{where}
a specific referent is: without the content noun, the question loses its grounding
entirely, and model alignment collapses more sharply than human agreement does.
This reveals that the backbone decoder's advantage is itself referent-dependent---
co-training instills strong priors for commonsense operations but cannot substitute
for the visual specificity that Identity and Spatial questions demand.
