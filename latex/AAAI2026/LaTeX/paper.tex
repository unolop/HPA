\begin{abstract}
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have achieved strong performance on visual
question answering by learning from large paired datasets of images and text.
Alongside these capabilities, VLMs are known to sometimes generate responses
that are fluent and plausible but not fully grounded in the visual input---a
tendency broadly discussed as hallucination. Part of this reflects statistical
regularities in training text: question-answer co-occurrences, lexical cues,
and world-knowledge patterns that can support answering even without visual
grounding, to the point where VLMs can produce answers without seeing any
image at all. Whether humans show analogous structure under the same
conditions---and whether model priors reflect something beyond dataset
leakage---is less clear and is what we investigate here.
We probe this by presenting blank images to both models and humans,
\emph{provoking} each to construct a plausible answer from language alone.
The contrast is stark: humans imagine a world where things exist---answering
\emph{yes} 65\% of the time on binary questions and distributing count answers
around small positive quantities (2--3)---while most VLMs default to absence,
collapsing to \emph{no} and \emph{zero}---as if applying an \emph{empty-world prior}
rather than imagining a plausible scene.
We compare blind responses from 37 models across three
architectural groups against a human blind-answer reference ($N{=}40$,
339 question--variant pairs), measuring semantic agreement rather than
accuracy. Questions are progressively reformulated by removing content-noun
anchors to isolate where humans and models anchor when lexical cues are
stripped away. A counterintuitive finding emerges: the model group most
aligned with human blind priors is not the full VLM but the
\emph{backbone decoder}---the same weights with the vision encoder bypassed.
Image-condition ablations show that the vision encoder actively modulates the
language model's output on uninformative input rather than cleanly withdrawing.
Alignment varies systematically by reasoning type: identity questions are most
sensitive to anchor removal while count questions show the largest persistent
human--model gap. Extended reasoning traces from thinking models partially
modulate these priors without closing the gap.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) achieve impressive performance on visual question
answering (VQA) benchmarks \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}. Yet prior
work has shown that text-only models can achieve non-trivial accuracy on VQA
without any visual input \citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin}, and recent
benchmark audits confirm that language-only signals remain exploitable in
modern multimodal evaluations \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
Analogous to hypothesis-only baselines in natural language inference
\citep{poliak2018hypothesis}, where models can predict entailment from the
hypothesis alone without seeing the premise, VLMs can exploit shortcut priors
carried by the question text to produce plausible answers without grounding
them in the image. This raises a broader question beyond benchmark accuracy:
when visual grounding is unavailable, what priors drive model answers, and to
what extent are those priors shared with human linguistic expectations? In this paper, we investigate this question by comparing blind model responses
against a human reference, finding that the degree of alignment depends
more on model architecture than on scale or question content, and that
human blind priors are more structured and operation-specific than
typically assumed.

Prior work on VQA has largely approached this issue through the lens of
dataset bias and shortcut learning. Early work showed that text-only models
can answer many VQA questions correctly without visual input
\citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin}, motivating the complementary-image
construction of VQA~v2.0 \citep{goyal2016making}. More recent evaluation
audits show that substantial language-only signal remains available even in
modern multimodal benchmarks \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind};
\citet{chen2024are} formalize this gap with a Multimodal Gain (MG) metric
and introduce MMStar, a benchmark filtered for visual indispensability,
to separate genuine visual reasoning from linguistic shortcut exploitation
and training-data contamination.
In our own blind evaluation on VQA v2.0 questions, we find that modern VLMs
answer 37--54\% correctly without seeing any image.
However, most prior work evaluates this phenomenon
primarily in terms of benchmark accuracy. Much less is known about the
\emph{structure} of the underlying answer priors themselves, including which
lexical anchors activate scene priors in place of visual grounding.

At the same time, research in cognitive psychology has long established that
humans facing decisions under uncertainty rely on systematic heuristics---such
as availability and representativeness \citep{tversky1974judgment}---rather
than responding randomly. When visual evidence is missing, these heuristics
produce structured prior-driven responses: predictable default answers that
reflect world knowledge, prototypical associations, and frequency-based
expectations \citep{kahneman2011thinking}. This makes human blind answers a
meaningful reference point, not mere noise. Recent work on human--AI alignment
argues that model behavior should be evaluated against human response
distributions rather than only against task labels
\citep{lan2025mind,bao2026imagination}. \citet{kwon2025whatllms} show that
LLMs exhibit distinct intrinsic generative biases even under minimal
prompts, with different model families favoring systematically different
topical and structural defaults. Under ambiguous or underspecified
inputs, humans show structured patterns of agreement and disagreement that
reveal latent expectations about plausible interpretations, whereas
contemporary models often collapse toward narrow modal regions of response
space \citep{bao2026imagination}.
The contrast is particularly sharp on categorical questions: when asked to
imagine a scene without visual evidence, humans treat objects as likely
\emph{present} and counts as small but non-zero, reflecting a positive-world
prior shaped by everyday experience. VLMs, by contrast, frequently default
to absence---\emph{no} on binary questions, \emph{zero} on count
questions---responding as though the blank image signals an empty world rather
than an unspecified one. This asymmetry runs deeper than benchmark accuracy:
it reveals that models and humans construct fundamentally different default
worlds when forced to think without sight. In our setting, this motivates treating
blind VQA answers not just as right or wrong guesses, but as distributions
over plausible interpretations: some questions induce strong shared human
priors, while others open up pluralistic answer regions once lexical anchors
are weakened. The question is not whether such priors exist, but which priors
remain stable, which collapse under underspecification, and where model priors
diverge from human ones.

In this work, we study human and model priors under referential
underspecification in visual questions. Starting from VQA questions, we
construct progressively weaker variants that degrade object-denoting lexical
anchors while preserving grammatical structure, creating a controlled
degradation axis from fully specified questions to increasingly
underspecified formulations. This lets us probe where humans and models rely
on those anchors, how much blind answering survives when the anchors are
weakened, and where linguistic priors begin to diverge from grounded visual
reasoning. We first characterize how human answer distributions change along
this axis, identifying question categories that maintain strong consensus and
categories whose answers rapidly diversify under ambiguity. We then compare
these patterns against a diverse set of vision-language models, backbone
decoders, and language-only models to investigate where model priors align
with human priors and where they diverge.

Our contribution lies in the combination of four methodological tools.
First, we recruit 40 participants to answer VQA
questions under the same blind condition as the models, providing a human
reference for what linguistically plausible prior-driven answers look like.
Second, we introduce an \emph{operation--entity taxonomy} that categorizes
each question along two semantic dimensions---the reasoning operation
required (count, identity, action, attribute, spatial, text) and the entity
type mentioned (person, animal, object, food, place)---enabling diagnostics
at a finer grain than the VQA dataset's original question-type annotations.
Third, we construct progressively weaker question reformulations that strip
content-noun anchors from the original text, creating a controlled
degradation axis that isolates which lexical elements carry prior information.
Fourth, in addition to answer-level semantic agreement, we use a simple
distributional alignment analysis that asks whether models place answer mass
over question types and answer types in ways that resemble the corresponding
human response patterns.

Together, these tools reveal several findings across 37 models from four
architectural groups. Human answer priors without visual evidence turn out
to be selectively structured: consensus depends on what kind of reasoning
the question requires, not what objects it mentions
(Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation}). On the model side, the group whose
answers most closely resemble those of humans is not the full VLM but the
backbone decoder---the same weights with the vision encoder bypassed
(Figure~\ref{fig:scale}). This gap persists across model scales, suggesting
that architecture, rather than capacity, determines how human-like the
resulting priors are. As content-noun anchors are progressively removed,
model--human agreement drops monotonically while the human--human ceiling
barely moves (Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation}), confirming that models depend
heavily on lexical anchors for prior activation, whereas human imaginative defaults are more robust to their removal.
Image-condition ablations (blank, gray, noise, white) further show that the
vision encoder actively modulates the language model's output even on
uninformative input (Table~\ref{tab:image_ablation_main}); the backbone
decoder, free from this modulation, falls back on the pure linguistic
prior---a form of language-embedded scene imagination---which turns out to be the most human-like. Finally, our taxonomy
reveals where human--model alignment holds and where it breaks: counting
questions show the largest persistent gap: humans imagine small populated
scenes (2--3 objects most frequent) while models collapse to zero,
treating absence of image as absence of content. We additionally probe
whether extended reasoning traces from thinking models help bridge this
gap---they partially modulate answer priors but do not restore
human-like scene imagination.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Question-Only Reasoning, Shortcut Learning, and Ambiguity in Visual Questions.}
Early analyses of VQA benchmarks revealed that text-only models could achieve
non-trivial accuracy without visual input, exposing exploitable
question--answer regularities in the data \citep{jabri2016revisiting}.
Subsequent studies identified systematic answer biases
\citep{zhang2016yin} and showed that many VQA systems rely on language
shortcuts rather than visual grounding \citep{agrawal2018dont}, motivating
debiasing and counterfactual intervention methods
\citep{niu2021counterfactual}. As VLMs have scaled in both architecture and
training data, these concerns have resurfaced: recent benchmark audits show
that language-only signals remain prevalent even in modern multimodal
evaluations \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}. In parallel, recent work has examined how
humans and AI systems behave under ambiguity, underspecification, and
incomplete grounding. AmbigQA \citep{min2020ambigqa} demonstrated that many
questions admit multiple plausible answers and are better represented as
answer distributions rather than single gold labels. Hallucination and
shortcut learning have likewise been widely documented in vision-language
models \citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object,xiao2021hallucination}. Prior
work shows that VLMs can exploit spurious visual or linguistic cues
\citep{han2024instinctive,ye2024mmspubench}, fail to maintain compositional
visual-linguistic bindings \citep{yuksekgonul2023when}, and answer benchmark
questions without requiring image evidence \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
These benchmark audits diagnose \emph{whether} models rely on language
priors---\citet{chen2024are} filter for vision-indispensable items, while
\citet{lee2024vlind} test with counterfactual images---but do not ask what
the structure of those priors is or how it compares to human behavior.
We take a complementary approach: rather than filtering out prior-solvable
questions or manipulating images, we remove the image entirely and compare
the resulting model answer distributions against those of humans under the
same blind condition, using a semantic taxonomy that decomposes prior
structure by reasoning operation and entity type.

\paragraph{Human--Model Alignment and Answer Priors.}
A growing body of work evaluates AI systems relative to human judgments rather
than benchmark labels alone. Prior work argues for evaluating alignment
through similarities between human and model response distributions,
particularly in settings involving ambiguity and underspecification.
\citet{bao2026imagination} study human--AI agreement at the level of response
distributions rather than only top-1 labels. In the multimodal domain,
\citet{lan2025mind} compare VLM uncertainty against human uncertainty, while
\citet{lee2023visalign} study alignment between human and model visual
judgments. From the human side, dual-process accounts of judgment under
uncertainty emphasize heuristic reasoning through availability and
representativeness \citep{tversky1974judgment,kahneman2011thinking}, which
provides a useful cognitive backdrop for interpreting blind answers as
plausible guesses under incomplete grounding rather than arbitrary noise. Our
work extends this line of research to visual questions under controlled
referential degradation. Rather than focusing on perceptual judgments or
confidence calibration alone, we compare human and model answer
distributions as visual grounding is progressively weakened.

\paragraph{Chain-of-Thought and Thinking Models.}
Chain-of-thought prompting \citep{wei2022chain} showed that eliciting
intermediate reasoning steps substantially improves LLM performance on
multi-step tasks. Subsequent work extended this to multimodal settings,
where reasoning traces help models integrate visual and linguistic evidence
\citep{zhang2023multimodal}. More recently, \emph{thinking models}---such as
DeepSeek-R1 \citep{deepseekai2025deepseekr1} and the Qwen3 thinking series---are
trained with reinforcement learning to produce extended internal reasoning
before emitting a final answer, developing what can be described as a capacity
for deliberate reflection before committing. In grounded settings, such traces
have been shown to improve calibration and reduce shortcut reliance. We probe
whether this capacity extends to the blind setting: does more deliberate
reasoning help a model construct a more human-like imagined scene when no visual
input is available? Our results suggest that extended traces modulate but do not
fundamentally alter the absence-default behavior of VLMs, pointing to a
distinction between reasoning \emph{capacity} and learned visual \emph{priors}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Question pool and abstention filtering.}
We begin with 1{,}000 questions randomly sampled from the VQA v2.0 validation split \citep{goyal2016making}, spanning yes/no, numerical, and open-ended types. A preliminary blind-only sweep over this full pool showed that abstention is highly model-dependent, especially without the explicit no-image instruction: some standalone configurations refuse a large fraction of questions, whereas most VLMs and backbone decoders remain near zero (Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_calibration}, Figure~\ref{fig:abstention_scale}). Because human--model comparison on refusal-heavy items would mostly measure whether a model declines to answer rather than what prior it expresses, we construct a matched 113-question subset that minimizes this distortion.

% \paragraph{Shared substantive-response subset.}
For this filtering step, we treat \emph{hard abstention} as explicit refusal or no-image language (e.g., \textit{``cannot''}, \textit{``no image''}) and \emph{soft abstention} as non-substantive placeholders that withhold an answer (e.g., \textit{``unknown''}, \textit{``unanswerable''}); hedged guesses such as \textit{``maybe 2''} are retained as committed responses (Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_classifier}, Table~\ref{tab:abstention_keywords}). We then select questions on which all model groups give substantive answers, while avoiding a narrow concentration in just a few semantic clusters. This produces a subset whose abstention stays under 12\% for all models (Table~\ref{tab:abstention_inst_effect_vABC_q113}) and whose questions remain spread across the broader pool (Appendix~\ref{app:tsne}).

\paragraph{Entity coverage and diversity.}
Our dataset subset is designed to preserve variety while keeping the human--model comparison meaningful under blind prompting. The original VQA metadata is organized largely around surface question forms such as \textit{what}, \textit{how many}, or \textit{is there}, but those labels do not cleanly separate the reasoning demand from the semantic field of the anchor-bearing noun phrase. For analysis, we therefore annotate each question along two orthogonal dimensions: \emph{operation} (e.g., count, attribute, action, spatial, identity) and \emph{entity} (e.g., person, animal, object, vehicle, food, place). This two-axis taxonomy separates \emph{what kind of reasoning} the question requires from \emph{what semantic field} its lexical anchors evoke, so agreement patterns can be analyzed independently by operation and by entity type rather than conflated in a single label space.

Within that taxonomy, we aim for relatively even coverage across \emph{entity} groupings. This matters for the control-variant analysis below: once lexical anchors are weakened or replaced by pronouns, some question types remain interpretable under broad commonsense priors while others become heavily anchor-dependent. Broad entity coverage therefore helps test that degradation pattern across a wider range of latent scene priors rather than concentrating it in a small number of repeated object domains. The resulting 113-question subset is also broadly spread across the larger question space (Appendix~\ref{app:tsne}, Figure~\ref{fig:tsne}).

% \paragraph{Question taxonomy.}
The resulting subset covers a broad range of entity categories and reasoning operations; Tables~\ref{tab:taxonomy} summarize the taxonomy. These labels were extracted with a structured prompt-based annotation pipeline, and Appendix~\ref{app:sem_extraction} provides the prompt and annotation details.
Each question is annotated along two axes:
\emph{operation type} captures the reasoning demand and \emph{entity type} captures the semantic field of the subject.
Human--model comparisons use 113 questions across three controlled reformulations (\S\ref{sec:control}), yielding 339 question--variant pairs.

\paragraph{Control question variants.}
\label{sec:control}
To test how strongly blind answers depend on entity-denoting lexical anchors,
we generate progressively abstracted variants of each VQA question using GPT-4.1-mini under a constrained
JSON schema. The transformations systematically remove lexical information while
preserving the underlying question structure and reasoning operation. We create
four variants: (1) deictic markers and spatial references removed, (2) specific
object nouns replaced with a neutral placeholder, (3) object nouns generalized
to a higher-level semantic category (e.g., \emph{dog} $\rightarrow$
\emph{animal}), and (4) full pronominalization, in which content noun phrases
are replaced with minimal pronouns while retaining the original grammatical
frame. The complete prompt, schema, and transformation rules are provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:ctl}.

These variants define a controlled abstraction hierarchy that progressively
reduces entity-specific information while preserving the operation required to
answer the question. This allows us to test whether human and model responses
depend primarily on lexical cues or on broader semantic and commonsense priors.
The same ladder also functions as a pronominalization test for anchor
dependence: if blind behavior degrades sharply when content-noun anchors are
weakened or replaced by pronouns, then the model is relying on surface lexical
anchors rather than stable, human-like scene priors.

\subsection{Models and Inference Setup}
\label{sec:inference}

We evaluate models in three groups:
\textbf{VLM} (full vision-language stack), \textbf{Backbone decoder} (same
checkpoint, vision encoder bypassed), \textbf{Standalone LLM}. VLMs span the Qwen3-VL \citep{bai2025qwen3vl}, LLaVA
\citep{liu2023visual,liu2024llavanext}, and InternVL \citep{zhu2025internvl3}
families; standalone LLM baselines include Qwen3 \citep{yang2025qwen3},
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Mistral-7B, Vicuna-7B/13B, and Phi-3.5-mini.
All evaluations are zero-shot at temperature~0.
% , and a 7--8B scale-matched subset is used for all main architectural comparisons; the scale analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:scale_variant}) spans the full model range.
\textbf{Backbone decoder} runs are weight-identical to their paired VLM checkpoints and differ only by removing image input at inference; \textbf{standalone LLM} checkpoints are family-matched to the language decoders of those VLMs (e.g., Mistral-7B for LLaVA-Mistral, Qwen3-8B for InternVL-8B).
All inference runs were executed on dual NVIDIA TITAN RTX GPUs.

Our experimental paradigm replaces the original image with a blank 224$\times$224 black image together with the instruction \textit{``Note: No images are provided. For each question, imagine an appropriate image exists and answer based on the most common or universal scenario.''} This framing elicits prior-driven answers while reducing refusal behavior.
We also run additional blind-only control experiments and report the resulting values in appendix tables (Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_calibration}).

\subsection{Human Data Collection}
\label{sec:human_data}

40 participants (online university forums, IRB-approved) answered text-only
blind-instruction VQA questions in Korean or English, yielding 15,336 total responses
(339 question--variant pairs per participant).
Participants were given the same blind-awareness instruction used for the model
condition: \textit{``Note: No images are provided. For each question, imagine an
appropriate image exists and answer based on the most common or universal
scenario.''}
Responses were translated and VQA-normalized uniformly before scoring.
Full protocol details and distributions are in Appendix~\ref{app:human}
and~\ref{app:human_stats}.

\subsection{Metrics and Evaluation Protocols}
\label{sec:eval_protocols}

\textbf{Preprocessing.} Before scoring, all answers are translated to English
when needed, normalized with the standard VQA pipeline
\citep{antol2015vqa}, and stripped of generic wrappers so human and model
responses are compared on the same cleaned answer string. These normalized
responses are the inputs to the human--human and human--model agreement
calculations. Appendix~\ref{app:human} and
Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_classifier} provide the full preprocessing and
cleaning details.

\textbf{Distributional alignment.} For categorical questions such as \textit{yes/no}
and \textit{count}, we compare each model response (a single answer per question--variant
at temperature~0) against the per-question human answer distribution using
Jensen--Shannon divergence, total variation distance, and a modal-match rate
(`Mode': whether the model's answer matches the human modal answer for that question).
All comparisons are per-question: the model's answer is treated as a point mass and
evaluated against the empirical distribution of 40 human responses for the same question--variant.

\textbf{Semantic alignment.} For answer-level semantic agreement, we compute pairwise
Sentence-BERT \citep{reimers2019sentence} cosine similarity between responses.
Specifically, we use the \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} encoder
\citep{song2020mpnet}. For each question and variant, we compare each model
answer against the set of human answers and average over humans to obtain a
per-question human--model score. Human--human (HH) agreement is computed
analogously from distinct human pairs.

% For model $m$, question $q$, and variant $v$,
% $\mathrm{HM}_{m,q,v}$ is the mean SBERT cosine between the model answer and
% all individual human answers, and $\mathrm{HH}_{q,v}$ is the mean SBERT cosine
% across distinct human--human pairs for the same question and variant. For
% grouped views such as the HH--HM scatterplots, we average these per-question
% scores over the questions and variants in slice $g$ to obtain
% $\overline{\mathrm{HM}}_{m,g}$ and $\overline{\mathrm{HH}}_g$.

Embedding-based agreement can be sensitive to answer length and response
format. This matters here because VQA-style answers are typically short,
whereas some standalone LLMs produce longer explanatory responses. We
therefore use raw SBERT as the primary metric, report lexical-overlap
robustness checks (chrF, ROUGE-1, exact match), and include a length-normalized
evaluation that truncates answers to $\leq$5 words after preprocessing. The
main ranking is largely stable, though some more verbose models shift more
under truncation; details are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:metrics} and
Appendix~\ref{app:thinking} (Table~\ref{tab:comprehensive_inst_blind};
Figure~\ref{fig:sbert_truncation_scatter}; Figure~\ref{fig:think_abs_vs_length}).

\textbf{Task Performance.} Although no image is provided at inference time,
we report blind VQA task accuracy as a complementary behavioral signal of how
models respond to question-only inputs. We include it as a secondary metric
because it also indicates how strongly models leverage question-side priors,
biases, and shortcuts rather than true visual grounding under blind answering
\citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.

\textbf{Confidence alignment.} We also analyze token-level confidence as an
auxiliary diagnostic of whether models express uncertainty in a human-like way.
These analyses are not a primary selection metric in the main paper, but they
provide a complementary view of calibration and question-level difficulty
tracking.

\section{Human-Model Blind Alignment}
\label{sec:alignment}

\subsection{Human Blind Priors: Reliability and Patterns}
\label{sec:human_patterns}

\paragraph{Signal convergence and reference reliability.}
Participants responded quickly (median RT 4.1~s) and concisely (92.6\%
single-word answers), with response times nearly identical across all three
formulations and abstention effectively absent throughout. Three independently
collected signals---subjective confidence, behavioral RT, and inter-rater
semantic agreement (HH SBERT)---converge on the same per-question difficulty
dimension (Figure~\ref{fig:conf_rt_sbert_triangle}): RT is strongly negatively
correlated with HH SBERT across all variants ($r{=}{-}0.854$ to $-0.860$;
all $p{<}.001$), and confidence tracks positively in the original and
weaker-object variants ($r{=}0.228$--$0.299$; both $p{<}.016$). Per-question
HH SBERT is also highly stable across formulations ($r{>}0.88$ for all variant
pairs), confirming that the human reference captures a reliable latent
difficulty signal rather than annotation noise. We use mean pairwise HH
agreement throughout as a behavioral reference ceiling.

when the noun anchor is present---humans converge on specific species---but drops
to $0.34$ when pronominalized, as the referent disappears and answers scatter.

\textit{``What type of bread does this appear to be?''} illustrates this, with HH SBERT
falling from $0.53$ to $0.32$ under pronominalization.

with tightly clustered answers appear more stable because semantically similar
responses remain close in embedding space even under lexical weakening.
Existence questions illustrate this: 93 of 95 exist-type questions are yes/no
(Table~\ref{tab:q113_sample_composition}), so humans converge by default and
HH SBERT remains high ($0.87$) even under pronominalization.
We therefore treat the weaker entity-group degradation as an interpretive
limitation of the metric and rely more heavily on operation-type differences
when characterizing anchor fragility.

\paragraph{Yes/no prior bias.} Most full VLMs suppress the human yes-leaning prior,
answering \textit{yes} on only 20--37\% of yes/no questions for the LLaVA family
and 0--41\% for Qwen3-VL, against a human baseline of 65\%

alignment on count questions as well.

\paragraph{Can instruction restore scene imagination?}
Given the VLM zero-default established above, a natural question is whether
an explicit instruction to \emph{imagine a scene} actually activates scene imagination---or
merely perturbs the answer surface without inducing a coherent imagined world.
Figure~\ref{fig:count_transition} shows this for the Qwen3 family across all
three architecture types; the architecture ordering is consistent, but none
fully recovers the human distribution.

Concrete examples expose the quality of the ``imagination'' instruction triggers.
For \emph{``How many birds?''}, humans blind-answer with a spread of small
positive numbers (modal: ``3''); VLMs answer ``0'' both blind
and with instruction, while backbone decoders shift to arbitrary values (10, 5, 2)
that are plausible but uncoordinated.
For \emph{``How many stripes does the zebra have?''}, humans distribute
across approximate counts (2, 20, 50, countless), reflecting calibrated uncertainty
about a recall-from-memory question; VLMs that are stuck at ``0'' blind jump to
arbitrarily large values (50, 100) when instructed, overshooting the human
distribution rather than anchoring near it.
% For \emph{``How many apple trees are in the picture?''}, humans imagine a
% small orchard scene (modal: ``3''; range 1--6), consistent with scene priors
% built from real-world experience; VLMs remain at ``0'' even under instruction,
% unable to disengage from a null-image interpretation. [redundant with birds example]
The architecture ordering in how much instruction shifts answers is itself
revealing: standalone LLMs change most readily (68\%) because their language
backbone retains count priors from text-only pretraining; backbone decoders
follow at 38\%; full VLMs resist most strongly (14\%), as the vision encoder's
null-image signal suppresses the language prior even when instruction explicitly
asks the model to imagine a scene
(Table~\ref{tab:image_ablation_main}, \S\ref{sec:ablation}).
This points to a specific failure mode of vision-language pretraining: the
language backbone defers count retrieval to the visual pathway, which atrophies
its independent linguistic count priors \citep{paiss2023teaching,parcalabescu2022valse}.
When the visual pathway receives no meaningful signal, that deference becomes a
liability---producing not an ``I cannot see'' abstention, but a committed
zero-default that instruction alone cannot dislodge.

\paragraph{Backbone decoders are the most human-aligned.}
Among all architectures, backbone decoders best reproduce human blind priors,
reaching an HM SBERT of $0.573$---within $0.004$ of the
human--human ceiling ($0.577$) and above both full VLMs ($0.543$) and standalone
LLMs ($0.433$).
This double advantage is interpretable: backbone decoders inherit the rich,
visually grounded world knowledge encoded in VLM language decoders during
multimodal training, while not receiving any visual signal from a blank image
that was never part of their training distribution.
Standalone LLMs lack the former; full VLMs are exposed to the latter.
The result is that the language decoder of a VLM---on its own---carries
the most human-calibrated blind prior among the architectures we test.
Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_sbert} makes this concrete: the backbone decoder panel
sits \emph{above} the full VLM panel across every operation and entity group,
despite weight-identical parameters.
Matched-family tests confirm this in four of five decoder--VLM pairs
(Holm-significant, $\Delta{=}{+}0.013$ to ${+}0.066$); InternVL is the lone
exception where the full VLM slightly leads ($\Delta{=}{-}0.021$, $p{=}0.008$).
Against matched standalone LLMs, every decoder comparison is positive and
significant ($\Delta{=}{+}0.085$ to ${+}0.327$;
Appendix~\ref{tab:matched_family_alignment_tests}).

\paragraph{Commonsense operations align; identity and spatial questions expose the lexical gap.}
Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_sbert} reveals two distinct alignment regimes by operation type.

\textit{Anchor-robust.}
Existence, Action, and World Knowledge questions sit in the top-right of the
scatter, near or above the diagonal across all model classes.
Both humans and models converge on strong priors regardless of whether the anchor
noun is present---\textit{``What is the animal doing?''} and \textit{``What is
it doing?''} draw on the same locomotion prior, so the lexical cue adds little.

\textit{Anchor-sensitive.}
Comparison, Identity, and Spatial questions cluster in the bottom-left and fall
\emph{below} the diagonal: human agreement is already low because these questions
are inherently referent-specific, and models fall further below humans once the
anchor is removed.
Count questions are a different case---they sit \emph{above} the diagonal for
VLMs, but this reflects the zero-default shortcut rather than genuine alignment.

Taken together, alignment is determined first by architecture, then by the

\section{What Modulates Alignment}
\label{sec:ablation}

\paragraph{Visual encoding.}
The backbone decoder advantage is not a black-image artifact.
Testing blank, gray, noise, and white inputs on Qwen3-VL-8B and LLaVA-Mistral
shows that no uninformative image condition recovers backbone-level alignment:
for Qwen3-VL-8B, SBERT ranges from $0.399$ (noise) to $0.549$ (white),
never reaching the backbone's $0.573$.
Any active vision pathway shifts answers away from the pure language prior---not
as a failure to help, but as a grounding-oriented bias that persists even when
the visual input carries no task-relevant signal.
Full ablation results are in Appendix~\ref{app:ablation} (Table~\ref{tab:image_ablation_main}).

\paragraph{Scale.}
Scale matters within groups, but not across them: increasing parameter count
does not erase the backbone $>$ VLM $>$ standalone LLM ordering.
On human--model alignment, backbone decoders improve modestly with scale and
approach the HH ceiling, while full VLMs are flat; \emph{group membership
predicts alignment better than size}, with a 1B backbone decoder exceeding a
7B standalone LLM.
Higher blind accuracy with scale does not track better alignment---VLMs at
4--8B reach ${\approx}51\%$ blind accuracy yet fall below the human--human
SBERT ceiling, reflecting shortcut convergence rather than human-like priors
(Appendix~\ref{app:scale_variant}, Figure~\ref{fig:scale}).

\paragraph{Extended reasoning.}
Thinking does not reliably improve human--model alignment.
Within the Qwen3 family, longer reasoning traces raise blind accuracy for 4B
and 8B models but do not consistently improve HM SBERT
(8B: $0.507$ vs.\ $0.522$ non-thinking, on original questions).
The 32B thinking model takes the opposite pattern: near-empty traces yet
slightly better alignment, at the cost of sharply lower accuracy.
Thinking therefore behaves more like a family-specific decoding intervention
than a general route to more human-like blind priors
(Figure~\ref{fig:think_abs_vs_length}, Appendix~\ref{app:thinking}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Backbone decoders carry the most human-calibrated prior.}
Vision co-training embeds rich, human-like linguistic priors in the language
backbone, but the vision encoder introduces distributional biases that push
responses away from those priors when no meaningful visual content is present.
The backbone decoder---approximating a question-conditioned prior $P(a \mid q)$
rather than $P(a \mid q, v_0)$---is therefore the most human-aligned
architecture, not because it is more capable, but because it is not exposed to
an out-of-distribution visual signal.
This sensitivity is not purely a defect: a VLM that remains responsive to the
visual pathway may be better positioned to use genuine visual evidence when
present. The limitation our diagnostic exposes is not that the encoder is
``too visual,'' but that the model lacks a reliable way to distinguish
informative from uninformative visual states---a gap that VLM design could
address through learned input-uncertainty gating.

\paragraph{Lexical anchors as prior activators, structured by operation type.}
The control-variant degradation chain reveals that blind model success is
predominantly carried by content-noun lexical cues. When these are stripped,
models diverge from human priors while humans barely change---but this effect
is not uniform.
Commonsense-grounded operations (Existence, Action, World Knowledge) are
anchor-robust: both humans and models draw on stable scene priors that survive
pronominalization.
Referent-specific operations (Identity, Spatial, Comparison) are
anchor-sensitive: once the content noun is removed, both human agreement and
model alignment collapse, but models fall further---revealing that model
answers in these categories are driven more by lexical cues than by robust
world knowledge.
This operation-level structure identifies a concrete mechanism---lexically
anchored prior recall---that benchmark design and debiasing interventions can
explicitly target.
We note that benchmark contamination may amplify this effect:
\citet{chen2024are} show that VLMs can achieve non-trivial scores without
visual input partly due to training-set leakage; our pronominalization test
partially disentangles memorization from corpus-level priors, since models
retain above-chance performance even after lexical cues are removed.

\paragraph{Linguistic priors as structured imagination.}
Backbone decoders achieve near-human semantic agreement ($r \approx 0.57$--$0.72$
on per-question difficulty) without any visual input, indicating that language models
have internalized structured world knowledge that functions as a form of visual imagination.
The gap between backbone decoders and full VLMs suggests this linguistic imagination is
\emph{disrupted} rather than enhanced by the vision encoder on uninformative inputs:
multimodal training introduces a form of sensory interference when images carry no
task-relevant signal, shifting responses away from the very priors the backbone had learned.
Our findings raise the question of whether ``visual grounding'' in current VLMs is as much
about activating language-embedded world knowledge as it is about extracting information
from pixels---a distinction with direct implications for interpretability and the design of
more robust visually grounded systems.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Participants were recruited from university communities (Korean/English), limiting demographic scope.
Model evaluation uses greedy decoding, which may underestimate answer diversity.
Human data covers only the instruction-blind condition; corpus-frequency correlation
\citep{mccoy2024embers} and broader image ablations are left to future work.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

This work studies how VLMs respond to visual questions in the absence of
any image, and how closely that behavior resembles the blind priors that
humans themselves express under the same condition.

Backbone decoders---the language components of VLMs, stripped of visual input---come
closest to human blind priors, nearly reaching the human--human agreement ceiling
and consistently outperforming both full VLMs and standalone LLMs.
This confirms the intuition that VLM language decoders carry rich world knowledge
from multimodal training, while also showing that the vision encoder introduces
a distributional pull on uninformative images that pushes responses away from
those very priors.
High blind accuracy does not accompany this: the models with the strongest shortcut
behavior score well on VQA metrics while diverging from human response distributions,
underscoring that accuracy alone is an unreliable proxy for whether a model is
doing what humans do.

The operation-level breakdown provides the most concrete evidence.
Existence, Action, and World Knowledge questions carry stable priors that survive
pronominalization for both humans and models---these are questions with strong
commonsense answers that do not depend on the specific referent.
Comparison, Identity, and Spatial questions are anchor-dependent: both human
agreement and human--model alignment drop when content nouns are removed, but
models fall further, revealing that their answers are driven more by surface
lexical cues than by the kind of robust scene imagination humans apply.
Entity type, by contrast, explains little of the variance---what matters is the
reasoning demand, not the semantic domain.
Together, these findings suggest that lexical prior exploitation in VLMs is not
uniform but structured, and that pronominalization-based probing offers a practical
tool for separating genuine world knowledge from surface-anchored shortcut behavior.

\subsection{Control Question Extraction Pipeline}
\label{app:ctl}

Control variants are extracted using GPT-4.1-mini-2025-04-14 (temperature~0,
seed~42, JSON schema mode) in batches of 50 questions per API call.
The output schema enforces
\texttt{\{deictic\_removed, object\_removed, weaker\_object, pronominalized\}}
as required string fields with \texttt{additionalProperties:~false}.
The system prompt is reproduced verbatim below:

\begin{quote}\small
Generate 4 control variants for each VQA question.
Return ONLY a JSON object \texttt{\{"results":[...]\}}; preserve input order.

\textbf{Rules:}

1) \textit{deictic\_removed}: Remove deictic words (this/that/these/those/here/there)
and spatial markers (left/right/top/bottom/foreground/background) while preserving
grammar. Restructure the sentence if needed to remain fluent.

2) \textit{object\_removed}: Replace the specific object noun with neutral placeholder
``object''. Do not replace pronouns or generic nouns already present.

3) \textit{weaker\_object}: Replace the specific object noun with a broader hypernym
one level up (dog$\to$animal, car$\to$vehicle, drink$\to$beverage, shirt$\to$clothing,
apple$\to$fruit, knife$\to$utensil, sofa$\to$furniture). If no clear hypernym
exists, use ``object''.

4) \textit{pronominalized}: Replace ALL content noun phrases with minimal pronouns.
Grammar-aware rules: attribute (``What color is the dog?'') $\to$ ``What color is it?'';
count (``How many cats are there?'') $\to$ ``How many are there?'';
location (``Where is the phone?'') $\to$ ``Where is it?'';
action (``What is the man doing?'') $\to$ ``What is it doing?'';
yes/no (``Is the dog affectionate?'') $\to$ ``Is it affectionate?'';
multi-NP (``between the girl and the giraffe'') $\to$ ``between them''.
Keep ONLY question words, copula, prepositions, predicate/attribute, and pronouns.
Use ``it''/``they''/``them''---never ``the object''.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Question Taxonomy Extraction}
\label{app:sem_extraction}

Each question in the pool is annotated along two axes --- \emph{operation type} (what reasoning the question demands) and \emph{entity type} (what semantic field its subject belongs to) --- using GPT-4.1-mini-2025-04-14 (temperature~0, seed~42, JSON schema mode).
The output schema enforces \texttt{op} and \texttt{ent} as required enumerated string fields.
Annotations were extracted in batches of 50 questions per API call with \texttt{additionalProperties:~false} enforced throughout.
The system prompt is reproduced verbatim below:

\begin{quote}\small
Extract VQA question semantics from text only; do not invent visual details.
Return ONLY a JSON object \texttt{\{"results":[...]\}} matching the schema exactly; preserve input order.

\textbf{Decision rules:}

1) \textit{op} priority (highest to lowest):
\texttt{text} (reading cue: written/word/number/say/spell/label/sign/license; brand/logo only if implied visible/printed) $>$
\texttt{count} (how many/number of) $>$
\texttt{spat} (explicit left/right/top/bottom/foreground/background; where-questions; relations like behind/next to) $>$
\texttt{temp} (how long/how old/aged/before/after) $>$
\texttt{cause} (why/reason) $>$
\texttt{comp} (comparative/superlative) $>$
\texttt{attr} (color/material/type/age/duration/name/location asked) $>$
\texttt{exist} (only existential presence: ``is/are there'', ``any'', ``present'') $>$
\texttt{ident} (who/what/which entity is) $>$
\texttt{act} (activity/doing) $>$
\texttt{know} (world knowledge beyond simple visual attributes, when no text-reading cue) $>$ \texttt{other}.

2) If \texttt{w=yesno} AND \texttt{op=exist} $\Rightarrow$ \texttt{ans=bool}, \texttt{space=bin}. Other yes/no use \texttt{op} based on content.

3) \texttt{attr} consistency: color$\to$\texttt{ans=color}, \texttt{space=cat}; material$\to$\texttt{mat,cat}; age$\to$\texttt{age,num}; duration$\to$\texttt{dur,num}; count$\to$\texttt{num,num}; name$\to$\texttt{text,cat}; location$\to$\texttt{loc,cat}; type/ident$\to$\texttt{cat,cat}.

4) \texttt{txt=true} iff \texttt{op=text}. \texttt{know=true} iff \texttt{op=know}.

5) \texttt{sp}/\texttt{rel}/\texttt{dx} only if explicit in question.

6) If \texttt{w=where} and \texttt{op} not in \{\texttt{text,know}\} $\Rightarrow$ \texttt{op=spat}, \texttt{attr=loc}, \texttt{ans=loc}, \texttt{space=cat}.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Human Study Protocol and Preprocessing}
\label{app:human}

A total of 40 participants were included. Participants were recruited via
online student community forums at universities. All procedures were conducted
under an IRB-approved protocol adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki.
Informed consent was obtained prior to participation.

Participants answered text-only explicit no-image blind-instruction questions
across the original, weaker-object, and pronominalized formulations. The
analysis applies a raw-response qualification threshold
($\geq$348) before subset intersection, then restricts to the question IDs
answered by all qualifying participants across all three reformulations
(339 question--variant pairs per participant).
Participants were compensated at 10,000~KRW per hour. Responses in Korean or
English were translated to English and processed with the same VQA-style
normalization described in Section~\ref{sec:eval_protocols}. Model outputs are
processed with the same normalization plus the abstention-cleaning pipeline
described in Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_classifier}.

As a supplementary human-only diagnostic, Appendix~\ref{app:entropy_diagnostics}
examines the relationship between per-question answer entropy and human VQA
accuracy. We treat this as a difficulty/ambiguity check rather than a primary
result, since the main paper focuses on semantic agreement and anchor
sensitivity rather than accuracy.

\subsection{Abstention Classifier Details}
\label{app:abstention_classifier}

\input{tables/abstention_keyword_table}

\paragraph{Hard vs.\ soft abstention.}
Hard abstention is detected from the raw output and corresponds to explicit
refusal or visibility-based non-response. Soft abstention is detected after
normalization and corresponds to non-substantive placeholders that withhold an
answer without an explicit refusal. Table~\ref{tab:abstention_keywords} lists
the keyword categories used in the shared classifier.

\paragraph{Design choices.}
Two categories are deliberately excluded from soft abstention:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\textit{``nothing''}}: a valid content answer for questions about
    empty scenes or absent objects (e.g., \textit{``What is in the bowl?''} $\to$
    \textit{``nothing''}). Treating it as abstention would remove meaningful
    negative-evidence responses from the distribution.
  \item \textbf{Hedged yes/no words} (\textit{maybe, possibly, perhaps, uncertain,
    unsure}): these represent partial committed responses to yes/no questions rather
    than refusals. They fall into the \textit{other} category in the answer
    distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:dist}), consistent with how human responses are
    categorized, enabling a fair distributional comparison.
\end{itemize}
All abstention figures and tables in the paper first apply
\texttt{clean\_answer(..., strip\_think=True)} to recover the final answer and
then run the same shared classifier on that cleaned string. The same
classifier is applied uniformly to both model and human answers.
Human hard abstention is 0.00\% and soft abstention is near-zero ($<$0.04\% per variant) across all
three variants---the abstention distinction is therefore a model-side
phenomenon. Crucially, human abstention is invariant to question abstraction
across the original, weaker, and pronominalized variants, whereas model abstention tends to rise as
lexical anchors are removed (Section~\ref{app:human_stats}).

\subsection{Instruction Gating Effect}
\label{app:inst_gating}

For reference, human abstention is effectively zero (hard: 0.00\%, soft: $<$0.04\% per variant),
so the abstention patterns below are a model-side phenomenon rather than a general property of blind answering.

Instruction (blind $\to$ blind with awareness instruction) increases human--model agreement most on
original questions ($+$0.020 SBERT) and attenuates as lexical anchors are
removed (pronominalized: $+$0.012). The behavioral mechanism is abstention
collapse: standalone LLMs shift from $\sim$30\% soft abstention (epistemic hedges
such as \textit{``unknown''} or \textit{``unanswerable''}) to near
zero (Table~\ref{tab:abstention_inst_effect_vABC_q113}), while VLMs and backbones barely
change---indicating their priors are expressed unconditionally. Accuracy
does not improve (Appendix~\ref{app:inst}), confirming that instruction
primarily converts refusal into committed hallucination rather than grounded
reasoning.

\subsection{Abstention Across Control Variants}
\label{app:abstention_variants}

On the 113-question subset, combined soft and hard abstention remains low
relative to the full 1{,}000-question pool but still rises across the
control variants from original to pronominalized.
Hard abstention---explicit refusals such as \textit{``I cannot answer without seeing the image''}---is
suppressed to near-zero for VLMs and backbone decoders by the baked-in
single-word response instruction, and remains negligible for most standalone LLMs.
The exception is Mistral-7B, which produces verbose refusals that bypass
the instruction.
Soft abstentions---single-word hedges such as ``unknown'' or ``unanswerable''---drive
the overall pattern: abstention rises as entity anchors are removed from original to pronominalized
across all groups, most sharply for VLMs (${\sim}2$--3\% on the original variant to ${\sim}6$--11\% on the pronominalized variant).
Under the paper's abstention classifier, human hard abstention is 0.00\% and
soft abstention is near-zero ($<$0.04\% per variant) at every variant, so the abstention patterns below are a
model-side phenomenon.
The lower panel of Figure~\ref{fig:abstention_scale} shows the full per-model breakdown.

\subsection{Blind Awareness Instruction Effect}
\label{app:inst}

The explicit blind-awareness instruction was: \textit{``Note: No images are provided. For each
question, imagine an appropriate image exists and answer based on the most
common or universal scenario.''}

\paragraph{Effect on abstention.}
On the full 1{,}000-question pool, VLMs and backbone decoders remain near zero ($<$2\% and $<$5\%
respectively) in both conditions; standalone LLMs span a wide range (Mistral-7B: 43\%$\to$33\%).
The per-model numerical breakdown is in Table~\ref{tab:abstention_inst_effect_vABC_q113}.

\paragraph{Effect on response content.}
Table~\ref{tab:response_change_variants} shows the fraction of answers that change
between blind and instruction-aware conditions, across all three question
variants (original, subject-ablated, and pronominalized).
Instruction is a far stronger behavioral perturbation for standalone LLMs
(25--96\% of answers change) than for VLMs (12--42\%) or backbone
decoders (20--42\%). Change rates are broadly stable across variants for VLMs and
backbones, but standalone LLMs tend to be more responsive on the pronominalized variant
(pronominalized), where lexical anchors are removed.
This large behavioral shift without corresponding accuracy gains
confirms that instruction reroutes the model's language prior rather than
unlocking latent visual knowledge.

\input{tables/response_change_variants}

\paragraph{Effect on accuracy and agreement.}
Instruction changes accuracy by only a few points on average, confirming
that it does not reliably improve task performance even when it
substantially changes answer behavior. Human--model SBERT agreement
increases by ${\approx}{+}0.09$ on average (VLM $+$0.10, backbone $+$0.10,
standalone LLM $+$0.07), with the largest gains on
the original variant and diminishing returns on the pronominalized variant, consistent with
instruction acting primarily as a lexical-anchor activator rather than a
general alignment mechanism.

\textit{polar bear}, and \textit{asian black bear} on the original,
but agreement collapses further when the subject becomes \textit{``it''}
since any large animal becomes a plausible referent.

reflecting the same species-frequency prior.
For artifact identity, \textit{``What type of shoe is that?''}
($\Delta{=}0.32$) sees humans spread across \textit{sneakers},

\textit{sneaker} (12/13 responses)---overconfidently selecting the
corpus-frequent default regardless of variant.

$\Delta{=}0.24$, HH-HM gap${=}0.11$) show the largest human--model gap,
driven by dialect variation (\textit{goalie} vs.\ \textit{goalkeeper})

\subsection{Does More Thinking Help?}
\label{app:thinking}

To test whether explicit reasoning traces improve blind priors, we again focus on Qwen3,
which provides matched thinking and non-thinking variants within the same model family
across sizes (0.6B--32B), allowing scale and family to be held constant.
We compare standalone Qwen3 models with and without thinking mode at matched
parameter sizes. Two quantities matter: the length of the emitted reasoning
trace and whether the model emits a trace at all. Table~\ref{tab:think_stats} and Figure~\ref{fig:think_abs_vs_length}
show that neither quantity predicts better human alignment in a monotonic way.
The longest traces come from the 4B and 8B thinking models, but their HM SBERT
is comparable to or lower than the corresponding non-thinking models. The 32B
thinking model is the opposite extreme: it frequently emits no reasoning trace,
has much lower blind accuracy than the 32B non-thinking model, but is slightly
more human-aligned under SBERT. The strongest positive case is Qwen3-1.7B,
where thinking improves both HM SBERT and blind accuracy on the original
question form, but that benefit does not scale cleanly. Taken together, these
results suggest that extra reasoning text changes the decoder's answer policy,
but does not provide a dependable path to more human-like blind priors.

\subsection{Confidence Alignment by Architecture}
\label{app:confidence_alignment}

Per-question Pearson correlation between mean human self-reported confidence and mean
model token log-probability reveals a structural dissociation: answer alignment and
uncertainty alignment can diverge substantially across architecture groups.

Backbone decoders and standalone LLMs exhibit the highest positive
human--model confidence correlation on original questions
(backbone: $r{=}0.38$--$0.39$; standalone LLM: $r{=}0.41$), and both degrade
under anchor removal (to $r{=}0.27$ on the pronominalized variant). VLMs are weakest throughout
($r{=}0.13$, $0.13$, and $0.04$ for the original, weaker, and pronominalized variants respectively), despite strong
blind accuracy. A model can answer blindly in a
benchmark-favored way without matching the questions humans experience as easy or hard.
Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_confidence} shows the same pattern at the grouped level:
confidence is comparatively flat across both operation and entity categories and
separates architecture groups less clearly than semantic agreement does.

Figure~\ref{fig:ecdf_confidence_7b} shows the ECDF of mean token log-probability
for each 7B/8B model grouped by architecture (human confidence ECDF shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:human_ecdf}).
Two patterns recur across groups.
First, several models operate in a compressed high-confidence regime: curves rise
sharply near logprob~$0$, indicating that many blind questions receive similarly
strong token confidence regardless of difficulty. Second, variant separation is
highly model-dependent: VLMs show little shift across variants, while backbone
decoders and standalone LLMs show larger original-to-pronominalized shifts. In contrast, the human
confidence ECDF below is nearly unchanged across variants, with only a modest
downward shift under pronominalization. Humans therefore appear to preserve a
stable confidence scale under abstraction, whereas model confidence is more
architecture-dependent and often less discriminative. Together, these
distributional differences explain why token confidence is a weak proxy for
human-like uncertainty despite being numerically high across most models.

uncertainty correlates with lower human--model alignment. \textbf{Right:} Entropy
mismatch (model $-$ human) vs original$\to$pronominalized degradation.}
\label{fig:entropy}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:entropy} provides the information-theoretic complement.
Human and model answer entropies are positively correlated, confirming
that both are more uncertain on the same questions. However, models
systematically show lower entropy than humans---they are more
concentrated on a single answer even when humans distribute across
alternatives. This entropy gap is largest on entity-grounded questions
where models exploit a dominant corpus-frequency prior (e.g., ``sneaker''
for shoe type, ``0'' for count questions).

We treat these entropy analyses as supplementary diagnostics rather than
primary evaluation metrics. Because entropy is computed over translated and
normalized answer forms rather than semantic clusters, it is best interpreted
as a proxy for response dispersion or surface-form uncertainty, not a direct
measure of semantic disagreement.

As a complementary human-only diagnostic, per-question human answer entropy
is strongly negatively correlated with human VQA accuracy ($r \approx -0.77$),
indicating that questions eliciting more diverse blind answers are also the
questions humans find harder to answer correctly.

HH--HM correlation summary. VLMs and backbone decoders cluster near
$r{=}0.87$--$0.94$ with tight variant spread, while standalone LLMs are
consistently lower.

\section{Answer Aggregation}
\label{app:aggregation}

We cluster semantically equivalent answers (e.g., ``3'' and ``three'') using
GPT-4o mini \citep{openai2025gpt4o}. Human responses are aggregated into a
confidence-weighted distribution:
\begin{equation}
H(a_k^*) = \frac{\sum_{i:\phi(a_i)=a_k^*} c_i}{\sum_i c_i}
\end{equation}
where $c_i$ is participant $i$'s Likert confidence (normalized to $[0,1]$)
and $\phi$ maps raw answers to canonical forms.
