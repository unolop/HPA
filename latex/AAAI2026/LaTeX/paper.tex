\begin{abstract}
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) can achieve non-trivial accuracy on visual
question answering (VQA) benchmarks without any visual input, suggesting
reliance on linguistic priors embedded in the question text. This is
typically treated as a training-data leakage artifact or shortcut
behavior, but humans facing questions under incomplete information also
rely on systematic cognitive heuristics---availability,
representativeness, and world knowledge---to produce structured rather
than random responses. Are model blind priors merely dataset leakage, or
do they reflect something closer to the human cognitive priors that
arise under the same conditions? Prior work has diagnosed this phenomenon
through benchmark filtering and counterfactual images, but the
\emph{structure} of these model priors---and whether they resemble the
human priors that arise under the same conditions---remains largely
unexplored. We address this gap by comparing blind responses from 37 models
across three architectural groups against a human blind-answer reference
($N{=}40$, 113 questions, 3 variants), measuring semantic agreement rather than ground-truth accuracy.
To analyze where human and model priors converge or diverge, we introduce an
\emph{operation--entity taxonomy} that categorizes each question by its
reasoning operation (count, identity, action, attribute, spatial) and entity
type (person, animal, object, food, place). A counterintuitive finding
emerges: the model group most aligned with human blind priors is not the full
VLM but the \emph{backbone decoder}---the same weights with the vision encoder
bypassed. Image-condition ablations show that on uninformative input, the
vision encoder actively modulates the language model's output distribution
rather than cleanly withdrawing. Progressive reformulations that strip
content-noun anchors reveal lexical-anchor dependence: model agreement drops
monotonically while the human--human ceiling barely moves. The
operation--entity decomposition further shows that alignment varies
systematically by reasoning type, with identity questions most sensitive to
anchor removal and count questions showing persistent human--model divergence
in answer distributions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) achieve impressive performance on visual question
answering (VQA) benchmarks \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}. Yet prior
work has shown that text-only models can achieve non-trivial accuracy on VQA
without any visual input \citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin}, and recent
benchmark audits confirm that language-only signals remain exploitable in
modern multimodal evaluations \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
Analogous to hypothesis-only baselines in natural language inference
\citep{poliak2018hypothesis}, where models can predict entailment from the
hypothesis alone without seeing the premise, VLMs can exploit shortcut priors
carried by the question text to produce plausible answers without grounding
them in the image. This raises a broader question beyond benchmark accuracy:
when visual grounding is unavailable, what priors drive model answers, and to
what extent are those priors shared with human linguistic expectations? In
this paper, we investigate this question by comparing blind model responses
against a human reference, revealing that the vision encoder's behavior on
uninformative input plays a key role in shaping how model priors diverge from
human ones.

Prior work on VQA has largely approached this issue through the lens of
dataset bias and shortcut learning. Early work showed that text-only models
can answer many VQA questions correctly without visual input
\citep{jabri2016revisiting,zhang2016yin}, motivating the complementary-image
construction of VQA~v2.0 \citep{goyal2016making}. More recent evaluation
audits show that substantial language-only signal remains available even in
modern multimodal benchmarks \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}. In our own
blind VQA setting, modern VLMs still answer 37--54\% of questions correctly
without seeing any image. However, most prior work evaluates this phenomenon
primarily in terms of benchmark accuracy. Much less is known about the
\emph{structure} of the underlying answer priors themselves, including which
lexical anchors allow models to shortcut image grounding.

At the same time, research in cognitive psychology has long established that
humans facing decisions under uncertainty rely on systematic heuristics---such
as availability and representativeness \citep{tversky1974judgment}---rather
than responding randomly. When visual evidence is missing, these heuristics
produce structured prior-driven responses: predictable default answers that
reflect world knowledge, prototypical associations, and frequency-based
expectations \citep{kahneman2011thinking}. This makes human blind answers a
meaningful reference point, not mere noise. Recent work on human--AI alignment
argues that model behavior should be evaluated against human response
distributions rather than only against task labels
\citep{lan2025mind,bao2026imagination}. \citet{kwon2025whatllms} show that
LLMs exhibit distinct intrinsic generative biases even under minimal
prompts, with different model families favoring systematically different
topical and structural defaults. Under ambiguous or underspecified
inputs, humans show structured patterns of agreement and disagreement that
reveal latent expectations about plausible interpretations, whereas
contemporary models often collapse toward narrow modal regions of response
space \citep{bao2026imagination}. In our setting, this motivates treating
blind VQA answers not just as right or wrong guesses, but as distributions
over plausible interpretations: some questions induce strong shared human
priors, while others open up pluralistic answer regions once lexical anchors
are weakened. The question is not whether such priors exist, but which priors
remain stable, which collapse under underspecification, and where model priors
diverge from human ones.

In this work, we study human and model priors under referential
underspecification in visual questions. Starting from VQA questions, we
construct progressively weaker variants that degrade object-denoting lexical
anchors while preserving grammatical structure, creating a controlled
degradation axis from fully specified questions to increasingly
underspecified formulations. This lets us probe where humans and models rely
on those anchors, how much blind answering survives when the anchors are
weakened, and where linguistic priors begin to diverge from grounded visual
reasoning. We first characterize how human answer distributions change along
this axis, identifying question categories that maintain strong consensus and
categories whose answers rapidly diversify under ambiguity. We then compare
these patterns against a diverse set of vision-language models, backbone
decoders, and language-only models to investigate where model priors align
with human priors and where they diverge.

This control-variant ladder also functions as a pronominalization test for
visual grounding. If a model continues to perform well after content-noun
anchors have been weakened or replaced by pronouns, then its blind behavior is
more likely to be carried by shortcut priors than by robust grounding cues.

Our contribution lies in the combination of four methodological tools.
First, we recruit 40 participants to answer VQA
questions under the same blind condition as the models, providing a human
reference for what linguistically plausible prior-driven answers look like.
Second, we introduce an \emph{operation--entity taxonomy} that categorizes
each question along two semantic dimensions---the reasoning operation
required (count, identity, action, attribute, spatial, text) and the entity
type mentioned (person, animal, object, food, place)---enabling diagnostics
at a finer grain than the VQA dataset's original question-type annotations.
Third, we construct progressively weaker question reformulations that strip
content-noun anchors from the original text, creating a controlled
degradation axis that isolates which lexical elements carry prior information.
Fourth, in addition to answer-level semantic agreement, we use a simple
distributional alignment analysis that asks whether models place answer mass
over question types and answer types in ways that resemble the corresponding
human response patterns.

Together, these tools reveal several findings across 37 models from four
architectural groups. Human answer priors without visual evidence turn out
to be selectively structured: consensus depends on what kind of reasoning
the question requires, not what objects it mentions
(Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation}). On the model side, the group whose
answers most closely resemble those of humans is not the full VLM but the
backbone decoder---the same weights with the vision encoder bypassed
(Figure~\ref{fig:scale}). This gap persists across model scales, suggesting
that architecture, rather than capacity, determines how human-like the
resulting priors are. As content-noun anchors are progressively removed,
model--human agreement drops monotonically while the human--human ceiling
barely moves (Figure~\ref{fig:variants} in Appendix), confirming that models depend
heavily on specific lexical cues that humans do not require.
Image-condition ablations (blank, gray, noise, white) further show that the
vision encoder actively modulates the language model's output even on
uninformative input (Table~\ref{tab:image_ablation_main}); the backbone
decoder, free from this modulation, falls back on the pure linguistic
prior---which turns out to be the most human-like. Finally, our taxonomy
reveals where human--model alignment holds and where it breaks: counting
questions show the largest persistent gap despite high human consensus,
with models collapsing to generic small numbers while humans distribute
more broadly (Figure~\ref{fig:op_hm}).

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Question-Only Reasoning, Shortcut Learning, and Ambiguity in Visual Questions.}
Early analyses of VQA benchmarks revealed that text-only models could achieve
non-trivial accuracy without visual input, exposing exploitable
question--answer regularities in the data \citep{jabri2016revisiting}.
Subsequent studies identified systematic answer biases
\citep{zhang2016yin} and showed that many VQA systems rely on language
shortcuts rather than visual grounding \citep{agrawal2018dont}, motivating
debiasing and counterfactual intervention methods
\citep{niu2021counterfactual}. As VLMs have scaled in both architecture and
training data, these concerns have resurfaced: recent benchmark audits show
that language-only signals remain prevalent even in modern multimodal
evaluations \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}. In parallel, recent work has examined how
humans and AI systems behave under ambiguity, underspecification, and
incomplete grounding. AmbigQA \citep{min2020ambigqa} demonstrated that many
questions admit multiple plausible answers and are better represented as
answer distributions rather than single gold labels. Hallucination and
shortcut learning have likewise been widely documented in vision-language
models \citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object,xiao2021hallucination}. Prior
work shows that VLMs can exploit spurious visual or linguistic cues
\citep{han2024instinctive,ye2024mmspubench}, fail to maintain compositional
visual-linguistic bindings \citep{yuksekgonul2023when}, and answer benchmark
questions without requiring image evidence \citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.
These benchmark audits diagnose \emph{whether} models rely on language
priors---\citet{chen2024are} filter for vision-indispensable items, while
\citet{lee2024vlind} test with counterfactual images---but do not ask what
the structure of those priors is or how it compares to human behavior.
We take a complementary approach: rather than filtering out prior-solvable
questions or manipulating images, we remove the image entirely and compare
the resulting model answer distributions against those of humans under the
same blind condition, using a semantic taxonomy that decomposes prior
structure by reasoning operation and entity type.

\paragraph{Human--Model Alignment and Answer Priors.}
A growing body of work evaluates AI systems relative to human judgments rather
than benchmark labels alone. Prior work argues for evaluating alignment
through similarities between human and model response distributions,
particularly in settings involving ambiguity and underspecification.
\citet{bao2026imagination} study human--AI agreement at the level of response
distributions rather than only top-1 labels. In the multimodal domain,
\citet{lan2025mind} compare VLM uncertainty against human uncertainty, while
\citet{lee2023visalign} study alignment between human and model visual
judgments. From the human side, dual-process accounts of judgment under
uncertainty emphasize heuristic reasoning through availability and
representativeness \citep{tversky1974judgment,kahneman2011thinking}, which
provides a useful cognitive backdrop for interpreting blind answers as
plausible guesses under incomplete grounding rather than arbitrary noise. Our
work extends this line of research to visual questions under controlled
referential degradation. Rather than focusing on perceptual judgments or
confidence calibration alone, we compare human and model answer
distributions as visual grounding is progressively weakened.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Models, Dataset, and Inference Conditions}

We evaluate VLMs from the Qwen3-VL \citep{bai2025qwen3vl}, LLaVA
\citep{liu2023visual,liu2024llavanext}, and InternVL \citep{zhu2025internvl3}
families, plus standalone LLM baselines (Qwen3 \citep{yang2025qwen3},
Qwen2.5-7B(-Instruct), Mistral-7B, Vicuna-7B/13B, Phi-3.5-mini).
All evaluations are zero-shot at temperature~0. Throughout the paper, we use
\emph{model family} for named lineages such as Qwen3-VL, InternVL, and
LLaVA, and \emph{model group} for the three evaluation categories below.
Models are organized into three groups:
\textbf{VLM} (full vision-language stack), \textbf{Backbone decoder} (same
checkpoint, vision encoder bypassed), \textbf{standalone LLM}, and
a 7--8B scale-matched subset is used for fair size-controlled comparisons.
Importantly, \textbf{Backbone decoder} runs are weight-identical to their paired
VLM checkpoints and differ only by removing image input at inference, whereas
\textbf{standalone LLM} runs use separate family-matched checkpoints (e.g.,
Mistral-7B as the standalone counterpart to LLaVA-Mistral, Qwen3-8B to InternVL-8B)
rather than decoders extracted from the VLM weights.
All inference and analysis runs were executed on a workstation with dual
NVIDIA TITAN RTX GPUs.

Our evaluation dataset is a subset of VQA v2.0 validation
split \citep{goyal2016making}, spanning yes/no, numerical, and open-ended
types. Human--model comparisons use 113 questions common to all qualifying
participants (\S\ref{sec:human_data}) across three controlled reformulations,
yielding 339 question--variant pairs.
The subset covers the full semantic range of the
pool, confirmed by t-SNE embedding visualization
(Appendix~\ref{app:tsne}).

Our experimental paradigm uses a blank 224$\times$224 black image in place of
the original visual input, together with the instruction \textit{``Note: No
images are provided. For each question, imagine an appropriate image exists
and answer based on the most common or universal scenario.''} This framing
elicits prior-driven answers while reducing refusal behavior. Because
abstention is also an informative aspect of model behavior, we additionally
evaluate blind answering without the instruction and report the resulting
abstention shifts as a supplementary robustness analysis
(Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_calibration}).

\subsection{Control Question Variants}
\label{sec:control}

To isolate the contribution of linguistic priors, we generate progressively
abstracted variants of each VQA question using GPT-4.1-mini under a constrained
JSON schema. The transformations systematically remove lexical information while
preserving the underlying question structure and reasoning operation. We create
four variants: (1) deictic markers and spatial references removed, (2) specific
object nouns replaced with a neutral placeholder, (3) object nouns generalized
to a higher-level semantic category (e.g., \emph{dog} $\rightarrow$
\emph{animal}), and (4) full pronominalization, in which content noun phrases
are replaced with minimal pronouns while retaining the original grammatical
frame. The complete prompt, schema, and transformation rules are provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:ctl}.

These variants define a controlled abstraction hierarchy that progressively
reduces entity-specific information while preserving the operation required to
answer the question. This allows us to test whether human and model responses
depend primarily on lexical cues or on broader semantic and commonsense priors.
For analysis, each question is additionally annotated along two orthogonal
dimensions: \emph{operation} (e.g., count, attribute, action, spatial,
identity) and \emph{entity} (e.g., person, animal, object, vehicle, food,
place). We use this two-axis taxonomy to separate \emph{what kind of reasoning}
the question demands from \emph{what semantic field} its lexical anchors evoke,
so that agreement patterns can be analyzed independently by operation and by
entity type rather than conflated in a single label space.

\subsection{Human Data Collection}
\label{sec:human_data}

40 participants were recruited via online university community forums under
an IRB-approved protocol. Participants answered text-only
explicit no-image blind-instruction questions in Korean or English; responses were translated and
normalized. Translation and normalization are applied uniformly before
scoring so that agreement metrics reflect semantic content rather than
language-specific formatting differences (Appendix~\ref{app:human}).
Agreement metrics are computed over 339 question--variant pairs
per human and per model.

\subsection{Metrics and Evaluation Protocols}
\label{sec:eval_protocols}

\textbf{Task Performance.} Although no image is provided at inference time,
we report blind VQA task accuracy as a complementary behavioral signal of how
models respond to question-only inputs. We include it as a secondary metric
because it also indicates how strongly models leverage question-side priors,
biases, and shortcuts rather than true visual grounding under blind answering
\citep{chen2024are,lee2024vlind}.

\textbf{Alignment.} For semantic agreement, we compute pairwise
Sentence-BERT cosine similarity \citep{wang2020minilm} between responses,
applied to human--human (HH) pairs (measuring within-group consensus),
human--model (HM) pairs (measuring alignment to human priors), and
model--model pairs. This allows us to compare how closely models approximate
the human prior structure at the per-question level. Because embedding-based
agreement can be mildly affected by response length before cosine comparison,
we use raw SBERT as the primary semantic metric and report truncated-answer
robustness tables in Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}; these are mainly relevant
for more verbose standalone LLM outputs. We additionally report lexical
overlap metrics (chrF, ROUGE-1, exact match) as robustness checks in
Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}. Concretely, for model $m$, question $q$, and
variant $v$, we write
\[
\mathrm{HM}_{m,q,v}=\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h \in H}s(y_{m,q,v},y_{h,q,v}),
\qquad
\mathrm{HH}_{q,v}=\frac{1}{|P_q|}\sum_{(h,h') \in P_q}s(y_{h,q,v},y_{h',q,v}),
\]
where $s(\cdot,\cdot)$ is SBERT cosine similarity and $P_q$ is the set of
human pairs for question $q$. For grouped views such as the HH--HM
scatterplots, we average these scores within operation or entity slices
$g$, e.g.
\[
\overline{\mathrm{HM}}_{m,g}=\frac{1}{|Q_g||V|}\sum_{q \in Q_g}\sum_{v \in V}
\mathrm{HM}_{m,q,v},
\qquad
\overline{\mathrm{HH}}_{g}=\frac{1}{|Q_g||V|}\sum_{q \in Q_g}\sum_{v \in V}
\mathrm{HH}_{q,v},
\]
where $Q_g$ is the question set assigned to slice $g$ and $V$ is the set of
included variants. We then compare these grouped profiles across models and
against the human reference.

\textbf{Distributional alignment.} Beyond answer-level semantic agreement, we
also compare model and human response \emph{patterns}. For a given answer type
$a$ (yes/no, count, or free text) and grouping slice $g$ (operation or
entity), we form pooled empirical distributions
\[
p_H(r \mid a,g), \qquad p_m(r \mid a,g),
\]
over normalized answer categories $r$, pooled across the matched question set
and, in the supplementary grouped tables, across all three variants. We then
compare $p_m$ against $p_H$ using Jensen--Shannon divergence and total
variation distance as the primary structural-alignment metrics, with
chi-square and Cramer's $V$ reported as supplementary mismatch diagnostics.
The supplementary grouped tables additionally report a human leave-one-out
baseline, which compares each participant's grouped distribution against the
pooled distribution of the remaining humans under the same protocol.
% \textbf{Difficulty correlation} is Pearson~$r$ between per-question human
% accuracy and per-model accuracy. \textbf{Krippendorff's $\alpha$} (nominal)
% measures within- and cross-group reliability. \textbf{Token confidence} is
% mean token probability from output logprobs.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} All answers undergo VQA-standard preprocessing before scoring: stripping
articles (\textit{a, an, the}), normalizing punctuation, and converting
common count words (e.g., \textit{three}, \textit{twelve}) to digits for
both human and model responses \citep{antol2015vqa}. Generic answer wrappers
(e.g.,\ \textit{``answer is ...''}) are removed before normalization, and
Korean answers are translated to English and then processed with the same
VQA-style normalization used elsewhere in the paper. These translated and
normalized strings are the inputs to the pairwise human--human and
human--model agreement calculations.

\textbf{Abstention classification.}
We distinguish two abstention types based on the cleaned, normalized answer string.
\emph{Hard abstention}: the output contains an explicit image-reference refusal keyword
(e.g., \textit{``cannot''}, \textit{``no image''}, \textit{``unable to see''}, \textit{``blank''}).
\emph{Soft abstention}: the full normalized answer is a pure epistemic hedge
(\textit{``unknown''}, \textit{``unanswerable''}, \textit{``unclear''}, \textit{``n/a''}, \textit{``indeterminate''}, etc.).
We intentionally exclude \textit{``nothing''} from soft abstention---it is a valid content
answer (e.g., \textit{``What is in the bowl?''} $\to$ \textit{``nothing''})---and
exclude hedged yes/no words (\textit{``maybe''}, \textit{``possibly''}, \textit{``uncertain''}, \textit{``unsure''})
which represent partial committed responses rather than refusals and therefore
appear in the answer distribution alongside human \textit{``other''} responses.
The same classifier is applied uniformly to both model and human answers; human
hard and soft abstention are effectively zero (0.00\% each under this classifier),
so the distinction primarily matters for models.

\textbf{Shared substantive-response evaluation} addresses the confound that
some models abstain while others answer. For this robustness view, we remove
hard and soft abstentions (as defined above), strip generic answer wrappers (e.g.,
\textit{``The answer is''}), apply VQA answer normalization, and recompute
agreement on the remaining substantive responses only. This isolates
content-level agreement from refusal behavior while preserving the paper's
main raw-answer evaluation in the primary results.

\textbf{Length-normalized evaluation} additionally truncates all answers to
$\leq$5 words after preprocessing, controlling for the format mismatch
between terse VLM outputs and verbose standalone LLM
responses. This primarily affects reasoning-mode (think) models
($\Delta\text{SBERT}{=}{+}0.046$) and has negligible impact on VLMs and
backbone decoders ($\Delta\text{SBERT}{<}{+}0.006$); standalone LLMs
without think mode are also minimally affected
($\Delta\text{SBERT}{<}{+}0.02$). We report both raw and
length-normalized tables in the supplement
(Tables~\ref{tab:comprehensive_inst_blind}
and~\ref{tab:comprehensive_inst_blind_w5}) to confirm that group rankings
are preserved regardless of truncation.

\section{Human-Model Blind Alignment}
\label{sec:alignment}

We begin with a basic comparison: when models answer VQA questions
without seeing any image, how closely do their ``imagined'' answers
match those of humans under the same condition? Rather than measuring
accuracy against ground truth---which conflates linguistic prior
exploitation with genuine visual reasoning---we measure \emph{semantic
agreement} between blind model outputs and blind human responses. The
results section proceeds from overall alignment to category-level patterns,
then to image-condition ablations, and finally to robustness analyses.

\subsection{Human Reference Patterns}
\label{sec:human_patterns}

To interpret model-human agreement, we first need the structure of human
blind agreement itself. We therefore measure pairwise human--human (HH)
SBERT per question across all three entity variants
(original $\to$ weaker-object $\to$ pronominalized). Throughout, we use mean pairwise HH
agreement as a human reference ceiling: because humans themselves disagree,
this quantity is below 1 and should be interpreted as a behavioral reference
level rather than a hard upper bound.

Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation} shows the distribution of
per-question HH SBERT by operation type and entity group across
variants. Operation type is the primary axis along which human
agreement varies: a one-way ANOVA of HH SBERT by operation type is
significant in every variant ($F{=}5.75$, $5.93$, $6.60$ for the
original, weaker-object, and pronominalized variants, respectively;
all $p{<}10^{-5}$). This operation-level separation is stable across
formulations (Kruskal-Wallis $H{\approx}41.5$--$41.8$ in both the
original and pronominalized variants), with full per-operation statistics
in Table~\ref{tab:hh_op_stats}. The associated effect sizes are practically
meaningful rather than merely significant, and the same conclusion holds under
robust variance checks (Appendix~\ref{app:chrf_human}). Entity group, by contrast, is never significant
($p{>}0.74$ in every variant).

However, the degradation under entity removal is not uniform. Paired $t$-tests
within each operation type (original vs.\ pronominalized) reveal that identity questions
collapse ($0.534 \to 0.338$, $p{=}0.002$), whereas count questions
remain stable ($0.663 \to 0.671$, $\Delta{=}{+}0.008$, $p{=}0.56$, n.s.)---the
answer space is already narrow and entity removal adds no new ambiguity.
Among entity groups, food shows the largest significant drop ($0.662 \to 0.598$, $p{=}0.031$),
with animal ($0.629{\to}0.576$) and object ($0.593{\to}0.552$) groups showing
borderline drops ($p{\approx}0.06$);
person and other groups are stable ($p{>}0.19$)
(Table~\ref{tab:hh_entity_stats}). The highest-HH questions
in the diagnostic ranking make this concrete: \emph{``How many bottles of
water are on the table?''} (count/object, HH${=}0.783$),
\emph{``Do the horses love each other?''} (action/animal, HH${=}0.770$),
and \emph{``How many hot dogs are on this bun?''} (count/food,
HH${=}0.770$) all lie in operations with tightly constrained answer spaces.
By contrast, the lowest-HH questions cluster in entity- and visually grounded
categories: \emph{``What kind of information does the yellow and white book
look like it contains?''} (identity/other, HH${=}0.183$),
\emph{``What does the shirt say?''} (text/object, HH${=}0.219$), and
\emph{``What does the man's gesture mean?''} (attribute/person,
HH${=}0.226$). These examples match Figure~\ref{fig:human_degradation}:
high-consensus human priors are concentrated in count and some action
questions, while low-consensus cases are disproportionately identity,
text, and person/other-grounded questions that require missing visual detail.
Identity is the clearest operation-level example of this anchor sensitivity in
our human subset. When the noun is preserved, questions such as \emph{``What
animal is this cake meant to represent?''}, \emph{``What type of bear is
this?''}, and \emph{``What type of shoe is that?''} still elicit partial
consensus around a compact set of plausible answers; once the noun is
pronominalized, the same questions spread across multiple candidate referents
and HH agreement drops sharply (Table~\ref{tab:identity_focus_examples}).
This complements the stable count examples above: count often remains compact
because its answer space is already narrow, whereas identity opens into a
broader set of plausible guesses once the lexical anchor is removed.

Human abstention under both classifier definitions is effectively zero:
no responses match the hard abstention keywords and no responses match
the soft abstention word list, yielding 0.00\% hard and 0.00\% soft
abstention across all three variants (C, B, A).
Participants responded with a committed answer in essentially every case,
and this committed-answer rate does not change with question abstraction:
unlike models---where soft and hard abstention measurably rise as
lexical anchors are stripped (see Section~\ref{sec:inst_abstention} and
Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_calibration})---human participants continued
to answer with direct committed responses even when content nouns were fully
pronominalized. Unlike models---where the cleaning pipeline strips refusals
and generic wrappers---human answers pass through essentially unchanged
(0.7\% of HH answer tokens are affected, all by minor VQA normalization).
For human--model (HM) comparison, the shared pipeline ensures that SBERT
scores reflect semantic content rather than format mismatch: model refusals
and preambles are stripped before embedding, so low human--model SBERT
indicates genuine disagreement rather than verbosity artifacts.

These patterns are robust to entity manipulation: per-question HH SBERT
correlates $r{>}0.88$ across all variant pairs (C--A: $r{=}0.889$; C--B: $r{=}0.935$; B--A: $r{=}0.922$), and overall human agreement
decreases only modestly under pronominalization. This gives the reference
point for the model analysis below: humans do lose some agreement when
entity cues are stripped, but the loss is concentrated in a few operations
rather than being global.
Within the human reference itself, identity shows the clearest observed
degradation, while count provides the most stable contrast case.

\subsection{Instruction Effect on Model Abstentions}
\label{sec:inst_abstention}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{merged_abstention_vC_q113}
\caption{Blind vs.\ instruction-aware abstention rates (soft and hard combined)
across model groups (variant~C, $q{=}113$).
Circles~=~soft abstention; squares~=~hard abstention; hollow~=~blind; filled~=~instruction-aware.
Think-mode standalone LLMs show the largest instruction-driven hard-abstention increases;
VLMs and backbone decoders remain near zero throughout.
Extended version with all 37 models on 1{,}000 questions in Figure~\ref{fig:merged_abstention}.}
\label{fig:inst_abstention}
\end{figure*}

While human abstention is effectively zero (0.00\% hard and soft across all
variants under the paper's classifier), models respond to the blind-awareness
instruction very differently depending on architecture.
Figure~\ref{fig:inst_abstention} shows the change in hard and soft abstention between
the blind and instruction-aware conditions.
VLMs and backbone decoders remain near zero abstention in both conditions ($<$2\%
for most): the instruction does not trigger refusals in models that already commit to an
answer when given a blank image.
Standalone LLMs (no-think) span a wider range: Mistral-7B drops from ${\sim}43\%$
hard abstention to ${\sim}33\%$ under instruction, while Qwen3 models cluster near 0--10\%.
The sharpest effect appears in think-mode models: Qwen3-4B (think) jumps from near-zero
to ${\sim}43\%$ hard abstention under instruction, and Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-32B (think)
also shift substantially. Chain-of-thought reasoning interacts strongly with the
``imagine a scenario'' framing and triggers hedging behavior that suppresses committed answers.

\paragraph{Abstention across control variants.}
Beyond the instruction effect, abstention also varies across the three question
formulations (C$\to$B$\to$A). In contrast to humans---whose abstention stays at
0.00\% regardless of variant---models show a systematic increase in soft abstention as
content nouns are replaced by pronouns. The Qwen3-VL family shows the clearest trend
among VLMs (8B: C$=$1.4\%, B$=$3.2\%, A$=$3.2\%; 4B: C$=$0.7\%, B$=$1.8\%, A$=$3.0\%),
and this pattern carries through to backbone decoders
(Qwen3-VL-8B decoder: C$=$0.4\%, B$=$1.0\%, A$=$2.4\%;
LLaVA-Mistral decoder: C$=$0.3\%, B$=$0.5\%, A$=$0.8\%).
Hard abstention shows a qualitatively different pattern:
Mistral-7B---whose refusals are explicitly entity-triggered
(``I cannot see the \{object\}'')---actually \emph{decreases} from C to A
(C$=$29.8\%, B$=$27.0\%, A$=$22.7\%), because pronominalized questions remove the
noun that triggers the refusal phrase.
Qwen3-VL-32B represents the opposite extreme: its hard abstention more than doubles
from C to A (C$=$13.5\%, B$=$15.2\%, A$=$24.1\%), suggesting the 32B model defers more
when the question becomes semantically underspecified.
Think-mode models amplify all of these trends (Qwen3-1.7B think:
C$=$49.1\%, B$=$50.7\%, A$=$59.1\%), confirming that chain-of-thought reasoning
magnifies sensitivity to question underspecification.

This instruction sensitivity extends to answer content beyond abstention:
standalone LLMs change 25--96\% of answers between conditions, compared to
12--42\% for VLMs and backbone decoders, with think models most sensitive (60--91\%).
Despite this large behavioral shift, instruction changes accuracy by only a few points
on average, confirming it reroutes the model's language prior rather than
unlocking latent visual knowledge.

Beyond aggregate agreement, three systematic distributional biases emerge across

\textbf{No-bias} in yes/no: full VLMs default to a narrower yes/no prior that

\textbf{Zero-bias} in count: full VLMs collapse toward a small set of numeric

\textbf{Abstention collapse} in standalone LLMs: they often fail to occupy the

At the grouped level, these patterns are stable across operation and entity categories

strongest on operation-grouped yes/no structure, while InternVL-8B best reproduces
the human small-number count distribution. Text-category slices are more heterogeneous,
mirroring the broad lexical diversity of low-HH identity and text questions
discussed above.

\paragraph{Group-level summary.}
We compute pairwise SBERT across questions within the human subset for all models
(Mistral-7B and Phi-3.5-mini excluded from scale plots due to degenerate outputs;
see Appendix~\ref{app:scale_variant}).
The human--human ceiling is $\overline{\text{SBERT}}_{\text{HH}} = 0.577$.
Backbone decoders reach $0.573$---within $0.004$ of the ceiling---while full VLMs score
$0.543$ and standalone LLMs $0.433$.
Answer length varies across groups (VLMs and backbone decoders: median 1 word;
standalone LLMs: mean 6.9 words, Mistral-7B: 29.7 words);
raw SBERT is used as the primary metric with length-normalized alternatives as
supplementary robustness checks (Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}).

Figure~\ref{fig:scatter_sbert} plots HH vs.\ HM SBERT across operation $\times$ entity
groups for all three model classes.
The backbone decoder column consistently sits \emph{above} the VLM column despite sharing
identical weights, confirming that blind-image processing introduces systematic bias
not present in the pure language prior.
Standalone LLMs cluster furthest below the diagonal; think-mode models sit lower than
their no-think counterparts at the same scale.
High blind accuracy co-occurs with a \emph{high} SBERT gap: VLMs outperform humans
by $+16$ pp on average, backbone decoders by $+11$ pp.
In the blind setting this is prior exploitation---not reasoning---so high blind accuracy
signals convergence on benchmark-favored shortcuts rather than faithful visual grounding.
The strongest human--model SBERT comes from InternVL-8B ($0.598$), with backbone
decoders clustered close behind; the highest blind-accuracy models (LLaVA-Mistral,
LLaVA-1.5 LM, all at $0.537$) do not lead on alignment.

\paragraph{Scale effects.}
Figure~\ref{fig:scale} separates group from scale.
On accuracy, VLMs show a \emph{negative} size trend while backbone decoders are flat;
standalone LLMs follow a strong positive trend (${\approx}7.7\%$ at 0.6B to
${\approx}33\%$ at 32B) but stay near or below the human baseline (${\approx}27\%$)
until 32B.
On SBERT, backbone decoders improve modestly with scale (${\approx}0.56$ at 2B
$\to$ ${\approx}0.57$--$0.59$ at 7--13B), approaching the HH ceiling;
full VLMs are flat or slightly negative---a 1B backbone decoder already exceeds 7--8B
full VLMs despite far lower accuracy.
Standalone LLMs (no-think) improve steeply with scale (${\approx}0.33$ at 0.6B
$\to$ ${\approx}0.50$ at 32B) but plateau below backbone decoders.
\emph{Group membership predicts alignment better than size}: a 1B backbone decoder
exceeds a 7B standalone LLM on SBERT.

\paragraph{Control-variant degradation.}
VLM and backbone accuracy drop 11--13 pp from the original to the pronominalized variant;
human accuracy barely changes. The per-variant degradation ladder
(Figure~\ref{fig:variants}, Appendix~\ref{app:degradation}) shows the full C$\to$B$\to$A
trajectory: backbone decoders stay nearest the human ceiling throughout, standalone LLMs
degrade most steeply, and the scale $\times$ variant interaction shows larger models can
become \emph{more} anchor-sensitive rather than less (Appendix~\ref{app:scale_variant}).

\paragraph{Chain-of-thought thinking.}
Enabling Qwen3 thinking mode interacts with scale in a non-monotonic way.
At 4B and 8B, models engage consistently (empty-think rate ${\leq}8\%$) and allocate
\emph{more} reasoning to harder pronominalized questions ($389{\to}478$ and $439{\to}505$
words from variant C to A).
At 1.7B and 32B, models disengage on the majority of questions ($52$--$66\%$ empty-think
rate), with the 32B model apparently short-cutting when the answer manifold is obvious.
Despite the apparent effort at 4B and 8B, think-chain length \emph{negatively}
correlates with HM SBERT on the same question (4B variant B: $r{=}{-}0.26$,
$p{=}0.008$; 8B variant C: $r{=}{-}0.28$, $p{=}0.004$)---more deliberation
is associated with lower human alignment, not higher.
Only Qwen3-1.7B shows a reliable alignment gain from thinking mode
($\Delta\text{SBERT}{=}{+}0.12$, $p{<}0.001$; Figure~\ref{fig:think_nothink}).
Full think EDA is in Appendix~\ref{app:think}.

\subsection{Confidence and Calibration}
\label{sec:confidence}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{confidence/distribution_human_vs_model}\\[0.4em]
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{confidence/confidence_alignment_by_variant}
\caption{Top: distribution of human self-reported confidence (normalized Likert, 1--5)
vs.\ mean model token probability, by group.
Models are severely overconfident relative to humans in the blind setting.
Bottom: per-question mean human confidence vs.\ mean model token probability across variants.

\label{fig:confidence}
\end{figure}

Human participants self-reported confidence on a 1--5 Likert scale for each response.
Normalized to $[0,1]$, per-participant means range from $0.30$ to $0.94$, with a group
mean well below ceiling (Appendix~\ref{app:human}).
Model token probability---the natural analogue of confidence---is substantially higher:
mean token probability is ${\approx}0.90$ for VLMs and backbone decoders
and ${\approx}0.80$--$0.88$ for standalone LLMs, even on questions with no image present.
Figure~\ref{fig:confidence} (top) shows this gap directly: models are clustered at
high-confidence bins that are nearly empty in the human distribution.

This overconfidence is not diagnostic of alignment. Backbone decoders and VLMs have
similar token-probability profiles yet differ substantially in HM SBERT (0.573 vs.\ 0.543);
standalone LLMs have slightly lower token probabilities but the lowest SBERT.
Figure~\ref{fig:confidence} (bottom) confirms that per-question model confidence
does not track human confidence across variants: the two signals are largely orthogonal,
suggesting model token probability measures ``commitment to the dominant token'' rather
than ``awareness that this question has a well-known answer.''

For think-mode models, confidence is additionally decoupled across phases:
the mean log-probability of thinking tokens is essentially uncorrelated with the
log-probability of the final answer token ($r{=}0.08$--$0.23$ across scales).
A model can reason extensively and commit to a single-token answer with equal certainty,
or skip reasoning and answer with the same high probability.
The thinking process does not calibrate the model's certainty in this blind-VQA setting.
Reliability diagrams and ECE values are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_calibration}.

\section{Image-Condition Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

The backbone decoder comparison raises a related question: is the blank image
simply an adversarial input that misleads the vision encoder, or does the
encoder fundamentally lack a neutral mode? If the former, a different
uninformative image might recover alignment; if the latter, backbone
decoders should outperform VLMs under \emph{any} uninformative visual input.

We test this with uninformative image conditions---blank, uniform gray, random
noise, and uniform white---on representative VLMs from two families
(Table~\ref{tab:image_ablation_main}). The main result is that once the vision
pathway is active, uninformative images perturb blind priors rather than
recovering a neutral no-vision regime. For Qwen3-VL-8B, moving from blank to
gray to noise lowers both blind accuracy and human alignment: accuracy falls
from 0.478 to 0.277, human--model SBERT from 0.541 to 0.399, and human--model
chrF from 0.215 to 0.136. LLaVA-Mistral is more stable overall, but still drops
under noise, showing that the effect is not unique to one family. The backbone
decoder is not shown as another table row because it has no image pathway at
all; it serves as the no-vision reference. Under every uninformative image
condition, the full VLM remains below that decoder-level alignment, and moving
from blank to gray or noise increases answer instability rather than restoring
the backbone behavior. Even when white slightly outperforms blank for
Qwen3-VL-8B on human--model metrics, the overall pattern remains image-condition
sensitive rather than converging to a decoder-like blind mode. We return to
related within-family heterogeneity in the later scale analysis.

Taken together, these results reframe the backbone decoder paradox:
vision co-training embeds useful visual-linguistic associations in the
language backbone, but the vision encoder itself has no mechanism to signal
\emph{``I have nothing useful to contribute.''} It always contributes,
and on uninformative input that contribution is noise that pushes the
language model away from the human-like linguistic prior it would otherwise
express. This explains why the backbone decoder---free from this
modulation---produces the most human-aligned blind responses.
Among the tested full VLMs, LLaVA-Mistral is the most stable across
uninformative image conditions, whereas Qwen3-VL-8B is the most
image-sensitive; neither family recovers the decoder-level reference.

\section{Confound Controls}
\label{sec:confounds}

We verify that the agreement patterns above are not measurement artifacts
of abstention or answer format. Across all checks, the central ordering
(backbone $>$ VLM $>$ standalone LLM) is preserved.

Adding the blind-awareness instruction substantially increases human--model agreement
(${\approx}{+}0.09$ SBERT on average; VLM ${+}0.097$, backbone ${+}0.103$,
standalone LLM ${+}0.065$), with the largest gains on the original
variant and diminishing returns as entity cues are removed
(Appendix~\ref{app:inst}). Instruction framing substantially shifts
model outputs but does not overturn the architectural ordering
or remove the backbone advantage.

\subsection{Abstention Effects}

Hard abstentions (explicit refusals) range from 0--18\% across VLMs
and 0--5\% across backbone decoders, with outliers at Qwen3-VL-32B
(17.7\%). Soft abstention---implicit
hedges such as ``unknown'' or ``nothing''---is 0--11\% for VLMs and
0--3\% for backbones; standalone LLMs show 0--14\% soft and up to
30\% hard (Mistral-7B)
(Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_calibration}).
The shared substantive-response comparison restricts to questions on which
models produced non-abstaining answers. human--model SBERT increases modestly under
filtering, but the backbone--VLM--standalone ordering is preserved,
showing that the main pattern is not an artifact of abstention-heavy items.
After these controls, the backbone-decoder group still remains closest to
human answers, indicating that the main alignment result is not a refusal or
formatting artifact.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{The vision encoder has no neutral mode.}
Our central finding is that removing the vision encoder from a VLM
\emph{improves} blind human-model alignment. Image-condition ablations
confirm this is not a blank-image artifact: the vision encoder actively
modulates outputs on any uninformative image, and no condition recovers
backbone-level alignment. Vision co-training embeds human-like
linguistic priors in the language backbone, but the encoder itself
introduces distributional biases that push answers away from those priors
when no meaningful visual content is present. This has practical
implications: blind VQA diagnosis should test backbone decoders
separately, and VLM architectures may benefit from a learned
``visual uncertainty'' signal or explicit gating mechanism that suppresses
encoder influence when input is uninformative.

\paragraph{An information-theoretic view.}
The backbone decoder computes $P(a \mid q)$ from the question alone---a
distribution shaped by the co-occurrence statistics of VQA fine-tuning
data, which closely mirrors human linguistic priors. The VLM computes
$P(a \mid q, v_0)$, where $v_0$ is the vision encoder's output for an
uninformative image. Critically, $v_0 \neq \mathbf{0}$: the encoder maps
any input---blank, gray, noise---to a non-trivial embedding that
carries its own distributional biases. This embedding acts as a
nuisance variable that shifts the output distribution away from
$P(a \mid q)$ without adding information.
Per-question entropy analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:case_study}) provides
three confirmations. First, human--model alignment correlates strongly
with shared uncertainty ($r = -0.82$ between human answer entropy and
human--model SBERT): questions where humans agree are exactly the questions where
models align with humans. Second, models show systematically higher
consensus than humans---their answer entropy falls below the human
diagonal on the majority of questions, with the gap largest on
entity-grounded categories (identity, attribute) where the encoder's
``default image'' representation activates spurious visual associations.
Third, entropy mismatch is orthogonal to lexical-anchor dependence
($r = 0.12$, n.s.), ruling out answer consensus as a confound for the
control-variant findings: the mechanism by which $v_0$ distorts
$P(a \mid q)$ is separate from the mechanism by which content nouns
activate specific priors. In this sense, the entropy mismatch is not just a
descriptive metric: it indicates a qualitative divergence in uncertainty
behavior. Humans manage underspecification through heuristics that preserve
plural plausible answers, whereas models often collapse that uncertainty into
a narrower shortcut answer.

\paragraph{Lexical anchors as prior activators.}
The control-variant degradation chain reveals that blind model success is
predominantly carried by content-noun lexical cues. When these are
stripped, models diverge from human priors while humans barely change.
This identifies a concrete mechanism---lexically anchored prior recall---that
benchmark design and debiasing interventions can explicitly target.
We note that benchmark contamination may amplify this effect:
\citet{chen2024are} show that VLMs can achieve non-trivial scores on
multimodal benchmarks without visual input, partly due to training-set
leakage. Our control-variant design partially disentangles the two
sources---pronominalized questions remove the lexical cues that
memorization would exploit, yet models still retain above-chance
performance, suggesting that corpus-level priors contribute beyond
direct memorization. A stronger mechanistic test would relate shortcut answer
frequencies directly to pretraining or fine-tuning corpus statistics; we view
that correlation analysis as a natural next step for strengthening the prior
account.

\paragraph{Linguistic priors as structured imagination.}
Our results reveal something beyond shortcut behavior: when instructed to
\textit{``imagine an appropriate image exists and answer based on the most
common scenario,''} models do not simply retrieve memorized answers---they
produce responses that are statistically coherent with the human prior on
the same questions. Backbone decoders achieve near-human semantic agreement
($r \approx 0.57$--$0.72$ on per-question difficulty) without any visual
input, indicating that language models have internalized structured world
knowledge that functions as a form of visual imagination. This connects to
broader questions about AI creativity and grounded cognition: a model that
correctly answers ``What color is the sky?'' blindly is not merely
pattern-matching---it is drawing on a learned prior that encodes the
statistical regularities of the visual world as represented in language.
The gap between backbone decoders and full VLMs further suggests that
this linguistic imagination is \emph{disrupted} rather than enhanced by
the vision encoder in the absence of informative visual input, implying
that multimodal training introduces a form of sensory interference when
images carry no task-relevant signal. More broadly, our findings raise the
question of whether ``visual grounding'' in current VLMs is as much about
activating language-embedded world knowledge as it is about extracting
information from pixels---a distinction that has direct implications for
interpretability and for the design of more robust visually grounded systems.

\paragraph{Implications for benchmark design.}
Any VQA benchmark can be partially solved by linguistic priors, and the
magnitude varies predictably with question type and lexical anchoring.
We propose that blind VQA evaluation become a standard diagnostic: blind
accuracy above 40\% signals over-indexing on knowledge-accessible
question types.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Human participants were recruited from university communities in
Korean/English contexts, limiting demographic generalizability. Model
evaluation uses greedy decoding (temperature~0), which may underestimate
answer diversity. Human data was collected under the explicit blind-awareness instruction only.
Image-condition ablations cover five VLMs from all three families
but use only four uninformative image types; a broader stimulus set
(e.g., task-irrelevant natural images) could further characterize encoder behavior.
A direct corpus-frequency correlation would strengthen the mechanistic
account \citep{mccoy2024embers}; specifically, future work should test whether
shortcut answer frequencies track corpus-level answer statistics for the same
question forms.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

We present a human-calibrated diagnostic study of linguistic prior
exploitation in Vision-Language Models. The central finding is
architectural: backbone decoders---the same VLM weights with the vision
encoder bypassed---achieve near-human semantic agreement (HM SBERT $0.573$,
compared with a human--human reference level of $0.577$, $\Delta{=}0.004$) while full VLMs
score lower ($0.543$) and standalone LLMs diverge further ($0.433$). Image-condition
ablations show this is not a
blank-image artifact: the vision encoder has no neutral mode and actively
distorts the backbone's linguistic prior on any uninformative input.

Control question variants show that blind model success is carried by
content-noun lexical anchors: as these are stripped, model--human
agreement drops monotonically while the human ceiling barely moves. The
resulting pronominalization test offers a simple diagnostic for separating
lexically anchored shortcut behavior from more robust visual grounding. The
pattern is stable across scales and robust to confound controls
(abstention filtering, length normalization, instruction effects).
Question-type
decomposition identifies where alignment holds (yes/no, action) and
where it breaks (count, grounded reference), providing a diagnostic
vocabulary for linguistic prior exploitation in any multimodal benchmark.

\section{Human Study Protocol}
\label{app:human}

A total of 40 participants were included. Participants were recruited via
online student community forums at universities. All procedures were conducted
under an IRB-approved protocol adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki.
Informed consent was obtained prior to participation.

Participants answered text-only explicit no-image blind-instruction questions
across the original, weaker-object, and pronominalized formulations. The
analysis applies a raw-response qualification threshold
($\geq$348) before subset intersection, then restricts to the question IDs
answered by all qualifying participants across all three reformulations
(339 question--variant pairs per participant).
Participants were compensated at 10,000~KRW per hour. Responses in Korean or
English were translated to English and processed with the same VQA-style
normalization described in Section~\ref{sec:eval_protocols}.

As a supplementary human-only diagnostic, we also examine the relationship
between per-question answer entropy and human VQA accuracy. Across variants,
the two are strongly negatively correlated ($r\approx -0.77$), indicating that
questions eliciting more diverse human blind answers are also the questions on
which humans are less likely to match VQA ground truth. We treat this as a
difficulty/ambiguity check rather than a primary result, since the main paper
focuses on semantic agreement and anchor sensitivity rather than accuracy.

\subsection{Participant Response Statistics}
\label{app:human_stats}

Figure~\ref{fig:human_stats} summarizes response-level and participant-level
statistics for the 40-participant study (15,336 total responses across three
variants).

\paragraph{Response time.}
The median response time is 4.1~s (mean 5.6~s, IQR 2.8--6.3~s after capping
at the 99th percentile of 35.8~s to exclude rare mid-session pauses).
Per-participant median RTs range from 2.6~s to 7.5~s with no extreme outliers,
indicating consistent engagement across all participants
(Figure~\ref{fig:human_stats}A--B).

\paragraph{Answer length.}
Responses are concise: 92.6\% of answers consist of a single word, with a
mean of 1.11 words (Figure~\ref{fig:human_stats}C). This is consistent with
the VQA-style instruction asking for short, direct answers and confirms that
participants were not providing free-form explanations.

\paragraph{Abstention.}
Hard abstention was 0.00\% and soft abstention was 0.00\% across all three
variants under the paper's classifier (exact match against the soft-abstention
word list on the normalized answer; Section~\ref{sec:eval_protocols},
Appendix~\ref{app:abstention_classifier}).
This rate does not increase with question abstraction: committed answers are
invariant across the original (C), weaker (B), and pronominalized (A)
variants (Figure~\ref{fig:human_stats}E), which sharply contrasts with
models---where soft abstention rises as lexical anchors are removed
(e.g., Qwen3-VL-8B: 1.4\%$\to$3.2\% C$\to$A; its backbone decoder:
0.4\%$\to$2.4\%). This confirms that the blind-instruction framing
successfully elicited prior-driven answers from all participants regardless
of question specificity.

\paragraph{Confidence ratings.}
Participants used the full 1--5 Likert scale with a mean of 3.07 ($\sigma =
1.26$) and an approximately uniform distribution across levels
(Figure~\ref{fig:human_stats}D). Confidence distributions are nearly
identical across the three variants, suggesting that question surface form does
not systematically inflate or suppress self-reported certainty.
Per-participant mean confidence ranges from 1.5 to 4.7, indicating
genuine heterogeneity in individual certainty levels rather than a ceiling or
floor effect (Figure~\ref{fig:human_stats}F).

\paragraph{Response time--confidence relationship.}
As a convergent validity check, we correlated log response time with
confidence rating per participant. The mean per-participant Pearson $r$ is
$-0.067$ ($t = -2.68$, $p = 0.011$, one-sample $t$-test vs.\ 0), confirming
a small but reliable tendency for participants to respond faster when they are
more confident. The effect is consistent across all three variants
($r_C = -0.082$, $r_B = -0.089$, $r_A = -0.116$, all $p < .001$) and mean
RT decreases monotonically from 6.2~s at confidence level~1 to 4.8~s at
level~5 (Figure~\ref{fig:rt_conf}). Together, these results support the
validity of the self-reported confidence scale as a behavioral measure.

\section{Answer Aggregation}
\label{app:aggregation}

We cluster semantically equivalent answers (e.g., ``3'' and ``three'') using
GPT-4o mini \citep{openai2025gpt4o}. Human responses are aggregated into a
confidence-weighted distribution:
\begin{equation}
H(a_k^*) = \frac{\sum_{i:\phi(a_i)=a_k^*} c_i}{\sum_i c_i}
\end{equation}
where $c_i$ is participant $i$'s Likert confidence (normalized to $[0,1]$)
and $\phi$ maps raw answers to canonical forms.

\section{Control Question Extraction Prompt}
\label{app:ctl}

Control variants are extracted using GPT-4.1-mini-2025-04-14 (temperature~0,
seed~42, JSON schema mode) in batches of 50 questions per API call.
The output schema enforces
\texttt{\{deictic\_removed, object\_removed, weaker\_object, pronominalized\}}
as required string fields with \texttt{additionalProperties:~false}.
The system prompt is reproduced verbatim below:

\begin{quote}\small
Generate 4 control variants for each VQA question.
Return ONLY a JSON object \texttt{\{"results":[...]\}}; preserve input order.

\textbf{Rules:}

1) \textit{deictic\_removed}: Remove deictic words (this/that/these/those/here/there)
and spatial markers (left/right/top/bottom/foreground/background) while preserving
grammar. Restructure the sentence if needed to remain fluent.

2) \textit{object\_removed}: Replace the specific object noun with neutral placeholder
``object''. Do not replace pronouns or generic nouns already present.

3) \textit{weaker\_object}: Replace the specific object noun with a broader hypernym
one level up (dog$\to$animal, car$\to$vehicle, drink$\to$beverage, shirt$\to$clothing,
apple$\to$fruit, knife$\to$utensil, sofa$\to$furniture). If no clear hypernym
exists, use ``object''.

4) \textit{pronominalized}: Replace ALL content noun phrases with minimal pronouns.
Grammar-aware rules: attribute (``What color is the dog?'') $\to$ ``What color is it?'';
count (``How many cats are there?'') $\to$ ``How many are there?'';
location (``Where is the phone?'') $\to$ ``Where is it?'';
action (``What is the man doing?'') $\to$ ``What is it doing?'';
yes/no (``Is the dog affectionate?'') $\to$ ``Is it affectionate?'';
multi-NP (``between the girl and the giraffe'') $\to$ ``between them''.
Keep ONLY question words, copula, prepositions, predicate/attribute, and pronouns.
Use ``it''/``they''/``them''---never ``the object''.
\end{quote}

\section{ChrF Agreement by Operation Type (Human--Human)}
\label{app:chrf_human}

ChrF measures character $n$-gram overlap and captures surface-level
lexical similarity rather than semantic similarity. Unlike SBERT, HH
chrF shows no significant operation-type effect in any variant
(ANOVA $F{=}0.83$, $p{=}0.59$ for the original formulation; $F{=}1.08$, $p{=}0.39$
for the weaker-object formulation; $F{=}0.98$, $p{=}0.46$ for the pronominalized formulation). Entity group is also
non-significant across all variants ($p{>}0.36$). This null result
confirms that the operation-type structure found in HH SBERT
(Section~\ref{sec:human_patterns}) is semantic: humans answering the
same question type give \emph{semantically} similar responses (captured
by SBERT) even when they use different surface forms (missed by chrF).
For the ANOVA-style comparisons reported in the paper, we checked the
homogeneity-of-variance assumption and also verified that Welch-style
variance-robust tests lead to the same qualitative conclusions. We therefore
report the standard ANOVA statistics in the main text for readability, while
interpreting them together with practical effect-size estimates ($\eta^2$)
rather than $p$-values alone.

\section{Blind Awareness Instruction Effect}
\label{app:inst}

The explicit blind-awareness instruction was: \textit{``Note: No images are provided. For each
question, imagine an appropriate image exists and answer based on the most
common or universal scenario.''}

\paragraph{Effect on abstention.}
Figure~\ref{fig:merged_abstention} shows blind vs.\ instruction-aware
abstention rates (soft and hard combined) for all 37 models (variant~C,
1{,}000 questions):
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=1em]
  \item \textbf{VLMs} have near-zero abstention in both conditions
  ($<$2\%), with instruction having negligible effect.
  \item \textbf{VLM backbone decoders} similarly show $<$5\% abstention;
  InternVL backbones decrease slightly when instruction is added.
  \item \textbf{Standalone LLMs (nothink)} span a wide range:
  Mistral-7B drops from ${\sim}$43\% hard abstention to ${\sim}$33\%
  under instruction (still the highest overall); Qwen3 models cluster near 0--10\%.
  \item \textbf{Standalone LLMs (think)} show the largest instruction
  sensitivity: Qwen3-4B (think) jumps from near-zero to ${\sim}$43\%
  hard abstention under instruction; Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-32B (think)
  also shift substantially. Chain-of-thought reasoning interacts strongly
  with the ``imagine a scenario'' framing and triggers hedging behavior.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Effect on response content.}
Figure~\ref{fig:response_change} shows the fraction of answers that change
between blind and instruction-aware conditions.
Instruction is a far stronger behavioral perturbation for standalone LLMs
(25--96\% of answers change) than for VLMs (12--42\%) or backbone
decoders (20--42\%). Think models are most sensitive (60--91\% change),
suggesting their chain-of-thought process is highly responsive to
framing. This large behavioral shift without corresponding accuracy gains
confirms that instruction reroutes the model's language prior rather than
unlocking latent visual knowledge.

\paragraph{Effect on accuracy and agreement.}
Instruction changes accuracy by only a few points on average, confirming
that it does not reliably improve task performance even when it
substantially changes answer behavior. Human--model SBERT agreement
increases by ${\approx}{+}0.09$ on average (VLM $+$0.10, backbone $+$0.10,
standalone LLM $+$0.07), with the largest gains on
variant~C and diminishing returns on variant~A, consistent with
instruction acting primarily as a lexical-anchor activator rather than a
general alignment mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:confounds}).

\section{Abstention and Calibration Ablations}
\label{app:abstention_calibration}

This section presents robustness analyses for the blind
setting: abstention behavior and instruction-induced abstention collapse.
Together they clarify that the backbone
advantage is not an artifact of refusal behavior.

\subsection{Abstention Classifier Details}
\label{app:abstention_classifier}

\paragraph{Hard abstention} is detected via a regex match on the raw output for
explicit image-reference refusal phrases: \texttt{cannot}, \texttt{can't},
\texttt{don't see}, \texttt{no image}, \texttt{unable}, \texttt{not visible},
\texttt{no visual}, \texttt{i'm not able}, and \texttt{\textbackslash bblank\textbackslash b}.

\paragraph{Soft abstention} is detected by exact match on the full normalized answer
against a fixed word list: \{\textit{unknown, unanswerable, unclear, nowhere, n/a, na,
indeterminate, unidentifiable, no sign, can't tell, cannot tell, not clear, not visible}\}.

\paragraph{Design choices.}
Two categories are deliberately excluded from soft abstention:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\textit{``nothing''}}: a valid content answer for questions about
    empty scenes or absent objects (e.g., \textit{``What is in the bowl?''} $\to$
    \textit{``nothing''}). Treating it as abstention would remove meaningful
    negative-evidence responses from the distribution.
  \item \textbf{Hedged yes/no words} (\textit{maybe, possibly, perhaps, uncertain,
    unsure}): these represent partial committed responses to yes/no questions rather
    than refusals. They fall into the \textit{other} category in the answer
    distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:dist}), consistent with how human responses are
    categorized, enabling a fair distributional comparison.
\end{itemize}
The same classifier is applied uniformly to both model and human answers.
Human hard abstention is 0.00\% and soft abstention is 0.00\% across all
three variants---the abstention distinction is therefore a model-side
phenomenon. Crucially, human abstention is invariant to question abstraction
(0.00\% in variants C, B, and A), whereas model abstention tends to rise as
lexical anchors are removed (Section~\ref{app:human_stats}).

\subsection{Instruction Gating Effect}
\label{app:inst_gating}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{merged_abstention_vC_q113}
\caption{Soft and hard abstention rates (blind $\to$ explicit blind-awareness instruction) per model.
Standalone LLMs show large shifts; VLMs/backbones are nearly unchanged.}
\label{fig:abstention_shift_models}
\end{figure}

For reference, human abstention is effectively zero (0.00\% hard and soft
under the paper's classifier), so the abstention patterns below are a
model-side phenomenon rather than a general property of blind answering.

Instruction (blind $\to$ blind with awareness instruction) increases human--model agreement most on
original questions ($+$0.020 SBERT) and attenuates as lexical anchors are
removed (pronominalized: $+$0.012). The behavioral mechanism is abstention
collapse: standalone LLMs shift from $\sim$30\% soft abstention (epistemic hedges
such as \textit{``unknown''} or \textit{``unanswerable''}) to near
zero (Figure~\ref{fig:abstention_shift_models}), while VLMs and backbones barely
change---indicating their priors are expressed unconditionally. Accuracy
does not improve (Appendix~\ref{app:inst}), confirming that instruction
primarily converts refusal into committed hallucination rather than grounded
reasoning.

\section{Chain-of-Thought Thinking Analysis}
\label{app:think}

We tested Qwen3 standalone LLMs at five scales (0.6B--32B) with thinking mode
enabled, which prepends an explicit reasoning chain inside
$\langle$\textit{think}$\rangle$ tags before the final answer token.
The analysis covers think-chain length, engagement rate, and its relationship
to human question difficulty and human--model alignment.

\paragraph{Scale determines whether models engage.}
Qwen3-4B and 8B almost always generate a reasoning chain (empty-think rate
${\leq}8\%$), while Qwen3-1.7B and 32B skip thinking on the majority of
questions ($51$--$66\%$; Figure~\ref{fig:think_variant}, bottom).
This suggests a capacity threshold: 1.7B cannot sustain coherent reasoning
under pure textual uncertainty, while 32B appears to have learned to shortcut
the reasoning step when the answer manifold is obvious from the question alone.

\paragraph{Mid-scale models allocate more reasoning to underspecified questions.}
For Qwen3-4B and 8B, mean think-chain length \emph{increases} from the
original to the pronominalized variant ($389{\to}478$ and $439{\to}505$ words;
Figure~\ref{fig:think_variant}, top). These models detect added ambiguity and
deliberate more. By contrast, Qwen3-1.7B and 32B disengage further under
pronominalization: the empty-think rate rises from $51.7\%{\to}71.6\%$ for 1.7B
and $60\%{\to}66\%$ for 32B, meaning they give up reasoning exactly when
questions are hardest.

\paragraph{Thinking does not improve human--model alignment.}
Enabling thinking mode provides no consistent gain in HM SBERT: only
Qwen3-1.7B shows a reliable improvement ($\Delta\text{SBERT}{=}{+}0.12$,
$p{<}0.001$ for the original variant; Figure~\ref{fig:think_nothink}).
All other scales yield $\Delta{\approx}0$ or small negatives.
Furthermore, within 4B and 8B, think-chain length \emph{negatively} correlates
with HM SBERT on the same question (4B variant B: $r{=}{-}0.26$, $p{=}0.008$;
8B variant C: $r{=}{-}0.28$, $p{=}0.004$): more deliberation is associated
with \emph{lower} human alignment, not higher. This suggests the additional
tokens reflect exploratory wandering rather than calibrated prior recall.

\paragraph{Thinking and answering are decoupled.}
The mean log-probability of thinking tokens is essentially uncorrelated with
the log-probability of the final answer token ($r{=}0.08$ to $0.23$ across
scales). A model can reason extensively and still produce a highly confident
single-token answer, or skip reasoning entirely and answer with equal
confidence. The reasoning process does not modulate the answer's certainty,
which suggests thinking tokens in this blind-VQA setting function more as
exploratory search than as confidence calibration.
