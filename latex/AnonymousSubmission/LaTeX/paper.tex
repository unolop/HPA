\begin{abstract}
Analogous to hypothesis-only baselines that exposed annotation artifacts in
Natural Language Inference \citep{poliak2018hypothesis}, we investigate how
much of VQA benchmark performance is achievable without any visual information.
Across multiple open-source Vision-Language Models (VLMs), blind inference with
a blank image still yields substantial VQA accuracy, often exceeding human blind
performance, indicating strong exploitation of linguistic priors shaped by the
training distribution \citep{mccoy2024embers}. To characterize this behavior,
we introduce \emph{control question variants}: four progressively weaker
reformulations of each question that remove lexical anchors while preserving
question type. We further analyze how blind model behavior changes under a
single additional instruction, revealing a spectrum from
\emph{instruction-gated} prior use to more unconditional hallucination. Blind
outputs are also highly overconfident: for several models, 80--90\% of outputs
have logprob $>-0.5$, a calibration failure invisible to accuracy-only
evaluations. Our human dataset (N=24, 641 questions, IRB-approved) provides a
reference point for blind reasoning without corpus-frequency bias. Preliminary
alignment experiments suggest that human blind responses may provide a useful
supervision signal, though robust cross-validated estimates remain future work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) achieve impressive performance on visual question
answering (VQA) benchmarks \citep{antol2015vqa,goyal2016making}, yet a basic
question remains unanswered: how much of this performance reflects genuine
visual grounding, and how much can be explained by linguistic priors inherited
from large-scale language model pretraining?

This question parallels a well-known episode in Natural Language Inference
(NLI): \citet{poliak2018hypothesis} showed that a model trained on hypotheses
alone---ignoring the corresponding premises---substantially exceeded the random
baseline across ten NLI datasets. The finding revealed annotation artifacts
embedded in the benchmarks themselves. We apply the same diagnostic logic to
VQA: the \emph{image} plays the role of the premise, and the question is the
hypothesis. A model that answers correctly from the question alone is exploiting
a hypothesis-only shortcut.

\citet{mccoy2024embers} provide the mechanistic explanation: large language
models are shaped by their training objective over internet-scale text, and
therefore produce outputs proportional to the probability of sequences in the
training corpus. When a VQA question has a high-frequency answer in training
data (``What color is the sky?'' $\to$ ``blue''), a VLM will produce that answer
reliably regardless of the image. Blind VQA accuracy thus directly measures
corpus frequency exploitation.

In this work, we conduct a diagnostic study of this phenomenon across multiple
open-source VLMs. Our study has four interlocking components.
First, we evaluate models under \emph{blind} conditions (blank image input) on
1,000 VQA questions, finding that several models substantially outperform human
participants answering the same questions without images.
Second, we introduce \emph{control question variants}---four progressively
weaker reformulations that strip away lexical anchors---to measure how robustly
models exploit priors as linguistic content is removed.
Third, we analyze model \emph{confidence} via logprobabilities, revealing
severe overconfidence in hallucinated answers and a systematic confidence boost
when models are given permission to use their priors.
Fourth, we compare all model behaviors against a human reference dataset
(N=24 participants, 641 questions) collected under the same blind conditions,
where humans are not subject to corpus-frequency bias and thus serve as a
calibration baseline for what blind inference \emph{should} look like.

\paragraph{Main findings.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item VLMs outperform humans on blind VQA (avg.\ $\sim$48\% vs.\ 39\% on
    VQA), with significantly different answer distributions, confirming
    corpus-frequency exploitation rather than genuine reasoning.

    \item Models exhibit characteristic null-hypothesis biases under blind
    conditions: 70\% of yes/no answers are ``no'', 75\% of count answers are
    ``0'', and color answers cluster on ``black''. These biases partially
    reverse under instruction, revealing the prior activation mechanism.

    \item We identify an \emph{instruction-gated} vs.\ \emph{unconditional}
    hallucination spectrum: Qwen3-VL-8B converts 72\% of hedging responses
    into specific answers when given an instruction to imagine the scene, while
    LLaVA v1.5 converts only 17\%---hallucinating unconditionally regardless
    of permission.

    \item Models are severely overconfident: 80--90\% of blind outputs have
    logprob $>-0.5$, and the instruction further raises confidence
    by $\sim$0.08 log-probability points uniformly.

    \item Initial fine-tuning experiments suggest that human blind responses may
    improve human-model alignment without obvious loss of visually-grounded
    accuracy, but the current evidence is still based on single-split results.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Hypothesis-Only and Shortcut Baselines.}
\citet{poliak2018hypothesis} demonstrated that NLI classifiers achieve
non-trivial accuracy using only the hypothesis, exposing annotation artifacts.
\citet{gururangan2018annotation} further characterized these artifacts through
systematic analysis of annotation bias. In VQA, \citet{agrawal2018dont} showed
that models relying on question-answer co-occurrence statistics could achieve
high accuracy without visual understanding. Our work extends this diagnostic
tradition by providing a human reference dataset alongside model evaluations,
enabling calibrated measurement of the gap.

\paragraph{LMs as Corpus Frequency Models.}
\citet{mccoy2024embers} showed that LLM failure patterns are predicted by three
factors: the probability of the task, the input, and the output in the training
corpus. Models are biased toward high-probability outputs regardless of task
requirements. We operationalize this insight: blind VQA accuracy directly
measures the probability of question-answer pairs in the pretraining distribution.

\paragraph{VLM Hallucination and Shortcut Learning.}
Hallucination in VLMs---confident generation of visually ungrounded
content---has been widely documented \citep{ji2023survey,rohrbach2018object,
xiao2021hallucination}. \citet{chen2024are} proposed MMStar to test genuine
visual grounding, requiring fine-grained and logical reasoning. VLind-bench
\citep{lee2024vlind} specifically measures language prior influence across
diverse benchmarks. \citet{han2024instinctive} identified visual shortcuts
through spurious image features. We complement these by providing a
\emph{behavioral} characterization---how and when models commit to
prior-driven answers.

\paragraph{Human Cognition Under Uncertainty.}
Human reasoning under missing information is characterized by fast, heuristic
System 1 thinking \citep{tversky1974judgment}: people rely on representativeness,
availability, and anchoring. Importantly, humans do not share the corpus-frequency
bias of pretrained LLMs, making them a natural reference point for what
linguistically-driven blind inference should look like in the absence of
training-distribution shortcuts.

Our primary evaluation dataset consists of 1,000 questions sampled from the
VQA v2.0 validation split \citep{goyal2016making}, spanning yes/no,
numerical, and open-ended question types. We additionally evaluate on
267 questions from the Science \& Technology category of MMStar
\citep{chen2024are}, which targets visual reasoning skills including coarse
perception (CP), fine-grained perception (FP), instance reasoning (IR),
and logical reasoning (LR). For the human study and fine-tuning analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:alignment}), we use a 641-question subset consisting of
374 VQA and 267 MMStar questions.

\subsection{Blind Inference Conditions}

We evaluate models under three conditions: (1) \textbf{Standard}: the original
image with the question; (2) \textbf{Blind}: a blank 224$\times$224 white PNG
replacing the original image; and (3) \textbf{Blind+Inst}: blank image with an
additional instruction appended to the question: ``\textit{Note: No images are
provided. For each question, imagine an appropriate image exists and answer
based on the most common or universal scenario.}''

\subsection{Control Question Variants}
\label{sec:control}

To characterize \emph{how} models exploit linguistic priors, we introduce four
progressively weaker reformulations of each question, generated using
GPT-4.1-mini (temperature 0, seed 42, JSON schema mode) for reproducibility:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{deictic\_removed}: Deictic markers (\textit{this, that, here,
    there}) and spatial qualifiers (left/right/top/bottom) are removed while
    preserving fluency. Tests whether spatial anchoring drives shortcut behavior.

    \item \textbf{object\_removed}: The specific object noun is replaced with the
    neutral placeholder ``object.'' Tests the contribution of entity identity to
    prior exploitation.

    \item \textbf{weaker\_object}: The specific noun is replaced with a
    one-level-up hypernym (dog $\to$ animal, car $\to$ vehicle, shirt $\to$
    clothing). Tests whether category-level identity is sufficient to trigger
    prior recall.

    \item \textbf{pronominalized}: All content noun phrases are replaced with
    minimal pronouns following grammar-aware rules (``What color is the dog?''
    $\to$ ``What color is it?''; ``How many cats are there?'' $\to$ ``How many
    are there?''). This maximally removes entity-specific prior cues while
    preserving question structure.
\end{enumerate}

The degradation chain from original $\to$ \texttt{pronominalized} forms a
monotonically decreasing axis of lexical prior cues, enabling a controlled
measurement of how much each entity reference contributes to shortcut
exploitation. The GPT extraction prompt and JSON schema are provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:ctl}.

\subsection{Human Data Collection}

We recruited N=24 participants (9 men, 15 women; age 20--43) through online
university community forums. All procedures were conducted under an
IRB-approved protocol (compensation: 10,000 KRW/hour, consistent with
standard rates). Participants answered 641 VQA-style questions in a single
session ($\approx$1 hour) using the same blank-image blind condition as the
models, relying only on the text of the question and general world knowledge.
Responses could be given in Korean or English and were translated and normalized
prior to analysis. After each answer, participants rated their confidence on a
5-point Likert scale. Full protocol details are provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:human}.

\subsection{Metrics}

We report: (1) \textbf{VQA accuracy} using the standard 10-annotator soft
evaluation protocol \citep{antol2015vqa}; (2) \textbf{semantic similarity}
via Sentence-BERT \citep{wang2020minilm} cosine similarity for open-ended
answers; (3) \textbf{Multimodal Gain} (MG = $S_{\text{vision+text}} -
S_{\text{text-only}}$), following \citet{chen2024are}, quantifying the
performance change when visual input is added; (4) \textbf{Spearman $\rho$}
between model and human per-question correctness as human alignment score;
and (5) \textbf{logprobability confidence}, extracted as the mean token-level
log-probability of the generated answer (top-5 logprobs per token, temperature 0);
(6) \textbf{Pearson difficulty correlation}: Pearson $r$ between the human group
accuracy curve $h(q)$ (proportion of 20 participants correct per question) and
per-model accuracy, measuring alignment at the question-difficulty level rather
than the individual rater level; and (7) \textbf{Krippendorff's $\alpha$}
(nominal scale) for within-group and cross-group inter-rater reliability,
which handles multiple raters simultaneously and avoids the prevalence-inflation
issue of Cohen's $\kappa$ and Gwet's AC1.
Note: $h(q)$ is computed from VQA soft-scores (0/0.33/0.67/1.0 averaged
across raters); model accuracy is binary (0 or 1). This asymmetry is
inherent to the task structure and is consistent with standard VQA evaluation.

\subsection{Blind Accuracy Across Models}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports zero-shot blind performance for all models and
humans across VQA and MMStar. Several patterns emerge.

\textbf{VLMs outperform humans on blind VQA.} The best models in the current
comparison achieve $\sim$48--49\% average VQA accuracy under blind conditions,
compared to humans at 39.0\%. This gap is especially pronounced on yes/no
questions (VLM: 68--75\% vs.\ human: 66.6\%) and open-ended questions (VLM:
23--29\% vs.\ human: 13.0\%).

\textbf{LLMs perform comparably to VLMs on blind VQA.} Qwen3 LLMs (text-only
backbones, no vision encoder) achieve 35.3--38.8\% on VQA, close to their
VLM counterparts. This confirms that blind performance is driven by the
language component rather than the vision encoder.

\textbf{Scale effects differ by dataset.} Within the currently tabulated model
set, larger models do not consistently improve blind VQA accuracy, consistent
with the ceiling on high-frequency question-answer patterns in the training
distribution. On MMStar, some larger variants show stronger logical reasoning
(LR), suggesting that this benchmark may benefit more from scale.

Adding the blind+inst instruction produces only modest accuracy changes in many
settings, though not uniformly below 2pp on every model

\textbf{No-bias in yes/no questions.} Under blind conditions, 70\% of model
yes/no answers are ``no'' (vs.\ ground truth: 47\% no). Humans show a smaller
bias (53\% no), suggesting that the model's extreme no-bias reflects an
epistemic default---the null answer under uncertainty---rather than a corpus
prior. Notably, this reverses under blind+inst (``yes'' increases from 30\% to
55\%), confirming that the ``no'' default is instruction-suppressed rather than
a genuine linguistic prior.

\textbf{Zero-bias in count questions.} 75\% of model numeric answers under
blind conditions are ``0,'' while humans produce a much more distributed
count distribution. The interpretation is consistent: in the absence of
visual evidence, models default to ``there are none.''

\textbf{Black-bias in color questions.} Among open-ended color answers,
``black'' accounts for 16\% of model responses under blind conditions, far
exceeding its ground-truth frequency. This likely reflects the modal color
in the training distribution for generic object descriptions.

These null-hypothesis biases illustrate that models are not randomly sampling
from their prior---they produce a specific failure mode that partially mimics
the structure of a reasonable uncertainty response.

\subsection{Instruction-Gated Hallucination}
\label{sec:gated}

A key question is whether models' blind answers reflect a readily available
prior that is always active, or a latent prior that requires explicit
permission to express. We investigate this by comparing model outputs between
the \textbf{Blind} and \textbf{Blind+Inst} conditions.

We classify model outputs in the blind condition as: \textit{hard-abstained}
(explicit refusal: ``I cannot answer'', ``no image''); \textit{soft-abstained}
(hedging outputs: ``nothing'', ``none'', ``unanswerable'', ``nowhere'');
\textit{hallucinated\_correct} (specific answer matching GT); or
\textit{hallucinated\_wrong} (specific answer not matching GT).
Hard abstentions are near-zero across all models (0.0--0.3\%), consistent
with the baked-in ``Answer using a single word'' instruction suppressing
explicit refusals. The real signal is \textit{soft abstention}: 11--19\%
of blind outputs are hedging responses, varying by model family.

We interpret this spectrum as measuring the degree to which prior activation
is \textit{instruction-gated}. Qwen3-VL, a highly instruction-tuned model,
largely suppresses its prior under blind conditions but rapidly activates it
when given permission. LLaVA v1.5, by contrast, hallucinates
\textit{unconditionally}: it produces specific answers whether or not
instructed to, and shows almost no response to the permission instruction.
This distinction is mechanistically important: instruction-gated models have
a functioning ``uncertainty'' signal that instruction can override, while
unconditional models lack this gate entirely.

Additionally, 33.6\% of all answers (not just soft-abstentions) change between
blind and blind+inst conditions for Qwen3-VL-8B---a large behavioral shift
driven by a single sentence. The yes/no distribution shifts from 70\% ``no''
in blind to 55\% ``yes'' in blind+inst, consistent with the instruction
activating a ``plausible scenario exists'' prior that overrides the default
epistemic null.

\subsection{Confidence Calibration}
\label{sec:confidence}

Logprobability analysis reveals that blind hallucinations are produced with
extreme confidence. For Qwen3-VL-8B under blind conditions, 80.8\% of output
tokens have mean logprob $>-0.5$ ($\approx$60\% probability per token), and
the mean logprob across all blind outputs is $-0.258$.
Under Blind+Inst, mean logprob rises to $-0.177$ and the fraction with
logprob $>-0.5$ increases to 89.5\%. The instruction does not merely change
\emph{which} answer is produced---it also makes the model more \emph{certain}
about whatever it produces.

The few soft-abstained outputs (``nothing'', ``none'') have lower mean logprob
($-0.37$ vs.\ $-0.26$ for answered outputs), confirming that hedging outputs
reflect genuine uncertainty. However, the effect size is small, and the
baseline level of confidence even for soft-abstentions is high. The primary
calibration failure is not that models are uncertain when they should be
confident, but that they are \emph{highly confident about wrong hallucinated
answers} they should be uncertain about.

\section{Human-Model Alignment}
\label{sec:alignment}

\subsection{Humans as a Calibration Reference}
\label{sec:human_ref}

Human blind performance provides a natural reference point for what
linguistically-driven inference \textit{should} look like in the absence of
corpus-frequency shortcuts. Unlike VLMs, human participants answering without
images must rely on general world knowledge and explicit reasoning---they do
not have a training-distribution prior over question-answer pairs.

This distinction is empirically visible in answer distributions: while models
produce a bimodal yes/no distribution dominated by ``no'' (70\%), humans show
a more calibrated distribution (53\% no) that better tracks ground-truth
frequencies (47\% no). For numerical answers, 75\% of model answers are ``0''
while human count distributions are spread across small integers, consistent
with humans reasoning about plausible scenarios rather than defaulting to
absence.

Human accuracy on VQA is 39.0\% averaged across question types. Humans perform
below the evaluated models on numerical questions as well (13.8\% vs.\ 17--22\%
for models), and models substantially outperform humans on yes/no and other
answer types.
On MMStar, both humans and models perform near random ($\approx$27\%), confirming
that MMStar successfully targets visual grounding.

\subsection{Human-Model Divergence in Answer Patterns}

We measure human-model alignment using two complementary metrics.
First, we compute \textbf{Pearson difficulty correlation}: for each question
$q$, we compute the human group accuracy $h(q)$ (proportion of 20 participants
correct) and correlate it with per-model accuracy.
We prefer Pearson over Spearman here because the relationship is approximately
linear (max $|r - \rho| = 0.026$ across all conditions), and operating on
proportions avoids the information loss from rank conversion on binary data.
Second, we compute \textbf{Krippendorff's $\alpha$} (nominal scale, binary
correctness) to measure within-group and cross-group reliability.

Human difficulty estimates are highly reliable: $\text{ICC}(2,20) = 0.963$
across 20 raters, justifying use of $h(q)$ as a stable difficulty signal.
Within-group human reliability is $\alpha_{\text{human}} = 0.554$ (moderate,
appropriate for a genuinely ambiguous task without visual input).

Pretrained VLMs show moderate correlation with human difficulty under blind
conditions (Pearson $r = 0.50$--$0.62$ depending on model), confirming that
human and model difficulty patterns partially overlap---both exploit linguistic
priors to some extent. However, adding any model to the human rater pool
\emph{decreases} $\alpha$ (from 0.554 to 0.535--0.543), confirming that models
are structurally less consistent with the human group than humans are with each
other.

In the current analysis, Blind+Inst often yields slightly higher
human-difficulty correlations than Blind alone for several model families.
This pattern is consistent with the soft-abstention-collapse result in
Section~\ref{sec:gated}, but it should be treated as provisional until the
human comparison conditions are fully matched across settings.

The inter-rater agreement matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:interrater}) further

cluster despite operating under identical conditions. This separation is not
merely about accuracy level---it reflects qualitatively different reasoning
strategies.

Semantic similarity analysis reinforces this finding: model answers have
higher embedding similarity to ground truth (mean 65.8 vs.\ human 64.2)
despite being generated without visual evidence, and the human similarity
distribution has a longer left tail (more diverse wrong answers), consistent
with exploratory reasoning rather than prior retrieval.

\subsection{Fine-tuning to Human Prior Distributions}
\label{sec:finetune}

We fine-tune VLMs on human blind response distributions to test whether the
human-model alignment gap can be closed without sacrificing visual grounding.
We apply LoRA adapters (rank 32, scale 64) to all linear layers while freezing
the vision encoder and alignment modules, training with AdamW ($\text{lr}=2
\times 10^{-5}$, cosine schedule, 5\% warmup, 5 epochs) on N=15 participant
annotations.

We investigate two objectives: (1) \textbf{SFT}: standard cross-entropy on
the most frequent human canonical answer; and (2) \textbf{JS}: distributional
alignment via Jensen-Shannon divergence loss \citep{lin2002divergence} between
model and human confidence distributions over canonical answers.

Initial single-split results suggest alignment improvement: Spearman $\rho$
increases from 0.455 (pretrained) to 0.598 (SFT) and 0.547 (JS), while visual
accuracy on the standard VQA task appears broadly preserved. We treat these
numbers as preliminary until the planned k-fold evaluation is complete.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{The instruction-gated spectrum as an architectural insight.}
The dramatic variation in soft-abstention collapse rates (17\% for LLaVA v1.5
vs.\ 72\% for Qwen3-VL) suggests that instruction-following capability plays
a role in prior activation that goes beyond accuracy. Instruction-tuned models
have learned to gate prior expression behind contextual permission signals, while
models with weaker instruction following leak priors unconditionally. This is
not simply a capability difference---LLaVA v1.5 is competitive on standard VQA
accuracy---but a behavioral difference in how priors are accessed.

\paragraph{Implications for benchmark design.}
Our results suggest that any VQA benchmark can be partially solved by
linguistic priors alone, and that the magnitude of this effect varies
predictably with question type, answer distribution, and entity specificity.
We propose that blind VQA evaluation should become a standard diagnostic for
new VQA benchmarks: a high blind accuracy ($>$40\%) is at least a warning sign
that the benchmark may over-index on knowledge-accessible question types.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our human study involves 24 participants recruited from university communities
in a single language (Korean/English), limiting cultural and demographic
generalizability. Model evaluation uses greedy decoding (temperature 0), which
may underestimate answer diversity and overestimate confidence. The control
variant GPT extraction uses a best-effort seed (seed is advisory in the
Responses API) and results may vary slightly across runs. The corpus frequency
correlation analysis is pending and represents an important gap in our
mechanistic account.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

We present a human-calibrated diagnostic study of linguistic prior exploitation
in Vision-Language Models. Analogous to hypothesis-only baselines in NLI, blind
VQA evaluation reveals that VLMs can answer many questions without visual input,
often by exploiting corpus-frequency priors rather than image evidence. We
introduce control question variants as a way to probe this reliance, and use
confidence analysis to expose the severe overconfidence that accompanies blind
hallucination. The instruction-gated hallucination finding reveals a useful
behavioral spectrum across model families. Human blind responses provide a
reference point for evaluating these behaviors and a promising, though still
preliminary, target for alignment.

\section{Human Study Protocol}
\label{app:human}

A total of 24 participants (9 men and 15 women; age range: 20--43 years) were
included in the study. Participants were recruited via online student community
forums at universities. All procedures were conducted under an IRB-approved
protocol and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed
consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation.

Each participant completed 641 VQA-style questions in a single session, which
took approximately one hour on average. Participants were compensated at a rate
of 10,000 KRW per hour, consistent with standard compensation for similar
annotation tasks.

For VQA, participants were instructed to provide free-form answers that were as
concise as possible. Responses could be given in either Korean or English and
were translated and processed using the same answer normalization and aggregation
strategy described in Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation}. MMStar questions were
presented with the original multiple-choice options, along with Korean
translations, in the same order as the dataset.

\section{Answer Aggregation}
\label{app:aggregation}

We cluster semantically equivalent answers (e.g., ``3'' and ``three'') into
canonical forms using GPT-4o mini \citep{openai2025gpt4o}. The prompt instructs
the model to group synonymous responses, select the most concise canonical form,
and return results in structured JSON. Human responses are then aggregated into
a confidence-weighted distribution over canonical answers, where each answer's
weight is the sum of confidence scores from all annotators selecting that answer,
normalized to sum to 1:
\begin{equation}
H(a_k^*) = \frac{\sum_{i:\phi(a_i)=a_k^*} c_i}{\sum_i c_i}
\end{equation}
where $c_i$ is participant $i$'s Likert confidence (normalized to $[0,1]$),
$\phi$ maps raw answers to canonical answers, and $K$ is the number of
canonical answer clusters.

\section{Control Question Extraction Prompt}
\label{app:ctl}

Control variants are extracted using GPT-4.1-mini-2025-04-14 (temperature 0,
seed 42) with strict JSON schema mode for reproducibility.
The system prompt instructs the model to generate all four variants in a single
API call per batch of 50 questions. The schema enforces:
\texttt{\{deictic\_removed, object\_removed, weaker\_object, pronominalized\}}
as required string fields, with \texttt{additionalProperties: false}.
For pronominalized, the prompt specifies grammar-aware substitution rules to
ensure natural pronominalization (``What color is it?'' not ``What color is
the object?'').

\section{Training Details}
\label{app:training}

We apply LoRA to all linear layers with rank 32 and scaling factor 64, while
freezing the vision encoder and alignment modules. We optimize using AdamW with
a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$, cosine learning-rate scheduling with 5\%
warmup, and weight decay of 0.1, training for a maximum of 5 epochs. We select
the checkpoint with the best validation performance for evaluation.
Hyperparameters were selected based on prior literature and preliminary runs
rather than exhaustive optimization.

\section{Question Sampling for New Human Study}
\label{sec:sampling}

To select maximally diagnostic questions for the new human study
(Section~\ref{sec:new_human}), we stratify 1,000 control questions into five
tiers based on model behavior:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier A}: High confidence + wrong (mean logprob $>-0.25$,
    accuracy $<20\%$). Questions where models hallucinate with extreme certainty.
    \item \textbf{Tier B}: Answer changes across control variants ($\geq$2
    answer changes across 4 variants). Questions sensitive to linguistic
    surface form.
    \item \textbf{Tier C}: $\geq$80\% of models wrong on baseline. Shared
    corpus prior (hard questions with misleading priors).
    \item \textbf{Tier D}: Large accuracy drop from original to pronominalized
    ($\Delta$acc $>30$pp). Shortcut-dependent questions where entity name
    drives the answer.
    \item \textbf{Tier E}: Large confidence drop from original to pronominalized
    ($\Delta$logprob $>0.2$). Questions where entity removal creates uncertainty
    even if the answer is unchanged.
\end{itemize}

The target sample is 300 questions: $\sim$60 from each of Tiers A/B/D/E and
$\sim$120 random (stratified by entity type, capped at 20\% per type).
